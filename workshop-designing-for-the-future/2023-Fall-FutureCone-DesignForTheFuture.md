### The future cone

And so there's a whole genre of social science called futures studies that tries to think about how do we think about the future, and how do we diagram out things to help others also think about the future.

Okay. So to think about the future, we have to start with thinking about time. Our kind of normal concept of time is that it's just an arrow that points off. In this case, the future is that way in my diagram. So it's just pointing off towards the future. And it's some kind of linear thing.

And so with that, we know that there is a *now*. Like — it's now now, okay? Now it's now again. It's now again. Okay. But there's now. And then there's the past. And oftentimes the past is just sort of a linear set of events that led us to — that we can see as the past as having led us to now.

And we don't still know what's in the future. And so that means that the future isn't just *that way*, but there's also a range. There's a potential. And the farther and farther out we get into the future, the wider the potential gets for what could happen.

And then, if you have ever studied relativity in physics, there's this concept called the light cone. Any time there's a photon traveling in space that intersects with you, there's this sort of range of area that it could have come from that would have let that photon end up exactly where you are. But on the other side, if that photon keeps traveling, there becomes a cone of possibilities for where that photon will end up.

And so the future cone is that idea — that we're now, and there's this three-dimensional space of possibility that's out in the future in front of us. Maybe it's actually a four-dimensional space. Or maybe it's an 11-dimensional space. But it's at least not just a plane. There's a whole range.

### The P-words: projected, probable, plausible, possible, preposterous, preferable

Okay. So there's more and less likely places in the future as well. So I'm just going to give you the range of "future possible."

There's the **projected** future, which is just: if we do nothing and we just say I'm going to keep living exactly like today and just do that forever. That's the projected future. For the most part, that's a bad future to point towards. We probably don't want to just point towards today moving forward forever into the future.

Then there's the **probable** future, which is sort of the basic stuff that we're already doing that could improve people's lives. We'll probably keep doing that and that will become what's next. So in the present day, the probable future is that all cars will be electric — most vehicles will be electric in the near and definitely distant future. That's a prediction, but it's a highly likely to come true prediction.

Then there's the **plausible** future, which is basically: with all the information that we know, all these things are plausible or possible. But they become less and less likely as they get more fringe or more avant-garde.

Then there's the **possible** — like, oh, we're probably going to figure this out. Small-scale nuclear reactors we can build in research labs, but we haven't figured out how to build them to put on trucks and just power cities wherever we want. But it's possible to presume that there's a future where tiny safe nuclear reactors are just scattered around your city or neighborhood. It's also possible to make a completely renewable energy grid. We just aren't quite there to figure it out, but it's within the realm of the possible.

But then there's the **preposterous**, and this is things that are ridiculous or impossible, or that people are just like, that will never happen. And often things that will never happen eventually do still happen — but in the now (remember, we're in the now now), it's preposterous still. This could also be fantasy. It doesn't even have to be that this could be real. It could be: there's no way that this will ever be real. Sometimes that's still helpful to think about.

And then there's more P-words. There's also the **preferable** future, and that's a subset of all possible futures — the probable and the preposterous — that you personally prefer. And so this is a different area for different people. The one thing I would say is that I don't think anybody's preferable future is just the projected today into the future. So I drew it on my diagram as being at least a little bit away from the projected future.

But this is the stuff that you want to happen, or that should happen, or that collectively we think ought to happen. And this could still be fantasy — and when it's fantasy, it's sort of like (sorry, I'm just shining lasers on you over there) — when it's fantasy, it's sort of like whatever your ideals are, you're projecting them off into that fantastical space.

Okay. So this all came from an academic, Joseph Voros, who writes and thinks and talks about this stuff a lot. If you want to investigate this more on your own, you can find a lot of it on his website, The Voroscope.

### Speculative and critical design

Then I think the question is: why is it helpful, if you're a designer, to think more about the future?

I first was introduced to this idea reading this book called *Speculative Everything*, which is by these industrial designers Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby. They now teach at Parsons in New York, but they used to run an industrial design graduate program at the Royal College of Art in London.

Their main way of making work is this idea that they call speculative design, or they also refer to it as critical design. It's this idea that you can make design that is critical of the status quo, and that it's easier for an audience to embrace, because the language of design — especially in our modern world — is very familiar to most people. So objects and interfaces and tools and posters and books, advertisements, packaging — these are all things that we're — (I'm supposed to go home now.) Designed objects are all things that we use every day, and so they become a much easier window into other ideas than an art piece or an experimental piece of music might be. I think the reason film also investigates a lot of these issues is because film also is a thing that's easy for people to embrace and try to understand.

So Dunne and Raby say that you can use your design skills to *not* make things for the status quo, but to critique the status quo. And that's being critical — that's critical design. And if you choose to use them to then speculate about other possible futures, you're making speculative design. So design that speculates how the future could be, or should be, or we want it to be — that could also then be critical of the futures that we don't want to have happen.

And so we're designing things in the present, but they're critical of the projected future. Or designing things in the present that can lead us to the future that you prefer. And all of this, though, is meant to be design that challenges the status quo.

So obviously if you're down with the status quo, that's also fine. You wouldn't be making critical design — maybe this doesn't interest you. But I think everybody has something that they would prefer to have happen. And so I think you can think about your designing as always being able to envision these things that you would prefer, so we can embrace and learn more about them in the present.

Okay. Yeah. And so maybe the best way to phrase this simply is: it's design that provides options for people.
