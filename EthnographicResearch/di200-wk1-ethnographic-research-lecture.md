## 1 · What ethnography is (1:00–1:10)

Start with the discipline, not the design version.

**Malinowski, *Argonauts of the Western Pacific*, 1922.** The founding move of modern fieldwork: stop reading other people's reports, go live there, learn the language, stay long enough that people forget to perform for you. He was there for years.

**Clifford Geertz, "Thick Description," 1973.** The best twelve minutes you can spend teaching this. Use his example directly, and do it physically:

> Two boys contract the eyelid of their right eye. *(Do it.)* In one, it's an involuntary twitch. In the other, it's a wink to a friend — a conspiracy, a signal.
>
> Photograph both. The photographs are identical. A camera cannot tell them apart. Any description that stops at "rapid contraction of the right eyelid" has recorded everything and understood nothing.
>
> **Thin description** is what happened. **Thick description** is what it meant, and to whom, and how you know.

Then land it on the site:

> On Aug 31 you'll watch someone at the front desk pause for four seconds before answering a guest. Thin: she paused. Thick: she paused because the system takes four seconds to load and she's learned to fill the gap with eye contact so the guest doesn't feel ignored — and she's doing unpaid design work to cover for bad software, roughly two hundred times a shift.
>
> You cannot get to the second one by watching harder. You get there by asking her.

**Then be honest about the theft.** This is Blauvelt's "Design's Ethnographic Turn," which they're reading this week:

> An anthropologist spends eighteen months. You are going to spend five days, spread over a semester. Design took anthropology's methods and left behind the timescale, the language learning, the reflexivity, and most of the ethics apparatus.
>
> I'm not going to pretend that's fine. What I'll say is: knowing you're doing a compressed, partial version makes you careful. Believing you've "done ethnography" because you filled a wall with Post-its makes you dangerous. That's the same argument Natasha Jen made this morning, pointed at research instead of process.

## 2 · Four methods (1:10–1:20)

Four. Not twenty. They can hold four.

### Participant observation

Be in the space, on its terms, long enough to stop being interesting. The first twenty minutes are worthless — that's you being visible and everyone performing. Sit through it.

**Rule: you are looking for the ordinary, not the remarkable.** Students record the weird thing. The weird thing happened once. The pattern that happens two hundred times is where the design problem lives.

### AEIOU

Developed at Doblin, 1991. The scaffold for when you're standing there with a notebook and no idea what to write. Five columns:

| | |
|---|---|
| **A**ctivities | What are people doing? Goal-directed sequences. |
| **E**nvironments | What's the space like? Light, noise, temperature, layout. |
| **I**nteractions | Between people, between people and systems. Where's the friction? |
| **O**bjects | What's present? What's being used, misused, improvised? |
| **U**sers | Who's here? Roles, not demographics. Who has authority? |

Run the letters. It always gives you something to write.

### Traces — the one they'll be best at

Artifact and trace evidence. This is where designers beat anthropologists, because designers can read made things.

- **Desire paths** — the worn dirt line where nobody built a sidewalk
- **Wear patterns** — which door handle is polished, which corner of the counter is worn
- **Workarounds** — the doorstop, the taped-over sensor, the propped-open fire door
- **Hand-written signs** — the single richest signal in any building

Spend a full minute on hand-written signs:

Every hand-lettered sign taped to a wall is a fossil of a failure. Somebody had a problem often enough to get a Sharpie. "PLEASE DO NOT PUT TRAYS HERE." "DOOR STICKS — PUSH HARD." "ASK AT DESK." Somebody designed that space, and then reality filed a complaint in marker.

Photograph every single one you see at the Hilton.

### Semi-structured interviews

Not a survey. Not a chat. A prepared set of *topics*, asked in whatever order the conversation wants, with the freedom to follow what's interesting.

Give them Spradley's **grand tour question** as the opener that always works:

> **"Walk me through a typical shift."**
>
> Not "what's frustrating about your job" — that's asking a stranger to complain to someone who might report it. "Walk me through a typical shift" gets you sequence, and the frustrations arrive on their own, attached to the moment they happen in.

Then the follow-ups, which are the whole craft:

- "What happened right before that?"
- "Can you show me?" ← **the best one in the list**
- "Is that how it usually goes, or was that unusual?"
- "Who else deals with this?"
- Silence. Count to five. They'll keep talking. Students find this unbearable and it works every time.

**And the failure modes, which matter more:**

- **Leading questions.** "Doesn't it get frustrating when the elevator's slow?" You just put the answer in their mouth and now you can't unhear it. Weak-link problem: that one question can poison the whole team's understanding.
- **Solutioning at people.** "What if there were an app?" You're not there to pitch.
- **Interviewing your idea instead of the person.** If you already know what you want to make, you'll hear agreement everywhere.
- **The 5 Whys, used badly.** It's a real technique. It's also, at four whys deep, an interrogation of a person who has to get back to work. Two whys and gratitude.

## 3 · How to write it down (1:20–1:30)

**This is the part that actually determines whether Wk5 works.** Give it real time. If something has to be cut from this block, cut a method, not this.

> In four weeks we sit in this room and try to synthesize everything the class collected. That session is only as good as the notes. I have watched teams lose an entire site visit because six people wrote "front desk seemed busy" and nothing else. You cannot code a vibe.

### Jottings vs. fieldnotes

From Emerson, Fretz & Shaw, *Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes* — the standard text.

- **Jottings** happen in the space. Fragments, quotes, times, sketches. Nobody writes prose while standing in a lobby.
- **Fieldnotes** are what you write *afterward*, expanding the jottings into full description while you can still see it in your head.

**The 24-hour rule, and mean it:**

> Full fieldnotes within twenty-four hours. Not "when I get to it." Memory for this kind of detail collapses overnight — you keep the gist and lose every specific, and the specifics are the entire value. On Hilton days we will write notes on the light rail home. That's not a suggestion, it's the schedule.

### The two-column split

Draw this on the board. Make them use it this afternoon.

```
OBSERVATION                          |  COMMENTARY
what a camera would have caught      |  what I think it means, what it
                                     |  reminds me of, what to ask next
-------------------------------------|--------------------------------
10:14 — guest approaches desk,       |  Is the sign the problem or is the
stops, looks left at sign, looks     |  desk in the wrong place? Ask:
back at desk, waits ~6 sec before    |  does this happen all day?
speaking                             |
                                     |
"Yeah the mornings are the worst,    |  "The worst" — worst how? Volume?
nobody tells us anything"            |  Communication? FOLLOW UP.
```

Three rules on that:

1. **Keep the columns separate and never let them merge.** The moment interpretation gets written as observation, you can't recover the original.
2. **Verbatim quotes go in quotation marks. Everything else is paraphrase.** If you didn't catch it exactly, don't quote it. A fake quote is worse than no quote.
3. **Time-stamp constantly.** Duration is data. "Waited 6 seconds" is worth more than "waited a while," and you can't reconstruct it later.

### Also

- **Draw.** Plans, sections, arrows showing how people move. You are design students; this is your advantage over a sociologist. A bad plan sketch beats a good paragraph.
- **Record what's absent.** The chair nobody sits in. The entrance nobody uses. The amenity nobody touches. Absence is invisible unless you write it down on purpose.
- **Write down what surprised you, and that it surprised you.** Surprise is the sound your assumptions make when they break. It fades within a day and it never comes back.

## 4 · What coding is (1:30–1:35)

Just enough for the afternoon to make sense. The real version is Wk5.

> You'll come back with hundreds of fragments. Coding is how a pile of fragments becomes something you can act on. It's from grounded theory — Glaser and Strauss, 1967 — and it's three passes.

**Open coding.** Go through your notes. Tag each distinct observation with a short label for what it's about. No categories decided in advance; the labels come from the material.

**In vivo codes.** Where you can, **use the person's own words as the label.** If a housekeeper says "the mornings are the worst," the code is `the mornings are the worst`, not `morning workflow inefficiency`. Two reasons: it keeps their framing instead of substituting yours, and it stays specific enough to argue with.

**Axial coding.** Group the codes. What clusters? What's the relationship between clusters? This is where the affinity wall comes in — Jiro Kawakita's KJ method, which you'll see called affinity diagramming everywhere in design.

**The rule for the wall, which is non-negotiable:**

> One observation per note. In the person's own words where you have them. **No interpretation on the note itself.** The interpretation happens when you move the notes around, and if the interpretation is already written on the card you've pre-decided the answer and the wall is theater.

That's Jen's Post-it critique, answered:

> A wall of Post-its that just says what you already believed is exactly the ritual she's attacking. A wall of specific, verbatim, uninterpreted observations is a tool. Same Post-its. The difference is entirely in what's written on them.
