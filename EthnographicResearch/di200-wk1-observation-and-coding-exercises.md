# Observation, Fieldnotes, and Coding — Week 1 Afternoon Exercises

**DI 200 / Structured Creativity — Monday Aug 24 2026, 1:35–3:00**

Run these immediately after the ethnography lecture (`di200-wk1-ethnographic-research-lecture.md`). Campus stands in for the Hilton. Every mistake they make today is one they don't make on Aug 31 in front of a housekeeper.

**The governing idea:** they will be bad at this today, on purpose, somewhere it costs nothing. Say that out loud at the start. It lowers the stakes and it's true.

---

## Setup before class

- Print the **observation post assignments** (§2) — one slip per pair
- Print or project the **two-column fieldnote template** from the lecture
- Post-its, one color per pair if you can; index cards work better if you have them
- A long wall or a roll of kraft paper for the coding wall
- Thick markers only. **No pens.** A fine pen lets people write paragraphs on a card; a fat marker enforces one observation per note.

---

## 1 · Calibration: the same thing, twice (1:35–1:45, in the room)

Ten minutes, and it does more work than it looks like.

Put a short video on with no sound — a busy intersection, a train platform, a café. Any fixed-camera street scene, 3 minutes.

**Round 1.** "Write down what you see." Play it. Stop.

Ask three people to read theirs aloud. You will reliably get: *"It's chaotic." "People seem stressed." "A busy morning."*

Then:

> Every one of those is a conclusion. Not one of you told me anything a camera caught. If I gave that note to a teammate who wasn't here, they'd know what you felt and nothing about the place.

**Round 2.** Same clip. New rule: **only what a camera would catch, plus a count or a duration in every line.** Play it again. Read three aloud.

> That's the difference between thin and thick, and it's also the difference between notes you can code in four weeks and notes you can't. You just did it. Now go do it somewhere real.

---

## 2 · Out of the room: observation posts (1:45–2:15, 30 min)

Pairs. Each pair gets an assigned post and a slip. **Sit or stand still for the whole thirty minutes.** No wandering, no phones except to photograph traces.

Good MICA-area posts — pick from these, adjust to what's actually busy on a Monday:

- Main entrance or lobby of a building at a class change
- The café or coffee line
- A building's elevator lobby
- A stairwell landing
- The library entrance / circulation desk
- A crosswalk or campus street corner
- A bike rack or a smoking spot
- A corridor with a bulletin board
- The loading dock or a service entrance ← **assign this one to somebody**, it's the back-of-house rehearsal and it's the least glamorous post on the list

**The slip says:**

> **Post:** ______  **Time in:** ______  **Time out:** ______
>
> 1. **First 5 minutes: do nothing but draw the space.** Plan view. Where can people
>    enter, sit, stand, wait? Where does the light come from?
> 2. **Next 20 minutes: two columns.** Observation left, commentary right. Time-stamp
> everything. Count things. Get at least **three durations** ("waited 8 sec," "conversation lasted 2 min").
> 3. **Throughout: hunt traces.** Photograph at least **five** — wear, desire paths,
>    workarounds, hand-written signs, anything improvised. Signs are worth the most.
> 4. **Last 5 minutes: write down three things that are ABSENT.** What nobody uses,
>    where nobody goes, what you expected to see and didn't.
>
> Do not talk to anyone. Today is looking only. Interviews are Wk4.
>
> **Minimum: 25 distinct observations.** Count them. If you have 9, you're narrating instead of observing — go back to counting and timing.

**Why no interviews today:** they haven't been briefed enough to approach strangers well, and a bad first attempt on a stranger teaches the wrong lesson. Observation is the weak-link skill they can practice safely.

## 3 · Fieldnotes: the 24-hour rule, compressed to 20 minutes (2:15–2:35)

Back in the room. Silent. Individual — **not** in pairs yet.

> You have jottings. Now you write fieldnotes, and you do it now, while it's still in your head. This is the twenty-four-hour rule, except I'm giving you twenty minutes and you were only there for thirty. On Aug 31 you'll have six hours of material and you'll do this on the train.

On the board:

1. **Expand every jotting into a full sentence** someone who wasn't there could picture. "Guy hesitated" → "A man in a backpack stopped two steps inside the door, looked left at the directory, then right at the desk, then walked to the desk without reading it."
2. **Mark every verbatim quote with quotation marks.** If you're not sure it's exact, paraphrase it instead. Never invent a quote.
3. **Keep the two columns separate.** Do not let commentary migrate left.
4. **At the bottom, write three questions** you'd ask someone there if you could. These become real interview questions in Wk4.
5. **Write one sentence about what surprised you** — and note that it surprised you.

Circulate. The thing to look for and correct on the spot: interpretation written as observation. "She was annoyed" is commentary. "She exhaled, looked at the ceiling, and repeated the question" is observation.

## 4 · Coding wall (2:35–2:55)

The first real synthesis of the semester, in twenty minutes.

**Step 1 — Transcribe to notes (8 min).** Everyone takes their fieldnotes and writes **one observation per Post-it, in marker.** Verbatim where you have it.

State the rule again, because they'll break it:

> One observation per note. No interpretation on the note. If your note says "wayfinding is confusing," that's a conclusion — throw it away and write the thing you actually saw instead.

Target 10–15 notes each. That's 200–300 on the wall for a class of twenty, which is exactly the right amount of chaos.

**Step 2 — Everything on the wall (2 min).** No order. Just get it up. Deliberately messy.

**Step 3 — Silent clustering (7 min).** **No talking.** Anyone may move any note. If you disagree with a placement, move it — don't argue. Notes that get moved back and forth are the interesting ones.

> Silence matters here. The moment people talk, the loudest person's categories win and everyone else stops thinking. Silent clustering surfaces disagreement instead of suppressing it.

**Step 4 — Name the clusters (4 min).** Now talking. Each cluster gets a name on a different-colored note. **Push hard for in vivo names.** If someone wrote down an actual overheard line, that's the cluster name.

> "Circulation issues" is a name that lets you stop thinking. "People stop two steps inside the door" is a name you can design against.

**Step 5 — Find the orphans (2 min).** Which notes fit nowhere? Put them in their own zone. Say this:

> Don't throw those away. The thing that fits nowhere is either bad data or the most interesting thing on the wall, and you usually can't tell which one yet. Anthropologists call that the anomaly. Keep it.

## 5 · Debrief and assignment (2:55–3:00)

Ethics was briefed before lunch — don't re-run it here. **One minute on what just happened:**

- Where did you interpret when you meant to observe?
- Which pair's post was hardest, and why? *(It'll be the loading dock or the stairwell — boring posts are harder, and the Hilton has a lot of boring posts.)*
- What could you not have learned by watching, that you'd need to ask?

> That last list is why Wk4 exists. You've just found the ceiling of observation. Half of what you want to know is inside somebody's head and the only way in is to ask.

**Then set the assignment** (§6) and end. If anyone missed the morning, they are **not cleared for Aug 31** until they've had the ethics briefing — hand them `research-ethics-primer-hilton.md` and talk to them before they travel.

## 6 · Assignment: Fifty Observations

Due in Canvas before Wk2.

> Pick one public or semi-public space you don't control — a bus, a laundromat, a waiting room, a grocery store, a park, a lobby. Not your apartment, not a friend's studio.
>
> Sit there for **one hour, in one sitting.** Write **fifty numbered observations.**
>
> Rules:
> - One observation per line, numbered 1–50
> - Only what a camera would catch
> - At least ten include a count or a duration
> - At least five are about something **absent**
> - At least three are verbatim quotes, in quotation marks
> - Photograph any hand-written sign you find and include the images
>
> Post: your fifty, your photos, and **three sentences** on what got harder or easier between observation 10 and observation 50.

**Why fifty, and why say so:**

> Fifty is more than you think you can find, and that's the point. The first fifteen are the obvious ones everybody would get. Somewhere around thirty you run out of obvious and start actually looking. The last ten are where the real observations live.
>
> This is the strong-link move applied to noticing: quantity first, and the good ones surface because you kept going.

## Timing fallbacks

Monday will not run to plan. In order of what to cut:

1. **Cut the calibration video** (§1) if the morning ran long — but you lose the single clearest before/after moment in the day
2. **Cut clustering to 5 minutes** and skip naming; walk the wall and name three out loud yourself
3. **Cut the debrief** to one question

**Never cut:** time outside the room. Everything else can be recovered in Wk4 and Wk5; thirty minutes of real observation before a real site visit can't. (The ethics briefing is protected by living in the morning.)

**If you broke at 11:30 and came back at 12:30**, you have 2.5 hours. Put the extra thirty minutes into §2 (40 min outside) and §4 (30 min coding) — not into more lecture.

If you're somehow *ahead* — you won't be — the Disruptus deck from the Wk1 plan is the designed overflow.
