# The Bits That Make Up a Design Philosophy

## A pile

I keep an are.na channel called "The Bits That Make Up A Design Philosophy." It holds about 1,700 things. Half of it are just text blurbs: quotes, pasted paragraphs, a few sentences of my own. This talk asks the pile one question: what patterns exist in this library of miscellaneous materials? 

## What repeats

Six things.

1. Design is thinking. And making is how the thinking happens.
2. Form makes a claim. Every design carries an ideology, chosen or not.
3. What a thing is for matters more than what it's on.
4. Anything that can be shared should be.
5. Say what you're sustaining. Mine is the welfare of all life.
6. Make what belongs in the future you'd prefer.

## 1. Design is thinking

"Designer as apartment tidier." 

Most designers, "are visual designers. They try to make things look pretty. It's just like cleaning up your apartment."[^1780784]

The rest of the channel argues with that from every side.

- "Making is thinking."[^1820676]
- "I believe that designing is a tool for thinking."[^8874652]
- Frank Gehry: "I have always thought that if you know what you are going to do in advance, you won't do it."[^2842698]
- Buckminster Fuller: don't teach people a new way of thinking, give them a tool "the use of which will lead to new ways of thinking."[^2718027]

Going back through twenty years of my own work for a lecture, I wrote down that I don't answer questions so much as create them. Each question I pose myself usually produces more questions. Design gets called problem solving. I think of mine as problem finding.[^utopian]

The definition I keep is Herbert Simon's, from 1969. Design is "devising courses of action aimed at changing current situations into preferred ones."[^1803852] I use it as the spine of a whole semester. The word that carries the weight is "preferred." Preferred by whom? Every critique of design lives in that word. Tim Brown's 2008 definition of design thinking ends in "customer value and market opportunity." Simon's ends in "preferred."[^dt]

The thinking happens in the work, and the work leaves a trace. A recent block puts it this way: "Anything worth remembering came from a person who did not know where she or he was going until arriving. No shortcut gets you there: the process leaves a trace."[^51192781]

Ira Glass gives the practical version. For the first couple of years your taste is better than your work, and the only way through is volume. "Put yourself on deadline so that every week you finish one piece."[^2799761]

## 2. Form makes a claim

The Barcelona chair sits in corporate lobbies all over America, standing in for good taste and clear thinking. Mies and Lilly Reich designed it in 1929 for the king and queen of Spain to sit in at a ceremony. It's a throne.[^semio]

Modernism presents itself as neutral. Neutral means one of two things: the design reflects the beliefs of whoever made it, or it reflects the status quo. Both are ideologies.

Gunnar Swanson: "Form makes a claim and designers are responsible for the claims their work makes."[^1803838] Dunne and Raby, in 2001: "All design is ideological, the design process is informed by values based on a specific world view."[^dunne]

I got this wrong once, and the failure is the best evidence I have. *Green Acres* was a catalog about artists who use farming as their practice. I wanted the design to critique industrial agriculture, so I built the page grid on township squares, the tiny farm squares you see in aerial photos of the Midwest. Nobody gets it. No reader has looked at that book and learned something about industrial agriculture from the grid.[^bk06] What made the book more sustainable was boring: print on demand, recycled unbleached paper, short runs.

*Ecovention Europe* was the next book for the same curator. This time I put the ideology in a rule. Sara de Bondt's sustainable printing manifesto includes "use less ink," so no color in the palette adds up to more than 100 percent total ink coverage. The text runs the full height of the column. I reused the *Green Acres* grid instead of making a new one. No one chose these colors for how they look together. The ink-coverage rule chose them.[^eco]

In a comment on a video about aesthetics I described what I was trying to teach: "its not what it looks like, its why it looks like."[^c2992996] Mat Dryhurst, answering a call for new aesthetics, put it from the other side: "Bauhaus was not an aesthetic, but a new school of thought."[^43476544]

I built a workshop to test this. Students design the same content twice, once under each of two opposing ideologies. Then they trade pieces and guess, from form alone, which ideology made which. If nobody can tell, the ideology never reached the form. I haven't run it yet.[^fcc]

## 3. What it's for, and what it's on

In 2026 I stopped paying for betterlivingthroughsustainability.com. I had paid for it for nineteen years.

The site ran on Drupal, then became flat HTML, and the writing now lives in a wiki. The domain is gone. The Drupal install is gone. The company I built the publishing system with is gone. The writing is fine. It has moved four times and still reads, because it was structured and not styled, so every move was a rendering instead of a rescue.[^nineteen]

I've made the same argument four times without noticing:

- Pure content: don't let the format own the writing.
- CAPE, the publishing system I built with Kai Curry: don't let the tool own the content.
- The libre designer: don't let the vendor own the practice.
- Signs signaling on substrates: don't let the medium own the design.

Ben Duvall describes graphic design as manipulating elements on a surface in order to communicate. Signs on a surface. Widen the surface and you get a screen, a chair, a room, somebody's attention. What stays fixed is the intent to signal something.

That's also where the automation question gets answered. If what makes you a designer is technical skill, automation takes the job. If it's intent, it doesn't.[^menu] The channel has a lot to say about this, mostly from 2023 on.

- "Production is cheaper. Execution is abundant. The scarce skill becomes deciding what's worth making and why."[^43710725]
- "Speed is not fluency." You can now make something that looks finished without understanding how or why it works. "But looking finished isn't the same as being good."[^37771452]
- "Absence of choice is hard to sense, and mechanization's real innovation is in creating a complacent buyer with reduced expectations."[^25007527]

From a piece about Ivan Illich: "A tool can be taken up and put down, according to the user's own intentions, and the boundary between tool and user remains clear." A system "envelops the user," and the user becomes a component of it.[^39937340] A tool can leave. A system can't. That's the libre argument in two sentences.

## 4. Anything that can be shared should be

In 2019 the US sanctioned Venezuela. Adobe wasn't sure whether letting Venezuelans use Creative Cloud counted as part of the sanctions, and rather than find out, it cut off every Venezuelan IP address. No designer in the country could open Illustrator, InDesign, After Effects, or Acrobat. Adobe later decided the sanctions didn't apply to its tools. That didn't give anyone their week back.[^bk00]

That's enclosure. Something shared gets a wall around it, and someone charges for the gate. It happens to tools, to images, to materials. It happened to CAPE. The system ran on Flickr's API, Instagram's, Dropbox, and RSS. Instagram gutted its API, Twitter shut its own, and the platforms quietly dropped RSS. CAPE didn't fail. The commons it stood on got fenced.[^nineteen]

Massimo De Angelis defines a commons as three things: a set of resources held in common, a community of commoners who govern them, and the practice of doing things in common.[^6531604] The channel keeps circling the same idea from other directions.

- "Everything we are and everything we have is a gift and has to be given away in turn." (Léon Bourgeois)[^32755227]
- "i wish earth was more like a library. ppl speaking in soft voices on the streets everything shared and borrowed."[^3469150]
- "If you have a system that is always improving, then no one gets to use it." (Donald Knuth, on software stability)[^15750124]

We all copy each other. A student quoting a corporation is theft. A corporation quoting a student is research. Same act, different direction.[^bk03]

On a thread in the channel I wrote that designers "aren't historically good at sharing their behind the scenes thinking and labor." What I like about vernacular design and free/libre open source is that what one person comes up with is "available for the taking or building upon by others." And that noticing a problem doesn't make you the right person to fix it.[^c13817473]

The lever I actually have is the syllabus. Nobody decided the discipline would run on Adobe. It got taught that way for thirty years until it stopped looking like a decision. Defaults were taught. They can be taught differently. Teach one libre tool. Require one libre font. Run one project from the public domain.[^bk08]

## 5. Say what you're sustaining

For my MFA I built a manifesto instead of writing one. I had a stack of sustainability books I couldn't get through, so I made an essay per principle that pointed back at the books. Then I noticed I'd only photographed the books. Why not use the books.

For the exhibition, the books were my actual copies, on strings, connected to screen-printed aphorisms. The definitions were printed on the front matter of found paperback novels, so the novels stayed readable and went back to the book share. The embroidery floss was cut in the longest possible pieces so it could be reused. The piece assembled in the gallery and came apart when the show closed.[^bk05]

I'd been asking what sustainable graphic design looks like. Four answers came out of the work. It looks the same. It looks eco-friendly. It looks innovative. It doesn't exist.

The last one started as despair. Everything I make is waste. Then I noticed the piece I'd just finished didn't exist either, in a different way. It reused things that already existed and left no trace. So "sustainable design doesn't exist" turned into a question: can I make a designed object with no footprint? The despair version produces nothing. The question produces work.

John Ehrenfeld says that to do something about sustainability, "you first have to say what it is that you want to sustain." Most definitions don't. The Brundtland definition says development that meets present needs without compromising future generations, and never says what is being kept going. If you don't say, you sustain the status quo. Ehrenfeld's answer is that all humans and other life should flourish. Bruce Mau got to the same place: "the welfare of all life as a practical objective."[^bk06]

I also wrote a short manifesto in the shape of the one Gropius wrote to open the Bauhaus. It says: "Our client is our climate."[^bauerden] That's an answer to the question of who a designer works for.

I don't think this has a look. I spent a long time wanting it to, and I stopped. A trope can be stolen. A value has to be enacted. Kraft paper and a leaf mark can be wrapped around anything. The recycling triangle is the whole case in one mark: nothing on the container is false, almost nobody reads it correctly, and a #6 clamshell nothing will ever recycle still gets to wear it.[^bk07] Anyone can draw a leaf. A solar-powered website goes dark when the battery does.

What the principles do share is constraint. The manifesto I wrote says constraint is not sacrifice. It comes from looking at the options you actually have.[^sust] The channel keeps agreeing.

- "This paradox—that constraints fuel creativity rather than suffocate it."[^33293111]
- "How do you do the MOST POSSIBLE with the least advanced tools?" (Steve Lacy's production philosophy)[^39992960]
- "I would have never had this idea if I didn't pick up this trash... that's why I love working with trash."[^40737618]
- Paul Hawken: "Sustainability is the forerunner of greater diversity and choice, not less."[^7417941]

The Spontaneous Lamp workshop is the same thing at bench scale. Build a working lamp from what's already in the building, engineered so it comes apart into its original parts.[^menu]

The question under all of this is who pays. "How do we stop having the same people bear the cost of what we do collectively?"[^37041792] The neighborhoods due south of Baltimore's trash incinerator have the highest rates of asthma, lung cancer, and heart disease in the city.[^bk09]

## 6. Make what belongs in the future you'd prefer

In January 2007 Steve Jobs stood on a stage and demonstrated a phone that didn't work. The features he showed were being hacked together backstage by a team of engineers, in sequence, so that each one would survive being shown once.[^bk09]

At that moment the iPhone was buildable and nobody knew whether anyone wanted one. The critics who said it didn't fit the future were describing the future correctly as it stood. Then it shipped, and the future they described stopped existing.

The future is a cone, opening away from you, and the further out you look the wider it gets. Joseph Voros grades it by likelihood: projected, probable, plausible, possible, preposterous. A sixth word cuts across all of them, which is preferable. Nobody's preferable future is the projected one. Nobody wants today extended forever.

Every design nudges the cone. So the question I'd want you to leave with is a test you can run on a poster or a career: does the thing you're making exist in the future you'd prefer?

Murray Bookchin: "The assumption that what currently exists must necessarily exist is the acid that corrodes all visionary thinking."[^7843705] Sun Ra: "The possible has been tried and failed. Now it's time to try the impossible."[^50119096]

If every design is a prediction and every prediction is wrong, the useful question is which wrong prediction you'd prefer to live in.

## 7. How it's held

The lectures I've written are almost all about the world. A lot of the channel is about the person doing the designing.

Doubt shows up more than any other attitude. Bertrand Russell: "The fundamental cause of trouble in the world is that the stupid are so confident and the intelligent are so full of doubt."[^5950110] One block just asks, "how is my thinking wrong?"[^7129483] The 2009 manifesto says it the same way: "Keep an open mind and don't blindly follow the principles."[^sust]

Process counts for its own sake. The philosopher Kieran Setiya separates "telic" activities, directed toward an end, from "atelic" ones you do for themselves. Building a house is telic, because you can't live in it while you build it. Walking in the woods is atelic.[^11726907] Another block says to replace "productive" with "processive": you can't expect to build something new every day, but you can expect to go deeper into the process.[^10342667] Another says the modernists were wrong that time spent cooking is wasted, and that the opposite is the case.[^11738595]

Care and curiosity come up together. The abstract of a paper on design education concludes "that curiosity and care are inseparable."[^19851207] "Master your craft, but don't forget to master your humanity."[^14051549] I titled a block "Slow forms please": "Cultural pressures change so fast, any slow development of form becomes impossible."[^2943323]

How I work looks like how I played in bands. At Kalamazoo I was in maybe five. When we played my songs we were this band and I sang. When we played Joe's songs we had a different name and I played bass. People moved in and out of each other's orbits, and nobody's toes got stepped on. I've worked that way since: find the people to do the thing when it needs doing, get the help I need, and be ready to help others the same way.[^prof] I've been a landscaper, a book designer, a cookware salesperson, a biodiesel maker, a web developer, a farmer, and a professor. Almost none of it did I know how to do before I did it.

## Where it doesn't hold together

**Is "design" still a useful word?** The person who replied to a comment of mine on a block about design as problem solving wrote that it "is such a broad definition that the word 'design' loses its utility altogether." Problem-solving, they said, "is not a good defense of the value of design — if we try to ground it that way, we're better off telling students to go study engineering."[^13817473] The line I've been working with, that design is translating interests, desires, and intentions into things, has the same weakness. It covers chefs and plumbers. My answer in the thread was that I was "questioning a lot of choices I've made in what kinds of design projects I do, what kinds of clients I have."[^c13817473] I didn't resolve it.

**Speculative design.** One block I saved says "so called 'speculative design' or 'critical design' are in reality institutional projects that have little use or interest outside of the institutions that generate them," and ends "Your fantasy praxis is the residue of privilege."[^1179011] I lean on Dunne and Raby and I teach the cone. My answer so far is Bookchin, and teaching. Neither is a rebuttal yet.

**Purity.** One designer using Scribus doesn't move Adobe. I'm writing this on an Apple laptop, and I still open InDesign when a client needs an InDesign file.[^bk08] The libre path also needs a computer, technical skill, free time, and the energy to share. Not everyone has those.

**The surface.** In the semiotics lecture I say the surface is a sign too. In the pure content lecture I say don't let the medium own the design. Both are mine. They probably fit: pick the surface on purpose, and keep the content able to leave it. I haven't written that down anywhere.

**This channel.** Umberto Eco warned about a student who photocopies hundreds of pages and takes them home. "The manual labor he exercises in doing so gives him the impression that he possesses the work." Owning the photocopies exempts him from reading them. "There are many things I do not know because I photocopied a text and then relaxed as if I had read it."[^1316568] The channel is 1,700 photocopies. Nassim Taleb, writing about Eco's library, gives the kinder reading: the unread books are the research tool, and he calls the collection an antilibrary.[^12894473] I can't tell which one this is.

## Three questions

Three questions sit under all of this. What is the role of a designer? Who is the client? Does a design solution need to be unique?[^q3]

Here is what the pile and the lectures say back.

Who is the client? Our climate.

Does a design solution need to be unique? No. "Everything made now is either a replica or a variant of something made a little time ago."[^42738532] The honest move is to copy, transform, combine, and keep the trail.

What is the role of a designer? Finding the problem, and deciding what's worth making and why. I'm least sure of this one.
