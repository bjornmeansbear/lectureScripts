# STRUCTURED CREATIVITY

*A weekend sprint through human-centered design — research, synthesis, prototyping, critique*

> Working title, matching the course this compresses from (DI200: Structured Creativity, a new MICA course, Fall 2026). Alternates at bottom.

---

## The pitch

Design thinking has a real critique attached to it now — not just Natasha Jen's "design thinking is bullshit," but a live 2026 counterpoint from inside the industry, and a sharper structural one about whose voices the standard process actually centers. This workshop teaches the real mechanics — research, synthesis, sacrificial prototyping, critique — while being honest about where the standard process breaks down, instead of teaching it as unquestioned gospel.

Students run the whole human-centered design cycle in miniature, on a real, local, walkable site instead of a semester-long institutional partnership — same rigor, compressed into two days.

---

## Day one — research and synthesis

Short lecture: the design-thinking critique cluster — Natasha Jen's talk, a 2026 industry counterpoint, and the sharper "who does this process actually serve" question — framed as reasons to use the process with your eyes open, not reasons to throw it out.

Then LLAT: Learn (fast background research), Look (on-site observation), Ask (short semi-structured interviews with whoever's actually using the space), Try (informal in-situ probing). Students code their raw notes — tag behaviors, breakdowns, workarounds, evidence, not opinions — then build one visual synthesis tool, a journey map, affinity map, or 2x2 grid, whichever the data actually wants. Gaps in the data become next questions, not something to paper over.

## Day two — sacrificial prototypes and critique

Intro: the sacrificial prototype — deliberately rough, fast, disposable, built to be broken and questioned, not admired. Worth naming directly why this works: ideation and prototyping are what you might call strong-link problems — you need one good idea out of many bad, cheap ones, so make a lot of them fast. The final pitch is the opposite, a weak-link problem — one broken piece drags the whole thing down, so that part earns the extra care the prototypes don't need.

Students build multiple low-fidelity prototypes fast, bring them back for real critique — swap them, question them, break them — then give a short pitch: what was observed, what the prototype claims, what's still unknown.

## Who it's for

Undergraduate studio, any level — works well as a cross-major, cohort-building exercise, not just for design majors specifically. Graduate seminar. 12–20 students.

## What they leave with

A full pass through the human-centered design cycle — research, synthesis, prototyping, critique — compressed into two days instead of a semester. Real research methods (coding, affinity mapping, journey mapping) tested against real data they gathered themselves, not a classroom hypothetical. A working, critical vocabulary for design thinking's actual limits, not just its steps.

## What you need

- Two full days
- A real, accessible local site within walking distance, with actual people to observe and interview — not a hypothetical brief
- Studio space with wall room for affinity mapping and journey mapping
- Basic prototyping materials: cardboard, tape, paper, whatever's on hand — sacrificial prototypes are supposed to be cheap
- Sticky notes, index cards, or a shared digital board (Miro, FigJam) for synthesis work

## Fee

$2,000–$3,000 plus travel and materials. Single-day version available — research-and-synthesis only, or synthesis-and-prototyping only, depending on what a department wants most.

## Bibliography

Pulled from the DI200 reading list and research channel — real citations, not all confirmed against exact editions/years yet:

- Natasha Jen, "Design Thinking is Bullsh*t" (talk) — the standard critique this workshop answers directly rather than ignoring.
- Jenny Wen (Design Lead, Anthropic; formerly Director of Design, Figma), "Why Designers Can No Longer Trust the Design Process" (Hatch Conference) — a live, industry-current counterpoint to Jen, not a repeat of the same argument.
- Nigel Cross, *Designerly Ways of Knowing*
- Richard Buchanan, "Wicked Problems in Design Thinking"
- Robert McKim, *Experiences in Visual Thinking*
- Annie Murphy Paul, *The Extended Mind: The Power of Thinking Outside the Brain*
- Adam Mastroianni, "Science Is a Strong-Link Problem" — the actual argument for why generating a pile of mediocre ideas fast is correct design strategy, not sloppiness.
- Celine Nguyen, "Research as Leisure Activity" — on what research actually is; good framing before LLAT.
- Carly Ayres, "Designers! Designers! Designers!" — on what AI does and doesn't replace in design work.
- R. Buckminster Fuller — the generalist, not specialist, framing.
- Christopher Alexander, *Notes on the Synthesis of Form* — already cited in `workshop-form-content-context/`; same source, different application here.
- IDEO Method Cards
- Ken Garland, *First Things First* (1964) and its *2000* update — already cited in `workshop-form-content-context/` too.
