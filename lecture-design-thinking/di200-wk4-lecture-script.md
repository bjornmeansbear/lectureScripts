## Part 1 · The deal (18–20 min, before anyone leaves)

This is the new material. It replaces the three duplicated concept slides and it earns its time because it is the argument that makes the rules make sense, rather than a list of rules.

### Design borrowed the method. It did not borrow the ethics.

Open here, before the consent script, because the script sounds like bureaucracy until they know why it exists.

> Everything we are doing today came out of anthropology. Participant observation, field notes, coding, the whole apparatus. Designers took it, and it works — it is genuinely the best tool for finding out how a place actually operates.
>
> It works so well that nobody argues about it any more. One writer puts it this way: the connection between design and ethnographic research is now so well established for service design that it is *almost clichéd*. [CITE] That is worth sitting with. The borrowing is finished. It is not a debate, it is a default.
>
> But anthropology spent about a century building a structure around that method, because it kept hurting people. Ethics review boards. A professional code. Informed consent as a document, not a vibe. An expectation that you go back, that the community can read what you wrote and argue with it, that you are still there in five years.
>
> Design took the method and left the structure. [CITE] makes exactly this argument — that as ethnographic methods diffused out of anthropology into design, the ethical commitments did not necessarily travel with them.

### What we have instead

Draw the contrast plainly. This is a slide worth building — two columns.

| Anthropology built | A design project has |
|---|---|
| Ethics review before you start | A deadline |
| A professional code you can be held to | A client who set the question |
| Consent as an ongoing negotiation | One conversation, if that |
| You go back. You are accountable later. | You never return |
| The account is published and contestable | The findings become a product decision |

> Nobody is going to stop us today. There is no review board for a class visiting a hotel. The Hilton said yes to the school, so the door is open and the rules are whatever we decide they are.
>
> That is not freedom. That is the ethics being carried by you personally, in the lobby, in the moment, with nobody checking.

### Consent is not transitive

Existing slide [32], and it lands much harder here, after the above, than it does at slide 32.

> The Hilton agreed to host us. No individual worker agreed to anything.
>
> The person at the front desk did not get an email. They came to work and there are twelve design students in the lobby with notebooks. As far as they know, that is a management thing and they were not asked.

### The one that is specific to a hotel

New, and the most important thing in this section. Say it slowly.

> You are going to notice workarounds. Somebody props a door, somebody keeps a laminated cheat-sheet taped under the desk, somebody skips a step because the official way takes four minutes and they have a queue.
>
> That is the best data in the building. It is also the thing that could get them written up.
>
> We are studying the building. We are not auditing the staff. Nothing in your notes should be usable by a manager to identify a person who is doing their job in a way the system did not plan for.

Then the practical rule:

> Attribute by role, never by name or shift. "A front-desk worker" — not "the tall guy on the morning desk." If a detail would let their supervisor pick them out of three people, cut the detail. Keep the behaviour.

That is also why we anonymize — not because it is a form we fill in, but because **the people most worth watching are the ones with the least power to object.**

### Design did write some ethics down — and they don't say the same thing

The "design borrowed the method, not the ethics" line above is true about *structure*, not about effort. The industry has codified something. A representative version — [UX Design Institute's five principles](https://www.uxdesigninstitute.com/blog/what-are-user-research-ethics/) — runs: transparency and informed consent, privacy and data protection, do no harm, **neutrality**, and honest interpretation.

Four of those five we are already doing today. The fifth is a problem, and it is worth thirty seconds because it is a genuine disagreement rather than an oversight.

> **Neutrality**, in the UX version, means the researcher stays impartial and keeps personal bias out of the study.
>
> Ethnography says that is not available to you. You are the instrument. You cannot be a fly on the wall, your presence changes the room, and your reactions are a signal worth recording — not contamination to be scrubbed.

Both fields are trying to solve the same worry — that you'll find what you went in wanting. They just answer it oppositely. UX says *remove yourself*. Ethnography says *you can't, so write yourself down and let the reader account for you.*

> We are doing the second one. That is why the commentary column exists, and it is why "I was annoyed by the wait" goes in the notes instead of getting deleted.

This also connects forward: the UX list's **honest interpretation** — present the whole picture, don't cherry-pick what supports your conclusion — is [9] arriving in different clothes. *Do not solve anything today* is the same instruction, given early enough to actually work.

### Nobody is going to stop you — two cases

Use these right after the "no review board for a class visiting a hotel" beat. They are design-industry failures, not anthropology ones, which is the point.

**Facebook's emotional contagion study, 2014.** Researchers altered what appeared in roughly 700,000 people's news feeds to see whether emotional tone spread. No one consented in any meaningful sense; the defence was the terms of service.

**LinkedIn's algorithm experiment.** "People You May Know" was varied across roughly 20 million users over five years, studying how weak ties affect job mobility. Some people got worse job prospects than others, and none of them were asked.

> Neither of those was done by villains. They were done by competent people with good intentions, at companies with lawyers, and at a scale where nobody involved had to look at a single participant's face.
>
> Our protection today is the opposite of theirs. We are twelve people in a lobby and everybody we affect is standing in front of us.

**CHECK before these go on a slide** — the participant counts and dates are from memory of widely-reported coverage. Confirm them.

### The pair is the safeguard, not just the second notebook

This is the bridge from your existing argument to the new one, and it is worth building a slide for. You already tell them *"nobody saw enough alone"* [15] and *"two people watched the same door and saw different things — that is why we go in teams"* [17]. Both of those are epistemic claims: two people see more.

There is a second reason, and it is ethical.

> Anthropologists have historically done fieldwork alone, for a year, and lived with what that did to them. We work in pairs, on a Monday, for an hour. One writer on design research calls it a team sport — and notes that the extra pair of eyes and ears is not only better research, it is *an extra ethical safeguard*. [CITE]
>
> Your partner is the person who says: that's a face, don't shoot it. You're following her. You've been standing over that desk for ten minutes. You are about to ask something you shouldn't.

Then the thing that makes it real rather than a sentiment:

> That only works if you both have the same rules in your head, and if either of you can say stop without it being awkward. So before you leave this room: agree with your partner, out loud, what would make you stop. Say the sentence to each other now, so it isn't the first time you've said it when it matters.

The same source makes the point that questions about research ethics are rarely simple, which is why the team has to be **a space open to challenge**. For our purposes that translates into something small and concrete: if your partner calls something, you stop first and discuss after. Not in the lobby, and not in front of the person.

### What they get back

The strongest thing design has going for it against the extraction charge is that it *builds*. One practitioner writing from inside government service design puts it as **"we make services, not reports"** — a shipped service produces a real improvement in someone's day, which is more than a published paper ever did. [CITE]

**That is this course.** We are not gathering observations to write them up. We are gathering them to design from. Everything today feeds the work you make later, and that is the right reason to be in the building.

The honest part is the lag.

> What we design will not reach the person you talked to on Monday. Not because the work isn't real — because student work doesn't ship into a working hotel, and even professional work often launches long after the research that shaped it.

The same writer names precisely this case: if a service will not directly support the people you researched — because it launches too far out, or somewhere else — **what does beneficence mean then?** That is the question for us, and it is not rhetorical. They push past payment, arguing for offering participants something beyond a gratuity: "even if that's just a sense of value in their voice, or new skills."

So the answer is not *we'll fix it for you.* It's smaller and it has to be true:

> If someone gives you ten minutes, tell them what you noticed. One sentence, not a debrief. "The thing I keep seeing is people stopping right where you said they do." Somebody who does a job every day is rarely told that the difficulty is real and that a stranger wrote it down.
>
> And we are sending the Hilton what we find. Not a thank-you note — the actual findings. That costs us one PDF and it is the whole difference between research and extraction. More on that in a minute, because *when* we send it matters as much as whether.

Worth saying plainly to the room, because it is a design constraint and not a guilt trip: **you are about to take something from people whose situation your design will not improve.** Knowing that is what keeps the observation honest. The alternative is the version where you decide in the first twenty minutes what the fix is, and spend the hour collecting evidence for it — which is [9], arriving from the ethics side.

### The other cost of anonymity

This is the counterintuitive one, and it belongs right after, because it complicates the rule I just gave them.

The same source points out what gets lost when design stops producing formal reports: **accountability**, and **the visibility of participation for people who do not wish to be anonymous.** [CITE]

> I told you to anonymize, and I gave you the reason — the people most worth watching are the ones with the least power to object.
>
> Here is the other half. Anonymity protects people, and it also erases them. The worker with the laminated cheat-sheet taped under the desk invented something. They solved a design problem the building gave them. Strip the name off and they are "a front-desk worker," and the solution becomes an anonymous finding that a designer gets credit for later.
>
> Both things are true. We anonymize today because we cannot offer anyone a real choice in a lobby in ten minutes — not because anonymity is free.

That is worth thirty seconds and it is the most honest thing in the section. It also sets up the rest of the semester, because credit and authorship is a question this class comes back to.

### Speed is the thing that will cost us

This is the last new beat, and it is the one aimed squarely at what we are doing today.

> An anthropologist gets a year. We get an hour. That speed is real and it is an advantage — insights land in a design while the design is still soft, and you get to check them again next week.
>
> The cost is that reflection is the first thing dropped. Anthropologists have time to sit with a recording, change their mind, go back. We do not get that by default. If we want it, we have to build it in on purpose — otherwise ethics just gets skipped for expedience, and nobody notices, because nothing visibly went wrong.

Then the mechanism, which is the single most useful idea in the material you sent me:

> One practitioner builds **a right to reply** into every set of discovery notes — circulating them after a session, or going back for a follow-up chat, so the people described get to say *that's not what I meant* while it still matters. [CITE]

For us that is not abstract. It is the upgrade to the promise a few slides back:

> We are not sending the Hilton a report when we're done. We are sending them what we think we found, *before* we're done, and asking where we got it wrong.

That is worth saying out loud to the room, because it changes what the notes are for. A note you know a front-desk worker may read is a note you write more carefully and less cleverly. Which is the whole discipline of the observation column, arriving from a completely different direction.

It also means the **Q column has somewhere to go.** All semester the questions have been "what only a second visit could answer." The right to reply is that second visit.

### Then the operating rules

Existing slides, run fast — they are reminders, not lessons.

## Part 2 · How to look (10 min)

Unchanged content, reordered so the abstract arrives after the concrete.

Run [2] the three methods, [4] pick a post, [5] rules for the round, [7] what you are watching for. Then the two that manage expectations — [3] the first twenty minutes are worthless, which is where **reactivity** gets named, and [8] fifty is more than you think.

**One fix to [3].** Emerson says the opposite of what that slide says: capture your first impressions *immediately*, because you lose them as the place becomes familiar. Both are true about different things, and saying so makes the slide better rather than wrong.

> The first twenty minutes are worthless for watching *people* — they know you're there and they're performing. They are the only twenty minutes you get for watching *the place*. You acclimate fast, and the version of the lobby you see at 10:00 is gone by 10:30 and does not come back.
>
> So: first five minutes, write the room. Light, sound, smell, how it pushes you, what the staff are wearing, how it feels. Then put that away and start on people.

And the recovery trick, which is free in a hotel: **once you've acclimated, watch someone arriving.** A guest walking in for the first time has the eyes you just lost.

**[17] has a canonical version worth naming.** Emerson's example is three students describing the same supermarket express line — one maps the physical space and the groceries, one narrates her own reactions, one writes about a flamboyant character in the queue. Same three minutes, three unrecognizably different accounts, none of them wrong. That is your "two people watched the same door" slide with a citation and a better story attached.

Land on [9]:

> Do not solve anything today. The moment you have a fix, you stop seeing. You will spend the rest of the hour collecting proof you were right.

That is the single sentence most likely to change what they bring back. It is currently ninth, buried. It should be the last thing they hear before they walk out.

### First, say what a code is

The deck never defines one. [19] opens with "one short tag per note" and the room is expected to infer the rest. For a second-year class meeting this properly for the first time, that is a slide.

> A **code** is a word or short phrase that assigns an attribute to a section of text — a translation, a feeling, a category, a summary, an idea. (Saldaña) **Coding** is the act of attaching that word to a piece of raw data in order to interpret it. (Charmaz & Mitchell)
>
> The point of it is to filter and organise the raw pile so you can see patterns, find themes, and end up with something that answers the question you came in with. (Bogdan & Biklen)

Two things worth saying immediately after, because both are the failure modes you already have slides about:

> A code is not a summary. It is a handle — something short enough that you can pick the note up by it later and find its twelve siblings.
>
> And a code is not a verdict. "Bad design" is a verdict. [20]

### The vocabulary ladder — and a slippage in the deck

That Bogdan & Biklen line gives you the sequence the deck is missing: **codes → patterns → themes → an answer.** Right now four words are in play across the slides and their relationship is never stated.

- [19] uses **tag** and **code** interchangeably
- [21] says **"a theme is what you saw more than once"**
- [22] says reusing a tag **"is the finding"**
- [24] builds a **pattern statement** out of tags that co-occur

So a student hears that a repeated tag is a theme, and also that it's a finding, and also that co-occurring tags make a pattern. Those are three different claims and they are all doing the job of one.

The cleanest version, and it costs one slide:

> **Code** — the handle on one note. **Pattern** — the same code, or the same cluster of codes, showing up again and again. **Theme** — what that pattern is *about*; the thing you'd say if someone asked what you learned. **Then** you have something to design from.

Then [21] becomes *"a pattern is what you saw more than once"* — which is what it actually describes — and **theme** is reserved for the interpretive step above it, which is what [24] and [25] are really doing. One word change on [21], and the four slides stop competing.

**On tag vs. code:** I said earlier to pick one. Better to declare the relationship and keep both — it costs a sentence and it removes the problem, which is not that two words exist but that the deck never says they're the same word.

> Ethnographers call this **coding**. You can think of it as **tagging** if that's easier — it's the same move. A short word put on a note, so you can find it again later, and so you can see when the same thing keeps coming back.
>
> I'll use both. Everything you read will say coding, so that's the one to know.

That also front-loads *pattern* — "when the same thing keeps coming back" — so [21] has somewhere to land instead of introducing the idea cold.

### Three more from *Ethnography Made Easy*

The Saldaña / Charmaz / Bogdan & Biklen definitions above come from the coding chapter of **[*Ethnography Made Easy*](https://ethnographymadeeasy.commons.gc.cuny.edu/coding/)**, a CUNY open textbook written by instructors at Guttman Community College; the coding chapter is credited to Torres-Rivera (2019). It is free, it is written for exactly your students' level, and it is the most assignable thing on this list. Three things in it the deck needs.

**1 · Coding is a cycle, not three passes.** The chapter frames it as a repeating loop: **code → write memos → review, revise, refine** — and around again. That is a better shape than [19]'s linear open → in vivo → axial, for two reasons. It puts memoing inside the process rather than beside it, which is the Emerson point arriving from a second direction. And a loop is already your course's vocabulary — Week 1 defines *changing* as "a process, not an event. Iteration."

**2 · Saturation — the answer to "how do I know when I'm done?"** You will get this question at about 3:30pm and the deck has no answer. The chapter's is clean: you stop when **no new information seems to emerge** — when another pass through the pile stops producing codes you don't already have.

> You're not done when you've tagged everything. You're done when the next twenty notes don't make you invent anything new.

The chapter is careful that there is **no single threshold** — no bell. When the notes start feeling predictable, the same codes keep coming up, and your answer to the question seems reasonable, you decide whether another lap is worth it. That judgement call is the skill, and saying so protects the students who assume there's a rule they missed.

That also quietly justifies the pooling argument: with your fifty you hit saturation almost immediately, because you run out of your own material. With six hundred you don't.

**3 · Coding families — the scaffold for students who freeze.** "No categories decided in advance" is correct and it is also paralysing if you have never done this. The chapter offers families to think *with* rather than categories to sort *into*: **setting, definition of the situation, perspectives, activity, strategy, relationship.**

Two of those are worth naming out loud for a hotel:

> **Activity codes** — what people are doing. **Strategy codes** — how people get around something that isn't working.
>
> Strategy is where your workarounds live. The propped door, the cheat-sheet under the desk. That is a named category in the literature, and it is the single richest thing you will find in that building.

Which also closes the loop back to Part 1: the strategy codes and the ethics warning are about the same notes.

### What each phase actually involves

The deck teaches the passes but not the operations. These three are what a student actually *does*, and none of them are in it.

**Memos are informal, and that needs saying.** Students hear "write a memo" and freeze into paragraphs. The chapter is explicit: memos are short journal entries for your own use — bullets, fragments, no full sentences required, written fast. Say that out loud or you'll get twelve people writing essays instead of thinking.

> Bullets. Fragments. Nobody reads it but you. "keeps happening at the elevator — is this a sightline thing or a signage thing? check tomorrow."

**Conceptualization — define your codes in concrete terms.** This is the best single fix for the bad-tag problem on [20], and it's better than my bad-tag gallery because the student finds the fault themselves.

> Take your five most-used tags and write a one-line definition of each. Specific and concrete — what has to be true for a note to get this tag?
>
> If you can't finish the sentence, the tag is a mood, not a code. "Friction" dies at this step. "Checking a sign twice" survives it.

Definitions go in the memo, and future coding gets more intentional because the characteristics are decided rather than felt.

**Merge, divide, rename, eliminate.** The review phase has four concrete operations, and every one gets logged in the memo. That's the whole of it:

> You may find `listener` and `fan` are one thing — merge them, and write down which word you kept. You may find `waiting` is two things, waiting-in-a-queue and waiting-because-lost — divide it. Rename anything vague. Delete anything that never earned its second use.

Logging the decision matters because otherwise you re-litigate it at the next session, and because the merge itself is often the finding.

### One tension to resolve out loud

The chapter says **code frequency does not necessarily indicate importance.** Your deck says the opposite twice — [21] *"a theme is what you saw more than once"* and [22] *"that reuse **is** the finding."*

You already have the reconciliation on [21]: *"anything you saw once is a story. Keep it, it might be the best thing you have — but it is not yet a pattern."* Say both halves together and the tension becomes the lesson:

> Repetition tells you something is a pattern. It does not tell you the pattern matters. The single most important thing in your notes might have happened once — and it is your job, not the tally's, to decide.

That is also the honest version of "emergence is not mystical" [22]: the counting is mechanical, the judgement is not.

### Four additions from Emerson

Emerson's *Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes* ch. 6 is the source for most of what [19] does, and it has four things the deck doesn't. Emerson, Fretz & Shaw, *Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes*, ch. 6, "Processing Fieldnotes: Coding and Memoing" — summary at [jonkolko.com](https://www.jonkolko.com/phd/writing/26-02-08-processing-fieldnotes)

**1 · Read your notes as if a stranger wrote them.** Emerson's first move, before any coding: re-read the whole set, and read it as someone else's work. That is thirty seconds to say and it changes the O / I / Q sort completely — your own interpretations are invisible to you as interpretations, and they stop being invisible the moment the notes belong to somebody else.

> Before you sort anything: read all fifty straight through, and read them like you didn't write them. The ones that only make sense if you were there are the ones that need rewriting. The ones that sound like conclusions are the ones in the wrong column.

Put this in front of [16].

**2 · Code processes, not nouns.** This is the sharpest practical rule in the chapter and it fixes a real inconsistency in your starter tags. The list on [19] currently reads: *hesitation · wayfinding · signage · waiting · crowding · friction · workaround · staff support · access · comfort · asks for help · hidden labour.*

Some of those are things people **do** — waiting, asks for help, workaround. Some are things that **exist** — signage, access, comfort. Emerson's priority is the first kind, because a noun names a feature of the building and a verb names something that happened.

> "Signage" is a thing on a wall. "Checking the sign twice" is a thing a person did. Only one of those is an observation, and only one of them will cluster with anything.

Worth rewriting the starter list in gerunds before Monday. *Hesitating · checking a sign · asking staff · working around · queueing · doubling back · giving up.* That single change would do more for the quality of the wall than any other edit in this deck.

**3 · Memos — the actual gap.** [19] teaches coding and stops. Emerson's chapter is titled *Coding and Memoing*, and the pairing is the point: codes are labels, memos are where the thinking happens. Without them a student ends the day with sixty tags and no ideas.

The cheap classroom version:

> Every time you reuse a tag for the third time, stop and write three sentences about why it keeps coming back. Not a better tag — a thought. What is this actually about? What would confirm it? What would kill it?

That is Emerson's theoretical memo, compressed to something that fits in an afternoon. It also gives the pattern statements on [25] something to be built from, instead of being generated cold from a template.

**4 · Write with the audience in mind, at the focused stage.** Emerson's point about the second pass is that you start writing for people who weren't there. Which for you connects straight to the right-to-reply promise in Part 1 — the audience is the Hilton, and they will read it.

### Two corrections

**"In vivo" is not a pass.** [19] lists open → in vivo → axial as three stages. In vivo codes are a *kind* of code — participant's own words — that occur *during* open coding. They aren't a stage between open and axial. Easy fix: make [19] two passes, open and focused, with in vivo shown as a technique inside the first.

**And a vocabulary mismatch worth settling.** "Axial coding" is Strauss and Corbin; Emerson and Charmaz say "focused coding." Both describe the same second pass. If you're pointing students at Emerson, use his word, or say out loud that the two traditions name it differently — otherwise a student who reads the chapter finds a term that isn't in your deck.

### And one thing I told you earlier that was wrong

I suggested capping the room at ~15 tags to force reuse. Emerson says the opposite for the open pass: **generate as many codes as possible**, no pre-set categories, and let consolidation happen in the focused pass — which is what your axial step already is. The cap would suppress exactly the variety the second pass needs. Drop it.

The other three still stand: the bad-tag gallery, negative cases after [24], and putting the twelve O/I/Q ratios up on the board.
