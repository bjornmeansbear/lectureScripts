# Field card — Hilton, 14 Sep

## The one rule: show, don't tell

Write what a camera and a microphone would have caught. Evaluative words are useless tomorrow; concrete detail rebuilds the whole scene.

| Don't write | Write |
|---|---|
| Front desk was inefficient | 4 in line, 1 agent, 6 min from join to counter |
| Guest seemed frustrated | "I booked a king, this is a queen." Phone face-down on counter |
| Confusing signage | Stopped, looked left at sign, looked back at doors, waited ~6 sec, went right |
| Staff were helpful | Agent left the desk, walked the guest to the elevator, pressed the button |

**Never write why.** You saw the sigh; you did not see the reason. Record how the feeling got expressed and how people around them treated it. The cause is not observable.

## First twenty minutes: write the place, not the people

People behave differently because you are visibly there — that fades if you are boring and stay put. So the *behaviour* in the first twenty minutes is not trustworthy yet.

Your own fresh eyes are, and only now. Spend the first five minutes on the sensory field, because you will lose it as the place becomes normal:

> light · sound · smell · temperature · how the space pushes people · what staff wear · how staff hold themselves · the feeling of the room

Once you have acclimated, **watch someone arriving.** A guest walking in for the first time has the eyes you just lost. Their hesitations are the ones you can no longer feel.

## What to jot, standing up

- **Exact words, in quote marks.** Verbatim beats paraphrase every time.
- **The sequence.** Who did what, in what order, and how the other person answered.
- **Gesture and posture, tied to what was said.** The glance at a colleague. The bag set down. The phone turned over.
- **Space and objects.** Where people stand, what they touch, where the line bends, what they do with luggage while waiting.
- **Times.** `10:14` costs you a second and anchors everything.
- **Hunches**, even when you can't say why. Mark them `?`. They connect later.

## Where meaning concentrates in a hotel

Go looking for these three. They are where the good notes are.

**Thresholds and waits** — arrival, the check-in queue, the elevator, the bag, checkout. Watch how people manage the wait: what they do with their hands, when they look at the desk, when they give up and sit.

**Troubles and repairs** — a booking error, a late room, a noise complaint. Note *both* moves: what the guest does, and what staff do to fix it. Also note the distinctions staff draw between kinds of guest or kinds of problem. Those distinctions are the building's real categories.

**Handoffs** — desk to bellhop, housekeeping to desk, shift to shift. Where does a thing get passed along, and what gets dropped in the pass?

## Two columns, always

| Observation | Commentary |
|---|---|
| Timestamped. Verbatim quotes. What a camera caught. | What you think it means. What to ask later. What you brought in with you. |

Your reactions belong in the right column, labelled. *"I was annoyed by the wait and I think it shaped the next ten minutes"* is better data than pretending you were neutral.

A strong reaction is a **lead, not a finding.** If something surprises or irritates you, note it — then check whether anyone in the building sees it that way.

## Logistics

- Small pad. **Eyes up.** Develop shorthand — nose in the notebook means you miss the thing you came for.
- Jot every 20–30 minutes, and immediately after anything notable.
- If jotting openly would break the moment, step out — hallway, back of the lobby — and write it down within a minute.
- **Full notes the same day.** On the light rail, not that evening. Jottings only trigger memory while the memory is still there.

## Ethics, short version

- Say what you're doing if asked. You're writing things down to remember them accurately.
- **Attribute by role, never by name, shift, or description.** "A front-desk worker," not "the tall guy on mornings."
- Workarounds are the best data in the building and the thing that could get someone written up. **We study the building, not the staff.**
- **Never:** faces, name badges, guest information, screens, luggage tags, audio without asking, following anyone, standing over a work area.
- Your partner can stop you at any time, and you stop first and discuss after.

---

**You are not capturing the truth of the hotel.** You are producing one interpretation, shaped by where you stood and what you chose to write down. Your partner will come back with a different hotel. Compare deliberately — the differences are data, not errors.
