# Week 5 — Coding Day

## Why this gets its own day

Week 4 produced roughly six hundred notes across twelve people. Nobody touches the Hilton today. Today is the shift Emerson names precisely: from producing field notes to *sifting* them — reading for threads that can be connected into something that speaks to people who were never in that lobby.

That shift is the whole day. Say it at the top:

> Last week you were the instrument. Today you're the reader. Different job, same notes.

## Part 1 · Read, sort, define (12 min)

### Read it like a stranger wrote it

Emerson's first move, before any coding: re-read the whole pool, and read it as someone else's work. Put this before the sort — it changes what lands in which column, because your own interpretations are invisible to you as interpretations until the notes belong to somebody else.

> Before you sort anything: read straight through everyone's notes, and read them like you didn't write them. The ones that only make sense if you were there need rewriting. The ones that sound like conclusions are in the wrong column.

### Sort into O / I / Q

[04] the sort card, [05] where each column goes. Three columns, one home per line, count each column.

> This is why we sort before we code. "Bad design" never reaches the wall, because it went in the middle column ten minutes ago.

[06] Two people watched the same door and saw different things — that's not a problem with the notes, it's why the pairs went in as pairs.

### What a code actually is

Nobody has defined this yet. Say it plainly before the room starts tagging:

> A **code** is a word or short phrase that assigns an attribute to a section of text — a translation, a feeling, a category, a summary, an idea. (Saldaña) **Coding** is the act of attaching that word to a piece of raw data in order to interpret it. (Charmaz & Mitchell)
>
> The point of it is to filter and organise the raw pile so you can see patterns, find themes, and end up with something that answers the question you came in with. (Bogdan & Biklen)

Two failure modes, immediately after:

> A code is not a summary. It's a handle — something short enough that you can pick the note up by it later and find its twelve siblings.
>
> And a code is not a verdict. "Bad design" is a verdict, not an observation. What actually happened? Code for that — a Norman door, a six-second wait, not "bad design."

### Tag or code — pick a relationship, not a word

> Ethnographers call this **coding**. You can think of it as **tagging** if that's easier — it's the same move. A short word put on a note, so you can find it again later, and so you can see when the same thing keeps coming back.
>
> I'll use both. Everything you read will say coding, so that's the one to know.

### The vocabulary ladder

Four words are about to be in play — code, tag, pattern, theme — and the class needs the relationship between them stated once, before any of them gets used loosely.

> **Code** — the handle on one note. **Pattern** — the same code, or the same cluster of codes, showing up again and again. **Theme** — what that pattern is *about*; the thing you'd say if someone asked what you learned. **Then** you have something to design from.

## Part 2 · How coding works (15 min)

### It's a cycle, not three passes

> **Code.** One short tag per note. No categories decided in advance — that's open coding. Use their words where you can.
>
> **Write a memo.** Stop and put down what you're starting to think. Not a better tag — a thought.
>
> **Review, revise, refine.** Merge, divide, rename, delete. Then go round again with the next stack.

Round and round until it stops producing anything new — the same shape as *changing* all semester: a process, not an event.

**A term worth naming out loud, once:** "in vivo" isn't a fourth stage — it's a kind of code, the participant's own words, that shows up *during* this first open pass. And if anyone reads ahead and finds "axial coding," that's Strauss and Corbin's word for the second pass; Emerson and Charmaz call the same move "focused coding." Same thing, two traditions. Use whichever, just say so.

**One instinct to resist:** don't cap the room at some tidy number of tags to force reuse. Generate as many as you can. Consolidation is the *next* pass's job, not this one's.

### Code what people do, not what the building has

[13] the cols slide. "Signage" is a thing on a wall. "Access" is a property of the lobby. "Comfort" is a mood. None of those happened.

> Hesitating · checking a sign twice · doubling back · asking staff · queueing · working around · giving up.
>
> Verbs. Every one of these is something a person did, at a time you can point to. Only one kind of tag will cluster with anything, and it's this one.

### Six families, if you freeze

"No categories decided in advance" is correct and also paralysing the first time you do this. These are families to think *with*, not boxes to sort *into*:

> Setting. Definition of the situation. Perspectives. Activity. Strategy. Relationship.
>
> **Strategy** is where your workarounds live — the propped door, the cheat-sheet taped under the desk. That's the richest thing in that building, and it's the one we agreed to protect.

### Watch it happen

[15] one pair's notes, tagged — walk the room through the right-hand column. *Hesitation* shows up three times because they kept needing it.

[16] Emergence is not mystical:

> You reach for a tag you've already used. That reuse **is** the finding — the concept came out of the data, because you didn't have it before you started tagging.
>
> Nothing emerges from your fifty. Things emerge from all six hundred. That's why the pile had to be pooled.

## Part 3 · Memos and saturation (15 min)

### Three things a memo is not

> **Not an essay.** Bullets. Fragments. No full sentences required. Nobody reads it but you.
>
> **Not a better tag.** It's the thinking the tag can't hold. "Keeps happening at the elevator — sightline thing or signage thing? Check tomorrow."
>
> **Not optional.** Without it you finish today with sixty tags and no ideas.

The cheap classroom rule: **the third time you reach for the same tag, stop and write three lines about why it keeps coming back.** That's a theoretical memo, compressed to fit an afternoon — and it's what the pattern statements in Part 5 get built from, instead of being invented cold.

### Conceptualization — define your five most-used tags

> What has to be true for a note to get this tag? Specific, concrete.
>
> "Friction" dies at this step. "Checking a sign twice" survives it.

The definitions go in the memo. The next round of coding gets sharper because the characteristics are decided, not felt.

### Merge, divide, rename, eliminate

> **Merge.** Two tags turn out to be one thing — write down which word you kept.
>
> **Divide.** "Waiting" is really waiting-in-a-queue and waiting-because-lost.
>
> **Rename.** Anything vague.
>
> **Eliminate.** Anything that never earned a second use.

Log every one in the memo. Otherwise you argue it again next session — and the merge is often the finding.

### When am I done?

You'll get this question. The answer is saturation, and it has no bell:

> You're not done when you've tagged everything. You're done when the next twenty notes don't make you invent anything new.
>
> There's no single threshold. When the notes start feeling predictable, the same codes keep coming up, and your answer starts looking reasonable — you decide whether another lap is worth it. That judgement call is the skill.

## Part 4 · Codes into patterns (18 min)

### Frequency is a signal, not a verdict

Rename [21]'s language here — it was "theme," it should always have been "pattern":

> A **pattern** is what you saw more than once. Anything you saw once is a story — keep it, it might be the best thing you have, but it's not yet a pattern.

Say the tension out loud, because the room will otherwise hear two contradictory rules:

> Repetition tells you something is a pattern. It does not tell you the pattern matters. The single most important thing in your notes might have happened once — and it's your job, not the tally's, to decide.

### One example, before the method

> Hesitation + signage + asks staff + staff workaround.
>
> People stop at the entrance because the signage answers the wrong question — and the desk has quietly absorbed that as part of the job.
>
> That idea is in none of the five notes. It's in the fact that the same tags kept coming back, and that somebody had already taped up a fix.

That's one cluster, spotted by eye. Now do it with the whole wall.

### Affinity diagramming — the method, not the vibe

This is the technique the room needs and the deck has never actually taught — it names something they'd otherwise do by feel, badly, under time pressure.

> **Generate, alone, five minutes.** One small note per tag that's come up three or more times. No talking yet — a room that clusters while it's still generating just follows whoever spoke first.
>
> **Cluster.** Post everything up. Move notes near ones that feel related. Don't name anything yet — group first, decide what it means after. Naming too early forces notes into a box that fits the label instead of the data.
>
> **Split the disagreement.** If two of you want the same note in two different clusters, duplicate it. Don't argue it into one place — the disagreement is often the more interesting finding than either cluster alone.
>
> **Name each cluster**, last, from what's actually sitting under it.

> A small cluster is not noise. Three notes that don't fit anywhere else are someone in your pair seeing something the rest of the room didn't. Don't fold it into a bigger pile just to tidy the wall.

If two clusters look equally promising and you can't agree which to build a pattern statement from first, dot-vote it rather than debate it — three dots each, put them where you think the room should look first.

### You just built an affinity map

> Getting everything where you can see it at once, and moving it until it groups — that's an affinity map. It has a name because an anthropologist built it for exactly this problem in the 1960s.

Two more tools do the same move with a different variable, and they're not today's:

> A **journey map** adds time — one person, start to finish, in order. A **service blueprint** adds everyone you never saw — what has to happen backstage for the front to work. There's a handout.

## Part 5 · Pattern statements (12 min)

[27] the template, on the card. Every group writes one, out loud, to the room:

> People struggle with ___ because ___, which affects ___.

[28] the workshop card:

> One pattern statement per group, full sentence, all three blanks filled. Name the notes it came from — if you can't, it isn't a pattern yet. Two minutes to the room: your sentence, and one question you still can't answer.

Bring back the sentence and its note numbers. The rest goes into the Canvas post by Wednesday.

Initial memos have been doing solitary work all day — this is where they start doing collective work. A group's pattern statement is, in miniature, what Emerson calls an integrative memo: it stops being about one pair's notes and starts being a claim about the building.

## Part 6 · Close (5 min)

Debrief, specific to today rather than to the round:

> What tag did you almost throw away that turned out to matter?
>
> Where did your cluster disagree with your partner's — and who turned out to be right?
>
> What's still a question, not yet a pattern?

Close on the ladder's last rung:

> Now you have something to design from.
