Glaser & Strauss, 1967 · The Discovery of Grounded Theory

Grounded theory.

Two sociologists at UC San Francisco.

They had no hypothesis to test. They were observing in and had copious notes, and they built the account out of the notes.

Normal science

Grounded theory

  • 02Compare

Part four · what counts as evidence

Not everything
you write down
is evidence.

One test. Use it on every line you are about to write.

Could a camera
have caught it?

If yes, it is an observation. If no, it is something else — and something else is not worthless, it just has to be labelled.

Observation 10:14 — person reaches the doors, stops, looks left at the sign, looks back at the doors, waits ~6 sec, then goes right
Interpretation The entrance doesn't say which side opens. They are using the sign to check a guess they already made
Question Does this happen to people who have been here before? Ask someone at the desk: do they get asked?

Three different kinds of thing. The failure is not having interpretations — it is writing one down as though a camera caught it.

Not evidence
  • ✕“People are confused”
  • ✕“The signage is bad”
  • ✕“It feels unwelcoming”
  • ✕“They waited a while”

  • ✓“Paused 6 sec at the door”
  • ✓“Looked at the sign, then asked staff”
  • ✓“Three people in nine minutes did the same thing”
  • ✓“Hand-lettered sign taped over the plaque”

A fieldworker, on her first weeks of dissertation notes
I wrote my fieldnotes as a running log of everything I saw. The stuff I wrote in those first few weeks ended up being almost completely unusable. Because it lacked detail. And because it lacked context.

Writing more is not the fix. Choosing what to write up is the skill.

After you leave. Three steps, in this order.
  • 01Dump. Running log of everything you saw. Fast, ugly, no editing. Ten minutes.
  • 02Circle three. The three moments most worth keeping — plus anything that does not fit the pattern. Always circle the thing that does not fit.
  • 03Write those three up properly. Context, who was there, what happened, in what order, what changed.

You are not writing down everything. There are not enough hours and there never will be.

d.school Ethnography Field Guide · the observation scaffold
What?What is the person doing? The obvious and the surprising. Objective facts only — no guessing yet.
How?How are they doing it? Does it take effort? Are they rushed, careful, stuck? Is it working for them or against them?
Why?Why this way? Here you are guessing at motivation — and you mark it as a guess. This is the column that becomes your interview questions.

Divide the sheet in three. When you do not know what to write, start at What and walk right.

What a fieldnote has in it · Singleton & Straits, via Sangasubana 2011
  • 01Running description. The setting, the people, individual actions, group behaviour, points of view. Record it; do not analyse it while you are still standing there.
  • 02Forgotten episodes. Things from earlier that come back to you on site. Write them down the moment they surface.
  • 03Ideas for next time. Who to look for, where to stand, what to follow up.
  • 04Personal impressions and feelings. Your own reactions — they are the clue to what is biasing the rest of the page.
  • 05Methodological notes. What went wrong with your method. Where you could not see. What you would do differently.

Header on every page: your name · the place · the date · time in, time out. Draw the plan before you write a word.
