Before you sort anything
Read straight through everyone's notes, and read them like you didn't write them. The ones that only make sense if you were there need rewriting. The ones that sound like conclusions are in the wrong column.

Sort your notes

  • 01Three columns on a sheet: O / I / Q.
  • 02Put every one in exactly one column. No line gets two homes.
  • 03Count each column. Write the three numbers at the top.
  • 04Take your five weakest O's and rewrite them so a camera could have caught them.
Where each column goes
Observation These get coded. Nothing else does. Only the O column goes into notion.
Interpretation These become your memos. You are not deleting them — you are moving them somewhere they are useful.
Question These go on the questions list. That list is what a second visit is for, and it is what we send back to the Hilton.

This is why we sort before we code. "Bad design" never reaches the wall, because it went in the middle column ten minutes ago.

What a code actually is
A code is a word or short phrase that assigns an attribute to a section of text — a translation, a feeling, a category, a summary, an idea. Coding is the act of attaching that word to a piece of raw data in order to interpret it.
Saldaña; Charmaz & Mitchell — via Ethnography Made Easy

The point of it: filter and organise the raw pile so you can see patterns, find themes, and end up with something that answers the question you came in with. (Bogdan & Biklen)

A code is not a summary.
A code is not a verdict.

A code is a handle — short enough that you can pick the note up by it later and find its twelve siblings.

"Bad design" is a verdict, not an observation.

Code what happened,
not what you concluded.

"Bad design" is not a code. What actually happened? Code for that — a Norman door, a six-second wait, not a verdict.

Ethnographers call it coding.

Think of it as tagging if that is easier — it is the same move. A short word put on a note, so you can find it again later, and so you can see when the same thing keeps coming back.

I will use both. Everything you read says coding, so that is the one to know.

The vocabulary ladder
  • —Code. The handle on one note.
  • —Pattern. The same code, or the same cluster of codes, showing up again and again.
  • —Theme. What that pattern is about — the thing you'd say if someone asked what you learned.

Then you have something to design from.

Coding is a cycle, not a checklist
  • 01Code. One short tag per note. No categories decided in advance — that is open coding. Use their words where you can: "the mornings are the worst", not "morning workflow inefficiency."
  • 02Write a memo. Stop and put down what you are starting to think. Not a better tag — a thought.
  • 03Review, revise, refine. Merge, divide, rename, delete. Then go round again with the next stack.

Round and round until it stops producing anything new. It is iteration, same as everything else in this class.

Stuck for a tag? Six families to think with
  • —Setting. Where this happens, and what the place is like.
  • —Definition of the situation. What people seem to think is going on.
  • —Perspectives. How they talk about it.
  • —Activity. What people are actually doing.
  • —Strategy. How people get around something that is not working.
  • —Relationship. Who is dealing with whom, and how.

These are prompts, not boxes. Strategy is where the workarounds live — the propped door, the cheat-sheet taped under the desk. That is the richest thing in the building, and it is the one we agreed to protect.

Emergence is not mystical.

3 Things a memo is not

  • 01Not an essay. Bullets. Fragments. No full sentences required. Nobody reads it but you.
  • 02Not a better tag. It is the thinking the tag can't hold. "keeps happening at the elevator — sightline thing or signage thing? check tomorrow"
  • 03Not optional. Without it you finish the day with sixty tags and no ideas.
The third time you reach for the same tag, stop and write three lines about why it keeps coming back.
Define your five most-used tags. One line each.

If you can't finish the sentence, it isn't a code.

What has to be true for a note to get this tag? Specific, concrete.

"Friction" dies at this step. "Checking a sign twice" survives it. Write the definitions in your memo and the next round of coding gets sharper on its own.

Then: review, revise, refine
  • —Merge. Two tags turn out to be one thing. Write down which word you kept.
  • —Divide. "waiting" is really waiting-in-a-queue and waiting-because-lost.
  • —Rename. Anything vague.
  • —Eliminate. Anything that never earned a second use.

Log every one of these in the memo. Otherwise you argue it again next session — and the merge is often the finding.

When am I
done?

Saturation. When another lap stops making you invent anything — same codes, over and over, and your answer is starting to look reasonable.

There is no bell. No threshold, no number. You decide whether one more round would be worth it, and that judgement is the skill.

A pattern is what you saw more than once.

Anything you saw once is a story. Keep it, it might be the best thing you have — but it is not yet a pattern.

Repetition tells you something is a pattern. It does not tell you the pattern matters. The most important thing in your notes might have happened once — that's your call, not the tally's.

Affinity map — the steps

  • 01Generate, alone, five minutes. One small note per tag that's come up three or more times. No talking yet.
  • 02Cluster. Post everything up. Move notes near ones that feel related. Don't name anything yet.
  • 03Split the disagreement. Two of you want a note in two clusters — duplicate it. Don't argue it into one place.
  • 04Name each cluster, last, from what's actually sitting under it.
Why in that order  naming first forces notes into a box that fits the label, not the data.

A small cluster is not noise.

Three notes that don't fit anywhere else are someone in your pair seeing something the rest of the room didn't. Don't fold it into a bigger pile just to tidy the wall.

Two clusters look equally promising and you can't agree which to write up first? Dot-vote it — three dots each — instead of debating it.

You just built an affinity map.

Getting everything where you can see it at once, and moving it until it groups — that's an affinity map. It has a name because an anthropologist built it for exactly this problem in the 1960s.

Two more tools do the same move with a different variable. A journey map adds time — one person, start to finish. A service blueprint adds everyone you never saw — what has to happen backstage for the front to work. Not today. There is a handout.
