Our whole class in a sentence?

  • 03“devises courses of action” — you have to propose something. Prototyping.
  • 04“changing” — a process, not an event. Iteration.

Part one

Where it
came from

None of this was new in 1962.

People had been doing the methods for centuries. What arrives in the twentieth century is the naming, the codifying, and eventually the selling.

Before it had a name
1452–1519Leonardo da Vinci. Roughly seven thousand surviving notebook pages. He drew to think, not to record — same object over and over, annotated and revised. He built working models. He dissected bodies himself instead of trusting the textbooks. Research, iteration, prototyping.
1919–1933The Bauhaus. The Vorkurs — the preliminary course under Johannes Itten, then László Moholy-Nagy, then Josef Albers — taught by “learning by doing”: paper folding, collage, model building. Discover what a material does by manipulating it. Every workshop had two masters, one craftsman and one artist. That is prototyping as a curriculum. The Vkhutemas school in Moscow, 1920–1930, was doing a parallel version.
The line you can actually trace
1927Max Bill enrolls at the Bauhaus in Dessau.
1953Bill co-founds the Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm — the Ulm School of Design. It adds cybernetics, operations research, ergonomics, and semiotics to a Bauhaus foundation.
1958Horst Rittel becomes Professor of Design Methodology at Ulm. That is the job title, in 1958.
1963Berkeley recruits Rittel. He founds the Design Methods Group there in 1967.
1973Rittel and Webber publish wicked problems.
1962 — 1973
1962Conference on Design Methods, London. Organized in part by John Christopher Jones, a Welsh ergonomist at Associated Electrical Industries, Manchester. Leads to the Design Research Society, 1966.
1964Christopher Alexander (1936–2022), Berkeley. Notes on the Synthesis of Form, Harvard University Press. Breaking a design problem into parts and solving it mathematically.
1969Herbert Simon (1916–2001), Carnegie Mellon. The Sciences of the Artificial, MIT Press. Also gives us satisficing.
1970 — 1973
1970John Christopher Jones (1927–2022). Design Methods: Seeds of Human Futures, Wiley. The definitive catalogue of the movement. Same year: first Professor of Design at the Open University.
1971Victor Papanek (1923–1998). Design for the Real World, Pantheon. “There are professions more harmful than industrial design, but only a very few of them.”
1973Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber, Berkeley. “Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning,” Policy Sciences 4. Wicked problems.

Wicked problems

  • 01No definitive formulation. How you frame it is the solution.
  • 02No stopping rule. You stop when you run out of time or money.
  • 03Not true or false. Only better or worse.
  • 04Every one is unique, and every attempt changes the problem.
1971 — Christopher Alexander, new preface to Notes on the Synthesis of Form
I reject the whole idea of design methods as a subject of study, since I think it is absurd to separate the study of designing from the practice of design.

Seven years after the founding text, its author repudiates the field it started — in the preface to that same book.

The founders quit
1971Alexander repudiates design methods. Goes on to A Pattern Language (1977) and The Timeless Way of Building (1979).
1970sJones turns away from systematic method toward chance and improvisation, reading John Cage. Chairs the Design Research Society 1971–73. Designing Designing, 1991.

Their objection — method substituting for judgment — is the same one Natasha Jen makes in 2017.

  • 02It made prototyping cheap and normal — out of art school, into hospitals and city governments.

Where’s the criticism?

  • —A religion — fixed liturgy, evangelists, no heretics
  • —The Post-it as emblem. Visible is not the same as rigorous.
  • —Missing: evidence and expertise

Not academics sniping at industry. The top of commercial practice saying the commercial product is thin.

Natasha Iskander · HBR, 2018

Design thinking is fundamentally conservative and preserves the status quo

Empathy takes existing conditions as given. It brackets out politics and power. Speed and delight favor solutions that don’t disturb the arrangement.

You end up making a bad situation more pleasant.

On the board all session
  • 01Who is the audience? What is being sold?
  • 02What gets left out?
  • 03Where is the research, the failure, the politics?
  • 04How does design get packaged, and for whom?

Why this matters
right now

  • 01It’s the working language of the field. You’ll be interviewed by people who use these words.
  • 02October 2023: IDEO cut about a third of its staff and closed Munich and Tokyo. Half its headcount gone since 2020. You’re inheriting the aftermath, not the hype.
  • 03Generating forty options fast used to be the expensive part. A machine does that in a minute now.

What’s left is knowing which problem is worth solving, and judging whether an answer is any good. Evidence and expertise — exactly what Jen said was missing.

You can build the conditions
for a good idea.

Put people in a room who don’t normally talk. Go look instead of guessing. Make twenty bad prototypes so the twenty-first is good!

Design-thinking doesn't supply success or ideas, it creates space for success and ideas to happen

A process amplifies what you brought into the room. Bring nothing and it amplifies nothing — a wall covered in Post-its and no idea on it.

The process is not what will make your project good. What you notice will make it good.

Research

weak-link

Quality depends on your worst input. One leading question and everything built on top inherits the damage.

Slow down. Rigor lives here.

 

Prototyping

strong-link

Quality depends on your best output. Nineteen of twenty can be bad — nobody builds on the bad ones.

Speed up. Being precious kills you.

Most design anxiety is rigor spent in the wrong place — careful about an ugly sketch, careless about an interview.

Record

  • ✓Spaces, objects, wear, traces, hand-written signs
  • ✓Your own drawings, always
  • ✓Quotes attributed by role — “a front desk agent said…”
  • ✓Counts, durations, sequences

Never

  • ✕Faces. Not in the background, not “they’re blurry”
  • ✕Name badges or anything with a name on it
  • ✕Guest information — room numbers, screens, luggage tags
  • ✕Audio without asking, as its own question

A photo of someone’s name badge can cost them a job if we accidentally catch the wrong thing.

Guests aren’t a captive audience either

  • —Never interrupt a transaction. Not at the desk, not at the bar, not mid-check-in.
  • —Approach only people visibly waiting or at rest. Same script.
  • —You’re not their complaint line. Don’t promise anything gets fixed.
  • —Never follow anyone. Not to an elevator, not down a corridor.

Extraction

You are taking people’s time and their knowledge of their own workplace, and converting it into a grade and a portfolio piece.

That’s not automatically wrong — it’s how most research works.

So: be worth talking to.

When something goes wrong

  • —Someone’s upset: stop, apologize once, don’t try to explain, apologize and leave. Tell me please.
  • —Someone discloses something serious: listen, take no notes, don’t repeat it, don’t put it in fieldnotes. Tell me. Should not be course material.
  • —A hotel person stops you: be polite, say what class, come find me or the hilton people we work with (Dionne, Rodney, Rachel).

Four methods

  • 01Participant observation. The first twenty minutes are worthless. Sit through it.
  • 02AEIOU. The scaffold for when you’re standing there with no idea what to write.
  • 03Traces. Where designers beat anthropologists.
  • 04Semi-structured interviews. Not a survey. Not a chat.

You are looking for the ordinary, not the remarkable. The weird thing happened once isn't necessarily design-able. The pattern that happens two hundred times is more likely where the design problem lives.

AEIOU · Doblin, 1991
  • AActivities — what are people doing? Goal-directed sequences.
  • EEnvironments — light, noise, temperature, layout.
  • IInteractions — between people, between people and systems. Where’s the friction?
  • OObjects — what’s used, misused, improvised?
  • UUsers — roles, not demographics. Who has authority?

Traces

  • —Desire paths — the worn dirt line where nobody built a sidewalk
  • —Wear patterns — which handle is polished, which corner is worn
  • —Workarounds — the doorstop, the taped-over sensor, the propped fire door
  • —Hand-written signs — the richest signal in any building

Somebody designed that space, and then reality filed a complaint in marker.

The opener that always works — Spradley’s grand tour

“Walk me through
a typical shift.”

Not “what’s frustrating about your job” — that’s asking a stranger to complain to someone who might report it. This gets you sequence, and the frustrations arrive on their own.

Follow-ups
  • —“What happened right before that?”
  • —“Can you show me?”
  • —“Is that usual, or was that unusual?”
  • —Silence. Count to five.
Failure modes
  • ✕Leading questions. You just put the answer in their mouth.
  • ✕Solutioning. “What if there were an app?”
  • ✕Interviewing your idea instead of the person.
  • ✕Five whys. Two whys and gratitude.
