# Affinity maps, journey maps, service blueprints

In class I named these three and then refused to teach them. Here they are, because the refusal was about time, not about whether they matter.

All three do the same underlying thing: get what you saw out of your head and into a form where you can see all of it at once and move it around. They differ in what structure they impose once it's out there. Nothing, then time, then who does the work.

It has a history worth knowing. The affinity diagram is the **KJ method**, developed by **Jiro Kawakita** in the 1960s. Kawakita was an anthropologist, and he built it for exactly the problem you had at 3pm: a pile of field data, too much of it, no obvious order, and a need to find structure without deciding the structure in advance.

So the rule about naming clusters afterward isn't a classroom nicety. It's the entire method. The moment you write the category headings first, you've stopped doing grounded theory and started filing.

**Reach for it when:** you have a lot of unsorted observations and no idea what they add up to. It's the first move, almost always.

## Journey maps add time

A journey map takes one person and follows them through an experience from end to end, in order. Each step gets what they did, what they encountered, and usually how it felt.

The difference from an affinity map is a single axis. Affinity has no time in it — clusters sit next to each other with no claim about sequence. A journey map says *this happened, then this, then this*, and that ordering is the finding. Problems that look unrelated on a wall often turn out to be one problem that compounds across four steps.

Two things that make them go wrong. They flatten to one composite person, so decide honestly whether you're mapping someone real or an average of several, and say which on the map. And the emotional line — the little rising and falling curve under the steps — is an interpretation, not an observation. It belongs in your I column. If you can't point at the note that supports a dip, the dip is decoration.

**Reach for it when:** the thing you're studying happens over time and the order matters. Arriving somewhere. Applying for something. Any process with a beginning and an end.

## Service blueprints add everyone you didn't see

A service blueprint is a journey map with the back of the house drawn in. Above a horizontal line is what the customer sees and does. Below it — the **line of visibility** — is everything that has to happen for the front to work: staff actions out of view, systems, handoffs, the thing somebody does at 6am so the door opens at 9.

That line is the point, and it's why this one belongs in an ethnography class. **"Hidden labour" is already on your tag list.** The service blueprint is the diagram whose entire job is to make hidden labour visible and countable. Most failures you observe at the counter were caused by something below the line, and you will never find them by watching the counter harder.

The technique comes from **G. Lynn Shostack**, who published it in *Harvard Business Review* in 1984, arguing that services were being designed by accident because nobody had a way to draw one.

**Reach for it when:** the experience depends on people and systems the user never meets. Which is most institutions, including the one you visited.

## Which to reach for

| You have | Use | It gives you |
|---|---|---|
| A pile of observations, no order | Affinity map | What clusters — the themes |
| Something that happens in sequence | Journey map | Where it breaks, and what compounds |
| Front stage and back stage | Service blueprint | The causes you couldn't see |

They stack in that order. The affinity map finds the themes; the themes tell you which journey is worth mapping; the journey tells you which backstage handoff to go look at. You can't usefully start at the third one, which is the actual reason today was the first one.

## The note discipline carries over

Your O / I / Q columns don't stop mattering when you move to these.

**Observations** are what goes on an affinity card — nothing but the observation, as we did today. **Interpretations** are cluster names and the emotional line on a journey map. **Questions** are what a second visit answers, and on a blueprint they're mostly below the line, because that's the half you couldn't see from where you were standing.

A map made of interpretations looks more finished than one made of observations. It's worth less, and it's harder to argue with, which is the same problem twice.
