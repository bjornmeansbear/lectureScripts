Part two · field observations

Theories emerge
out of the data.

Glaser & Strauss, 1967 · The Discovery of Grounded Theory

Grounded theory.

Two sociologists at UC San Francisco.

They had no hypothesis to test. They were observing in and had copious notes, and they built the account out of the notes.

Normal science
  • 01Start with a hypothesis
  • 02Collect data to test it
  • 03Confirm or reject
Grounded theory
  • 01Collect
  • 02Compare
  • 03The concepts emerge from the data

You do not arrive with a hypothesis. You instead leave with a question(s). This seems to work for design too.

Grounded theory: theories are "grounded" in the observed, collected data

Constant comparison.

Every new note gets compared with your existing pile. Similarities, differences.

A concept surfaces.

It does not stop until new notes stop changing the groups. That has a name — saturation. We will probably not reach it. My hope is we all feel ourselves getting closer, which is enough to learn...

This is why nothing interpretive goes in notes.

If the conclusion is already on the note, the theory did not come out of the data. It came in with you.

Why the notes are the whole thing
  • 01The output of ethnography is a written account. Not a photo, not a video, not a feeling you had in a lobby.
  • 02In grounded theory, the notes are the data. Constant comparison means comparing notes against notes.
  • 03A concept can only emerge if the notes are specific enough to be compared. Two vague notes look identical.
  • 04After our visits we will synthesize what the class collected. That work is only as good as the notes.
Three ways ethnographers get data · Angrosino

Observe

Perceiving what people do and how they relate, in the setting itself.

Interview

Directing a conversation to get information.

Archive

The material already lying around. Floor plans, printed signage, their own briefing deck, the menu, the schedule on the wall — this can also mean deep research.

People tend to ignore the “archive.” It is often just there, it needs nobody’s consent, and it is sitting on every surface of that building.

Two different documents · Emerson, Fretz & Shaw

Jottings

Written in the space. Fragments, times, counts, quotes, sketches.

It's hard to write prose standing in a lobby.

Fieldnotes

Written afterwards, from the jottings, while you can still see it in your head.

This is the document that lasts. Helpful to do this within 24 hours

d.school Ethnography Field Guide · the observation scaffold
What?What is the person doing? The obvious and the surprising. Objective facts only — no guessing yet.
How?How are they doing it? Does it take effort? Are they rushed, careful, stuck? Is it working for them or against them?
Why?Why this way? Here you are guessing at motivation — and you mark it as a guess. This is the column that becomes your interview questions.

Divide the sheet in three.
When you do not know what to write, start at What and move right… What, How, Why…

What a fieldnote has in it · Singleton & Straits, via Sangasubana 2011
  • 01Running description. The setting, the people, individual actions, group behaviour, points of view. Record it; do not analyse it while you are still standing there.
  • 02Forgotten episodes. Things from earlier that come back to you on site. Write them down the moment they surface.
  • 03Ideas for next time. Who to look for, where to stand, what to follow up.
  • 04Personal impressions and feelings. Your own reactions — they are the clue to what is biasing the rest of the page.
  • 05Methodological notes. What went wrong with your method. Where you could not see. What you would do differently.

Header on every page: your name · the place · the date · time in, time out.

A fieldworker, on her first weeks of dissertation notes
I wrote my fieldnotes as a running log of everything I saw. The stuff I wrote in those first few weeks ended up being almost completely unusable. Because it lacked detail. And because it lacked context.

Writing more is not the fix. Choosing what to write up is the skill.

After you leave. Three steps, in this order.
  • 01Dump. Running log of everything you saw. Fast, ugly, no editing. Ten minutes.
  • 02Circle three. The three moments most worth keeping — plus anything that does not fit the pattern. Always mark things that really do not fit.
  • 03Write those three up properly. Context, who was there, what happened, in what order, what changed.

You are not writing down everything. There are not enough hours and there never will be.

Coding — the three passes.
  • 01Open coding. One short tag per note. No categories decided in advance.
  • 02In vivo codes. Use their words, not yours. “the mornings are the worst” — not “morning workflow inefficiency.”
  • 03Axial coding. Group the tags. What clusters is the finding.
