## 1 · Open with Simon (9:00–9:20)

One slide, this sentence, nothing else on it:

> "Everyone designs who devises courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones." — Herbert Simon, *The Sciences of the Artificial*, 1969

Say roughly this:

> That's from 1969, from a political scientist and economist who won a Nobel Prize, and it predates "Design Thinking" as a brand by about forty years. It belongs to no consultancy. Nobody can sell it to you.
>
> It's also the entire syllabus. Watch.

Then unpack it live, writing on the board as you go — this is the spine you'll return to at the start of every phase all semester:

- **"existing situations"** — you have to understand a situation before you can change it. That's *research*, and it's the next seven weeks.
- **"preferred"** — preferred *by whom?* Decided by whom? This is the load-bearing word, and it's where every critique we read today lives.
- **"devises courses of action"** — you have to propose something. Describing the problem is not designing. That's *prototyping*.
- **"changing"** — a process, not an event. That's *iteration*.
- And at any real scale — a 757-room hotel is hundreds of existing situations held by hundreds of people with hundreds of different "preferred"s — no one person holds the whole picture. That's why this course is *collaborative*, and not as a nicety.

> For the rest of the semester, when you lose the thread, we're still just somewhere inside that one sentence.

**Syllabus (8 min, fast).** Don't read it. Name only: five Hilton days and they're not optional, light rail is covered, weekly posts, 70% completion-based, and the fact that most of what they make will fail and that's the design. Point them at Canvas for the rest.

## 2 · Exercise: Arrival, in miniature (9:20–9:55)

They just arrived. That's unstaged, un-repeatable material and it expires by tomorrow.

Run it exactly as written in the Wk1 plan:

1. **Recall, 8 min, silent, individual.** On paper: What did you notice walking into Dolphin today? Where did you hesitate, get confused, or guess? What wasn't clear until someone told you?
2. **Insight, 5 min, pairs.** Compare. Find one *specific* shared pattern. Write it as a sentence in this form: **"People hesitate at X because Y."**
3. **Prototype, 12 min.** Paper, tape, marker. A fast, ugly fix. Literal or not.
4. **Share, 8 min.** Pin up. One sentence each. No defending.

**What you say at the end** — this is the whole point of doing it first:

> Nobody taught you a method and you just did research, synthesis, and a prototype in half an hour. So the method isn't the hard part. What's hard is doing it about someone who isn't you, in a building you don't know, with people whose jobs depend on the answer. That's the semester.

Do **not** say "sacrificial prototype" or "strong-link." Those words arrive in Wk4 and Wk6. Today just make the thing happen, so the vocabulary has something to attach to later.

## 3 · Where it came from (9:55–10:30)

Tell it as a story with a turn in it. The turn is that **the people who invented this disowned it.**

**1962 — the Conference on Design Methods, London.** Post-war optimism. The idea that design could be systematized like operations research or engineering.

**1969 — Simon**, our sentence. Also gives us *satisficing*: in a real problem you don't optimize, you find something good enough, because the search space is too big and you're out of time. Worth saying out loud, because students think a "right answer" exists.

**1969 — Victor Papanek, *Design for the Real World*.** Opens: *"There are professions more harmful than industrial design, but only a very few of them."* Note the date — the critique is exactly as old as the field.

**1973 — Rittel & Webber, "Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning."** *Wicked problems.* Give them four properties and skip the rest:

- No definitive formulation — how you frame it *is* the solution
- No stopping rule — you stop when you run out of time or money
- Solutions aren't true or false, only better or worse
- Every wicked problem is essentially unique, and every attempt changes the problem

> A hotel that a city built to prop up a convention center, and has lost $140 million on since, is a wicked problem.

**Mid-1970s — the founders bail.** This is the beat that wakes the room up. Christopher Alexander and J. Christopher Jones, two of the movement's architects, both publicly walked away from systematic design methods. Jones objected to *"the machine language, the behaviourism, the continual attempt to fix the whole of life into a logical framework."*

> The first design-methods movement was killed by its own founders, for the exact reason Natasha Jen is going to give us later.

**1980s — the rebuild on better foundations.** Nigel Cross, "Designerly Ways of Knowing" (1982): design is its own way of knowing, not a weak science and not a weak humanities. Donald Schön, *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983): reflection-in-action, a "conversation with the situation" — you learn by doing the thing and letting it talk back.

**1992 — Richard Buchanan, "Wicked Problems in Design Thinking," *Design Issues*.** The phrase enters the literature here, in a peer-reviewed design journal, sixteen years before HBR. Say it out loud: the academic tradition names it first.

**Stanford.** Robert McKim, *Experiences in Visual Thinking* (1972); then Rolf Faste turns it into something teachable; then David Kelley carries it into practice.

**1991 — IDEO.** **1999 — the shopping cart.** **2004–05 — the d.school.** **2005 — the UK Design Council publishes the Double Diamond.** **2008 — Tim Brown, "Design Thinking," *Harvard Business Review*.**

> 2008 is the year it stops being a way of working and becomes a product with a price. HBR is not a design magazine. It's read by people who buy consulting.

Put Brown's own definition up, whole:

> "Design thinking can be described as a discipline that uses the designer's sensibility and methods to match people's needs with what is technologically feasible and what a viable business strategy can convert into customer value and market opportunity."
> — Tim Brown, *Harvard Business Review*, 2008

Then point at the end of the sentence — the slide has *customer value and market opportunity* set in pink.

> It ends in *market opportunity*. Simon's sentence ended in *preferred*.

> It ends in *market opportunity*. Not a better situation. Not a preferred one, to use Simon's word from this morning. Market opportunity. The whole argument about what happened to this field is in the first paragraph, written by the person who did it.

Beside it, the IDEO house version — the one that ended up on a thousand slides:

> "A human-centered approach to innovation that draws from the designer's toolkit to integrate the needs of people, the possibilities of technology, and the requirements for business success."

Three things in the blender. One of them isn't like the others.

**2010s** — into education, hospitals, government, nonprofits. **2017 onward** — the backlash.

**Two projects, one name.** Split this explicitly before you land the section. Most of the confusion in the room lives here.

> Two different things have the same name.
>
> The academic one asks: what kind of knowledge is design? Is it a science, is it a craft, is it its own thing entirely? Simon, Rittel, Buchanan, Cross, Schön — sixty years of people arguing about the nature of design problems. That tradition produces descriptions. It is trying to explain what designers already do.
>
> The business one asks something else: how do we make this repeatable by people who aren't designers? That tradition produces procedures. Steps, diamonds, colored sticky notes, a certificate at the end.
>
> Both are real questions. But a description of how expertise works is not a recipe for people who don't have it. The second one is the one that got sold.

Land it:

> So: a real intellectual tradition, roughly sixty years old, with a serious critique already inside it — that got packaged in the 2000s into a five-step process you can teach in a day and sell for a lot of money. Both of those things are true. Today we separate them.

## 4 · The Deep Dive, watched twice (10:40–11:10)

ABC *Nightline*, "The Deep Dive," July 1999. IDEO redesigns a shopping cart in five days. Roughly 22 minutes; you need about 12 of it.

- The Deep Dive: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M66ZU2PCIcM>
- IDEO's own process explainer, if you want the sales pitch in its own words: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GYkb6vfKMI4>

**First viewing — take it at face value (12 min).** Tell them to write down the brainstorm rules when they appear on the wall: *defer judgment, encourage wild ideas, build on the ideas of others, stay focused on the topic, one conversation at a time, be visual, go for quantity.* Those rules are genuinely good and they'll use them in Wk6.

Also flag the line — *"enlightened trial and error succeeds over the planning of the lone genius."* That's the strong-link argument in 1999, before anyone called it that.

**Second viewing — different job (8 min; drop to discussion if short).** Same footage. New instructions:

- Who is in the room? Count. Note who talks and who doesn't.
- Who is *not* in the room? Where is the person who pushes a cart every day? Where is the person who collects them from the parking lot? Where is the person who builds them?
- Five days. Whose deadline is that — the problem's, or the TV crew's?
- At the end, is there any evidence the thing works? What would that evidence look like?

> This is the most successful piece of design PR ever made, and it's not dishonest. It just shows you the fun part. You're going to spend seven weeks on the part it skips.

## 5 · The case, and the critique (11:10–11:40)

**First, the case for it (4 min).** Don't skip this. Four critics in a row with no defense is a pile-on, not a critique, and they will smell it.

> Four things it actually did, and they aren't nothing.
>
> It got people who aren't designers to talk to users at all. Before this, decisions about what to build got made in conference rooms by people who had never met anyone who would use the thing. That's a low bar. Clearing it mattered.
>
> It made prototyping cheap and normal. Build it badly and find out — that moved from art school into hospitals, banks, and city governments.
>
> It got design into the room where budgets get decided. Whatever you think of HBR, that article is why a hospital has a design team.
>
> And it's teachable. Real design judgment is tacit and takes a decade. Design thinking made a version you can hand someone in a weekend.
>
> That last one is the pro and the con in one sentence. What makes it portable is what makes it thin. You can't get twenty years of judgment into five steps. You can get *something* into five steps. How much is the argument for the rest of the semester.

**Natasha Jen, "Design Thinking is Bullsh*t," 99U, 2017** (~14 min, or show 6–7 min and discuss). <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V8gjDsW3lsY>

Her argument, so you can restate it if the video runs slow:

- It's become a **religion** — a fixed liturgy, evangelists, and no heretics
- The **Post-it** is its emblem: process made visible, and visible is not the same as rigorous
- Two things are missing: **evidence** and **expertise**
- Her question, which is the whole talk: **"Where's the criticism?"**

**The slide with Brown and Jen on it together.** Brown's definition is about integration — people, technology, business, reconciled into one answer. Jen's question is about judgment — is this any good, says who, on what evidence.

> One is a merge. The other is a crit. Design education has always been the second thing. The product version quietly dropped it.

Say she's a Pentagram partner, and say why that matters: this isn't academics sniping at industry. It's the top of commercial practice saying the commercial product is thin.

Then the two that go further than Jen, because Jen is arguing about craft and these are arguing about power:

**Natasha Iskander, "Design Thinking Is Fundamentally Conservative and Preserves the Status Quo," HBR, 2018.** The most important reading for *this* course. The argument: design thinking's empathy phase takes existing conditions as given. It brackets out politics and power. Its bias toward speed and delight favors solutions that don't disturb the arrangement. You end up making a bad situation more pleasant.

> Hold that against our site. The Hilton is owned by the City of Baltimore. Taxpayers have covered $140 million in losses. The workers struck in 2024 and won a contract. If you go in there, "empathize" with a housekeeper for eleven minutes, and come back with a nicer cart, you have done exactly what Iskander is describing. That is the failure mode of this course and I want you to be able to name it by lunch.

**Lee Vinsel, "Design Thinking Is a Boondoggle" (2017).** Blunter: it's innovation theater, universities buy it for the brochure, and the results claims are unevidenced. Useful precisely because they're in a university being taught this.

**Maggie Gram, "On Design Thinking," *n+1* #35 (2019)** — the best-written history of the whole arc if anyone wants to go deeper. Put it on the slide, don't summarize it.

**Discussion, 8 min.** Use the crit frame from the Wk1 plan, on the board all session:

- Who is the audience? What is being sold?
- What gets left out?
- Where is the research, the failure, the politics?
- How does design get packaged, and for whom?

**The synthesis — say this explicitly, don't let them leave thinking it's all garbage:**

> Every critique today lands on the *branded product*. None of them land on "find out what's actually going on before you decide what to make." Jen isn't against research, she wants more rigor. Iskander isn't against listening, she wants you to listen to power too. Vinsel isn't against design, he's against the brochure.
>
> So we're keeping the practice and throwing away the packaging. Which means from here on, nobody in this room says "we did a design thinking." You say what you actually did: I interviewed four people, here's what I got wrong the first time.

**Then the conceit — the reason the course is called what it's called (3 min).** This is the last thing you say before the strong-link slide. Slow down for it.

> You can build the conditions for a good idea. That part is real. Put people in a room who don't normally talk to each other. Go look at the thing instead of guessing about it. Make twenty bad prototypes so the twenty-first has somewhere to stand. Defer judgment for an hour. None of that is snake oil. It works, and it works reliably enough to put on a schedule.
>
> What it cannot do is supply the idea. A process amplifies what you brought into the room. Bring nothing and it amplifies nothing, and you end up with a wall covered in Post-its and no idea on it.
>
> That's Jen's real complaint, under the jokes. People started doing the steps instead of doing the work, and the steps look enough like the work that nobody notices for a while.
>
> So we're going to run the process all semester. Research, synthesis, prototyping, critique, in order, on a real building with real people in it. The process is not what will make your project good. What you notice at the Hilton will make it good. The process means you'll notice more of it, and that you can show me.

This is the payoff for §2 — they did research, synthesis, and a prototype with no method at 9:20. This is the answer to why the method still matters anyway.

## 6 · The one slide they should photograph (11:40–11:45)

> **Research is a weak-link problem. Prototyping is a strong-link problem.**
>
> Weak-link: quality depends on your *worst* input. One leading question, one interview where you weren't really listening, and everything built on top inherits the damage. Slow down. Be careful. This is where rigor lives.
>
> Strong-link: quality depends on your *best* output. Make twenty prototypes, nineteen can be bad, it costs nothing, because nobody builds on the bad ones. Speed up. Be fearless. This is where being precious kills you.
>
> Most design anxiety is rigor spent in the wrong place — being careful about an ugly sketch, and careless about an interview.

This is the throughline for the semester and the bridge into the afternoon. Don't over-explain it now. Name it, let it sit, come back to it in Wk4 and Wk6.

## 7 · Ethics briefing (11:45–12:00)

**Full script and student handout: `../EthnographicResearch/research-ethics-primer-hilton.md`.**

It sits at the end of the morning rather than the end of the day on purpose — it's a gate for site access, the morning block is the one that survives contact with reality, and nobody absorbs a briefing on power and consent at 2:50 after four hours.

Twelve minutes, delivered flat. The four sections that cannot be cut:

1. **§1 — why this site isn't neutral.** City-owned, $301M borrowed, $140M in losses, the 2024 strike. And the load-bearing line: *the Hilton agreed to host us, no individual worker agreed to anything.* Consent is not transitive.
2. **§2 — the power problem.** They read as management-adjacent. "No" is expensive for a worker and free for a student. Never approach anyone mid-task.
3. **§3 — the consent script.** Three sentences, memorized, rehearsed aloud before Aug 31.
4. **§5 — the strike is not theirs to ask about.** The likeliest well-intentioned mistake in the whole course.

Print Part B, the one-page handout, and give it out here. They carry it to every site day.
