Part one

Where it
came from

None of this was new in 1962.

People had been doing the methods for centuries. What arrives in the twentieth century is the naming, the codifying, and eventually the selling.

Before it had a name
1452–1519Leonardo da Vinci. Roughly seven thousand surviving notebook pages. He drew to think, not to record — same object over and over, annotated and revised. He built working models. He dissected bodies himself instead of trusting the textbooks. Research, iteration, prototyping.
1919–1933The Bauhaus. The Vorkurs — the preliminary course under Johannes Itten, then László Moholy-Nagy, then Josef Albers — taught by “learning by doing”: paper folding, collage, model building. Discover what a material does by manipulating it. Every workshop had two masters, one craftsman and one artist. That is prototyping as a curriculum. The Vkhutemas school in Moscow, 1920–1930, was doing a parallel version.
The line you can actually trace
1927Max Bill enrolls at the Bauhaus in Dessau.
1953Bill co-founds the Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm — the Ulm School of Design. It adds cybernetics, operations research, ergonomics, and semiotics to a Bauhaus foundation.
1958Horst Rittel becomes Professor of Design Methodology at Ulm. That is the job title, in 1958.
1963Berkeley recruits Rittel. He founds the Design Methods Group there in 1967.
1973Rittel and Webber publish wicked problems.
1962 — 1973
1962Conference on Design Methods, London. Organized in part by John Christopher Jones, a Welsh ergonomist at Associated Electrical Industries, Manchester. Leads to the Design Research Society, 1966.
1964Christopher Alexander (1936–2022), Berkeley. Notes on the Synthesis of Form, Harvard University Press. Breaking a design problem into parts and solving it mathematically.
1969Herbert Simon (1916–2001), Carnegie Mellon. The Sciences of the Artificial, MIT Press. Also gives us satisficing.
1970 — 1973
1970John Christopher Jones (1927–2022). Design Methods: Seeds of Human Futures, Wiley. The definitive catalogue of the movement. Same year: first Professor of Design at the Open University.
1971Victor Papanek (1923–1998). Design for the Real World, Pantheon. “There are professions more harmful than industrial design, but only a very few of them.”
1973Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber, Berkeley. “Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning,” Policy Sciences 4. Wicked problems.

Wicked problems

  • 01No definitive formulation. How you frame it is the solution.
  • 02No stopping rule. You stop when you run out of time or money.
  • 03Not true or false. Only better or worse.
  • 04Every one is unique, and every attempt changes the problem.
1971 — Christopher Alexander, new preface to Notes on the Synthesis of Form
I reject the whole idea of design methods as a subject of study, since I think it is absurd to separate the study of designing from the practice of design.

Seven years after the founding text, its author repudiates the field it started — in the preface to that same book.

The founders quit
1971Alexander repudiates design methods. Goes on to A Pattern Language (1977) and The Timeless Way of Building (1979).
1970sJones turns away from systematic method toward chance and improvisation, reading John Cage. Chairs the Design Research Society 1971–73. Designing Designing, 1991.

Their objection — method substituting for judgment — is the same one Natasha Jen makes in 2017.

1982 — 1992 · the rebuild
1982Nigel Cross, Open University. “Designerly Ways of Knowing,” Design Studies 3:4. Design is its own form of knowledge.
1983Donald Schön (1930–1997), MIT. The Reflective Practitioner, Basic Books. Reflection-in-action — a conversation with the situation.
1992Richard Buchanan, Carnegie Mellon. “Wicked Problems in Design Thinking,” Design Issues 8:2. The phrase enters the literature here — sixteen years before HBR.
1972 — 2009 · the other track
1972Robert McKim, Stanford. Experiences in Visual Thinking. Rolf Faste makes it teachable; David Kelley carries it into practice.
1991IDEO founded, Palo Alto — David Kelley Design + Bill Moggridge’s ID Two + Mike Nuttall’s Matrix Product Design.
1999ABC Nightline, “The Deep Dive,” 13 July. A shopping cart in five days, on camera.
2005The d.school opens at Stanford. UK Design Council publishes the Double Diamond.
2008Tim Brown, IDEO CEO. “Design Thinking,” Harvard Business Review, June. Change by Design follows in 2009.

Design thinking is fundamentally conservative and preserves the status quo

Empathy takes existing conditions as given. It brackets out politics and power. Speed and delight favor solutions that don’t disturb the arrangement.

You end up making a bad situation more pleasant.

Why this matters
right now

  • 01It’s the working language of the field. You’ll be interviewed by people who use these words.
  • 02October 2023: IDEO cut about a third of its staff and closed Munich and Tokyo. Half its headcount gone since 2020. You’re inheriting the aftermath, not the hype.
  • 03Generating forty options fast used to be the expensive part. A machine does that in a minute now.

What’s left is knowing which problem is worth solving, and judging whether an answer is any good. Evidence and expertise — exactly what Jen said was missing.

You can build the conditions
for a good idea.

Put people in a room who don’t normally talk. Go look instead of guessing. Make twenty bad prototypes so the twenty-first has somewhere to stand.

What Design-thinking cannot do is supply the idea.

A process amplifies what you brought into the room. Bring nothing and it amplifies nothing — a wall covered in Post-its and no idea on it.

The process is not what will make your project good. What you notice will make it good.

Research

weak-link

Quality depends on your worst input. One leading question and everything built on top inherits the damage.

Slow down. Rigor lives here.

 

Prototyping

strong-link

Quality depends on your best output. Nineteen of twenty can be bad — nobody builds on the bad ones.

Speed up. Being precious kills you.

Most design anxiety is rigor spent in the wrong place — careful about an ugly sketch, careless about an interview.

Part three · nobody enters the site without this

Ethics

Research ethics, and what we owe the people we study

This is not a neutral site

  • —Owned by the City of Baltimore. $301M borrowed to build it.
  • —$140M in losses covered by taxpayers.
  • —The workers struck in 2024 and won a contract.

The Hilton agreed to host us. No individual worker agreed to anything.

Consent is not transitive.

The power problem

You came in through management. You read as management-adjacent, whatever you say.

“No” is free for you and expensive for them.

  • —Never approach anyone mid-task.
  • —One “no” ends it. No rephrasing, no trying their coworker instead.
Memorize this. Three sentences.
Hi — I’m a design student at MICA. We’re doing a class project about how this building works, and I’d love to hear how your job actually goes if you have a few minutes.

Nothing you say goes to management with your name on it, and you can stop any time or tell me to go away.

Is now okay, or is there a better time?

Who you are and what you want · what happens to their words and that they can stop · control of the timing. If you can’t say it without reading it, you’re not ready to talk to anybody.

Record

  • ✓Spaces, objects, wear, traces, hand-written signs
  • ✓Your own drawings, always
  • ✓Quotes attributed by role — “a front desk agent said…”
  • ✓Counts, durations, sequences

Never

  • ✕Faces. Not in the background, not “they’re blurry”
  • ✕Name badges or anything with a name on it
  • ✕Guest information — room numbers, screens, luggage tags
  • ✕Audio without asking, as its own question

A photo you didn’t take costs you nothing. A photo of someone’s name badge on a class blog can cost them a job.

Not yours to ask about

Do not ask anyone about the strike, the union, or their contract.

Not because it isn’t interesting — it’s the most interesting thing about this building. Because a worker discussing labor relations with a stranger who came in through management is being asked to take a risk for your class project.

If someone raises it themselves: listen, don’t push, don’t take notes on who said what. You research this through public records and reporting — not by putting a housekeeper on the spot in a hallway.

Guests aren’t a captive audience either

  • —Never interrupt a transaction. Not at the desk, not at the bar, not mid-check-in.
  • —Approach only people visibly waiting or at rest. Same script.
  • —You’re not their complaint line. Don’t promise anything gets fixed.
  • —Never follow anyone. Not to an elevator, not down a corridor.

Extraction

You are taking people’s time and their knowledge of their own workplace, and converting it into a grade and a portfolio piece. They get nothing automatically.

That’s not automatically wrong — it’s how most research works. But it is something you owe against, and the debt is real.

So: be worth talking to.

The standard for the whole semester

Would the person who gave you twenty minutes in September look at your final project in December and think their time was well spent?

If the answer is no, it doesn’t matter how good the object is.

When something goes wrong

  • —Someone’s upset: stop, apologize once, don’t explain, leave. Tell me that day.
  • —Someone discloses something serious: listen, take no notes, don’t repeat it, don’t put it in fieldnotes. Tell me. Not course material.
  • —A manager stops you: be polite, say what class, come find me. Don’t negotiate.
  • —Asked to leave: leave immediately. No clarifying questions.
  • —You made a mistake: tell me. Reporting it costs you nothing. Hiding it doesn’t.
Part four · afternoon

Looking,
writing it down,
making sense of it

ethnos + graphein

A people, and writing. Ethnography literally means writing people down.

The output of the method is a written account. Not a photo, not a video, not a feeling you had in a lobby. That’s why we spend the back half of this afternoon on how to write.

Malinowski · Argonauts of the Western Pacific · 1922

Stay long enough that people forget to perform for you.

Stop reading other people’s reports. Go live there. Learn the language. He was there for years.

The American line
1858–1942Franz Boas, Columbia. Cultural relativism — you understand a practice in its own terms, not by ranking it against your own. Design ethnography runs entirely on this idea and almost never credits it.
1891–1960Zora Neale Hurston studied under Boas at Barnard. Mules and Men, 1935 — folklore collected in Florida and Louisiana, in her own community. She was an insider, and she wrote about what that changed.

Boas trained Margaret Mead and Ruth Benedict too. Most of what you think of as anthropology comes out of one classroom.

Where the method comes from, honestly

Anthropology grew up in the same decades as European empire.

A great deal of early fieldwork happened in colonized territories, sometimes with colonial administrations as patrons or as the audience for the report. Studying people who cannot refuse to be studied was, for a long time, the normal condition of the discipline.

It has spent a century reckoning with that. You’re inheriting the method and the reckoning at the same time.

Emic

The insider’s categories. How the people there carve up their own world.

“The mornings are the worst.”

Etic

The outsider’s categories. The ones you brought with you.

“Morning workflow inefficiency.”

Kenneth Pike coined these in 1954, from phonemic and phonetic. You need both. The failure is using yours and believing they’re theirs.

Geertz, “Thick Description,” 1973

Two boys contract the eyelid of their right eye.

One is an involuntary twitch. The other is a wink — a conspiracy, a signal.

Photograph both. The photographs are identical.

Thin

She paused.

Thick

She paused because the system takes four seconds to load, and she’s learned to fill the gap with eye contact so the guest doesn’t feel ignored — unpaid design work covering for bad software, two hundred times a shift.

You don’t get there by watching harder. You get there by asking her.

Be honest about the theft

An anthropologist spends eighteen months. You get five days across a semester.

Design took anthropology’s methods and left behind the timescale, the language learning, the reflexivity, and most of the ethics apparatus.

Knowing you’re doing a compressed, partial version makes you careful. Believing you’ve “done ethnography” because you filled a wall with Post-its makes you dangerous.

1986 — Writing Culture, eds. James Clifford and George Marcus

You are in the account whether you admit it or not.

The reflexive turn. Ethnographic writing is constructed, partial, and rhetorical — somebody chose what to include, in what order, in whose words.

You are not a camera. Write down who you were in the room.

How it got into design
1984Lucy Suchman finishes a PhD in social and cultural anthropology at Berkeley and is working at Xerox PARC.
1987Plans and Situated Actions, Cambridge University Press. She videotaped people trying and failing to use a photocopier. Generally called the first corporate ethnography.
1987Her argument: people don’t follow plans. They improvise from whatever is in front of them, moment to moment. The machine assumed otherwise, so it was unusable.
1991Doblin develops AEIOU. 1998: Beyer and Holtzblatt publish Contextual Design.

Four methods

  • 01Participant observation. The first twenty minutes are worthless. Sit through it.
  • 02AEIOU. The scaffold for when you’re standing there with no idea what to write.
  • 03Traces. Where designers beat anthropologists.
  • 04Semi-structured interviews. Not a survey. Not a chat.

You are looking for the ordinary, not the remarkable. The weird thing happened once. The pattern that happens two hundred times is where the design problem lives.

AEIOU · Doblin, 1991
  • Activities — what are people doing? Goal-directed sequences.
  • Environments — light, noise, temperature, layout.
  • Interactions — between people, between people and systems. Where’s the friction?
  • Objects — what’s used, misused, improvised?
  • Users — roles, not demographics. Who has authority?

Traces

  • —Desire paths — the worn dirt line where nobody built a sidewalk
  • —Wear patterns — which handle is polished, which corner is worn
  • —Workarounds — the doorstop, the taped-over sensor, the propped fire door
  • —Hand-written signs — the richest signal in any building

Somebody designed that space, and then reality filed a complaint in marker.

The opener that always works — Spradley’s grand tour

“Walk me through
a typical shift.”

Not “what’s frustrating about your job” — that’s asking a stranger to complain to someone who might report it. This gets you sequence, and the frustrations arrive on their own.

Follow-ups
  • —“What happened right before that?”
  • —“Can you show me?”
  • —“Is that usual, or was that unusual?”
  • —Silence. Count to five.
Failure modes
  • ✕Leading questions. You just put the answer in their mouth.
  • ✕Solutioning. “What if there were an app?”
  • ✕Interviewing your idea instead of the person.
  • ✕Five whys. Two whys and gratitude.
Part five

You cannot
code a vibe.

Jottings

In the space. Fragments, quotes, times, sketches. Nobody writes prose standing in a lobby.

Fieldnotes

Afterward. Expanding the jottings while you can still see it in your head.

Full fieldnotes within twenty-four hours.

Memory for this collapses overnight. You keep the gist and lose every specific, and the specifics are the entire value. We write notes on the light rail home.

“Yeah the mornings are the worst, nobody tells us anything”
Commentary Is the sign the problem, or is the desk in the wrong place? Ask: does this happen all day?

“The worst” — worst how? Volume? Communication? FOLLOW UP.

What a camera would have caught, on the left. What you think it means, on the right.

Some guidelines

  • 01Don't Mix Your Thoughts Don't let interpretation get written as observation.
  • 02Verbatim quotes get quotation marks. Everything else is paraphrase. A fake quote is worse than no quote.
  • 03Time-stamp constantly. “Waited 6 seconds” is worth more than “waited a while,” and it is nearly impossible to reconstruct it later.

Also: draw. Record what’s absent — the chair nobody sits in. Write down what surprised you, and that it surprised you.

Coding · Glaser & Strauss, 1967
  • 01Open coding. Tag each observation with a short label. No categories decided in advance.
  • 02In vivo codes. Use the person’s own words. “the mornings are the worst” — not “morning workflow inefficiency.”
  • 03Axial coding. Group them. What clusters? That’s the affinity wall.
The rule for the wall

One observation per note.
No interpretation on the note itself.

If the interpretation is already written on the card, you’ve pre-decided the answer and the wall is theater.

That’s Jen’s Post-it critique, answered. Same Post-its. The difference is entirely in what’s written on them.
