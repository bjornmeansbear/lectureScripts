## 1 · Opening

**[Slide 2 — Simon quote]**

I want to start with one sentence, written in 1969.

"Everyone designs who devises courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones."

That's Herbert Simon. He was an American political scientist and economist who taught at Carnegie Mellon, and he won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1978. The sentence comes from a book called *The Sciences of the Artificial*, published by MIT Press in 1969.

I start here because this sentence predates the phrase "design thinking" as a brand by about forty years. It belongs to no consultancy. Nobody can sell it to you.

**[Slide 3 — the sentence unpacked]**

It's also the whole syllabus, so let me take it apart.

"Existing situations." You have to understand a situation before you can change it. That's research, and that's the next seven weeks of this course.

"Preferred." Preferred by whom? Decided by whom? That's the load-bearing word in the sentence, and every critique we read today lives inside it.

"Devises courses of action." You have to propose something. Describing a problem is not designing. That's prototyping.

"Changing." A process, not an event. That's iteration.

And at the scale we're working at — a hotel with seven hundred and fifty-seven rooms, hundreds of people, hundreds of different ideas about what "preferred" means — no one person holds the whole picture. That's why this course is collaborative, and not as a nicety.

For the rest of the semester, when you lose the thread, we are still somewhere inside that one sentence.

## 2 · What design thinking actually is

**[Slide — the five modes]**

Before I tell you where it came from or what's wrong with it, I should tell you what it is, because most of you have heard the phrase and never seen it written down.

The most common version comes from the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford, which everyone calls the d.school. It was founded in 2005. Their version has five modes.

Empathize. Talk to people and watch them. Find out what's actually happening.

Define. Turn what you found into a specific problem statement. Not "the lobby is bad." Something you could actually act on.

Ideate. Generate a lot of possible responses. Quantity first.

Prototype. Build rough versions, fast and cheap.

Test. Put them in front of real people and find out you were wrong.

They are not steps in a line, even though every diagram draws them that way. You go backwards constantly. Testing sends you back to research. That's the honest version and almost nobody teaches it that way.

**[Slide — the Double Diamond]**

The other model worth knowing is the Double Diamond. The UK Design Council published it in 2005, and I think it teaches something the five modes don't.

It's two diamonds side by side. Four phases: Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver.

Each diamond is a widening and then a narrowing. You go broad, then you come back in. And you do it twice.

The first diamond is about finding the right problem. The second is about finding the right answer to that problem.

The reason I like it: it says out loud that you will spend the first half of any project not knowing what the problem is, and that this is correct rather than a sign you're behind.

**[Slide — the methods]**

Underneath the phases are actual methods. These are what you'll be doing this semester.

For research: interviews, observation, shadowing someone through their day, and reading physical traces in a building. We'll cover all of those this afternoon.

For synthesis: affinity mapping, journey maps, and framing questions that begin "how might we."

For ideation: structured brainstorming, fast sketching, deliberately looking at unrelated fields for analogies.

For prototyping: paper, cardboard, role-play, and what's called Wizard of Oz prototyping, where a person pretends to be the system so you can test the idea before building anything.

For testing: put it in front of someone, shut up, and watch.

None of that is exotic. Most of it is a hundred years old and borrowed from somewhere else. Which brings me to where it came from.

## 3 · Where it came from

**[Slide — none of this was new]**

Before the chronology, one thing I want to get out of the way, because otherwise the whole story sounds like design was invented by consultants in the 1990s.

None of these methods were new in 1962. People had been doing them for centuries. What arrives in the twentieth century is the naming, the codifying, and eventually the selling.

**[Slide — before it had a name]**

Leonardo da Vinci, 1452 to 1519. About seven thousand pages of his notebooks survive. If you look at them, he isn't recording finished ideas — he's drawing the same object over and over, annotating it, revising it. He's thinking on paper. He built working models to test whether things would function. And he dissected bodies himself rather than trusting the anatomical textbooks, which were more than a thousand years old and wrong.

Research. Iteration. Prototyping. He didn't call it design thinking. He just did it.

Then the Bauhaus, 1919 to 1933. The preliminary course — the *Vorkurs* — was taught by Johannes Itten, then László Moholy-Nagy, then Josef Albers. The principle was learning by doing. Paper folding, collage, model building. You find out what a material can do by manipulating it, not by being told. And every workshop was run by two masters, a craftsman and an artist, so that making and thinking were taught together.

That is prototyping as a curriculum, thirty years before anyone wrote it down as a method. And the Bauhaus wasn't alone — the Vkhutemas school in Moscow was running a parallel version from 1920 to 1930.

**[Slide — the line you can trace]**

Here's the part that isn't just a resemblance. There's an actual line.

Max Bill enrolls at the Bauhaus in Dessau in 1927.

In 1953, Bill co-founds the Hochschule für Gestaltung in Ulm — the Ulm School of Design. Ulm takes the Bauhaus foundation and adds cybernetics, operations research, ergonomics, and semiotics.

In 1958, a man named Horst Rittel becomes Professor of Design Methodology at Ulm. That is his actual job title, in 1958.

In 1963, Berkeley recruits him. He founds the Design Methods Group there in 1967.

And in 1973, Rittel and Melvin Webber publish the paper that gives us wicked problems.

So the thread runs from the Bauhaus, through Ulm, through one person, to Berkeley, to the idea that half this field is still built on. It isn't a metaphor. You can follow the employment records.

**[Slide — timeline, 1962–1973]**

Now the chronology. Six decades, and there's a turn in the middle of it.

**1962. The Conference on Design Methods, London.** This is the usual starting point. Post-war optimism, and the idea that design could be systematized the way engineering and operations research had been. One of the organizers was a Welsh designer named John Christopher Jones. He'd been working as an ergonomist at Associated Electrical Industries in Manchester. That conference led to the founding of the Design Research Society in 1966.

**1964. Christopher Alexander publishes *Notes on the Synthesis of Form*,** Harvard University Press. Alexander was born in Vienna in 1936, raised in England, and taught architecture at the University of California, Berkeley for most of his career. The book came out of his doctoral thesis. It's the most rigorous attempt anyone made to break a design problem into parts mathematically and solve it systematically. For a while it was the most influential design book in the world.

**1969. Herbert Simon, *The Sciences of the Artificial*.** Our sentence. Simon also gives us a word worth keeping: satisficing. In a real problem you don't optimize. You find something good enough, because the search space is too large and you're out of time. I say that out loud because students arrive believing a right answer exists.

**1970. John Christopher Jones publishes *Design Methods: Seeds of Human Futures*,** John Wiley. It's a catalogue of dozens of design methods, and it's the definitive text of the movement. That same year he became the first Professor of Design at the Open University.

**1971. Victor Papanek, *Design for the Real World*,** Pantheon. It opens: "There are professions more harmful than industrial design, but only a very few of them." Papanek was Austrian-American, and he was arguing that designers were spending their talent on waste and vanity while real needs went unaddressed. Note the date. The critique of this field is exactly as old as the field.

**1973. Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber publish "Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning"** in the journal *Policy Sciences*. Rittel was a German design theorist, Webber an American urban planner, both at Berkeley. This is where we get the term wicked problems.

**[Slide — wicked problems]**

Four properties, and you should hold onto these all semester.

One. No definitive formulation. How you frame the problem is already part of the solution.

Two. No stopping rule. You don't finish. You run out of time or money.

Three. Solutions aren't true or false. Only better or worse.

Four. Every wicked problem is essentially unique, and every attempt to solve it changes it.

A hotel that a city built to prop up a convention center, and has lost a hundred and forty million dollars on since, is a wicked problem. There is no back of the book.

## 4 · The founders quit

**[Slide — 1971, Alexander]**

Here's the turn.

In 1971, seven years after publishing the book that made his name, Christopher Alexander wrote a new preface for the paperback edition of *Notes on the Synthesis of Form*. An entire academic field had grown up around his work, and people were calling him one of the leaders of it.

He wrote this:

"I reject the whole idea of design methods as a subject of study, since I think it is absurd to separate the study of designing from the practice of design."

That's the author of the founding text, publicly walking away from the field he founded, in the preface of that same text.

He didn't stop working. He went on to write *A Pattern Language* in 1977 and *The Timeless Way of Building* in 1979, and those books are arguably more systematic than the first one. But the system changed shape. It stopped being a method for processing a problem and became a vocabulary of things that had been observed to work. *A Pattern Language* went on to influence software engineering enormously — design patterns and the original wiki both come out of it.

**[Slide — Jones]**

John Christopher Jones moved in the same direction. Through the 1970s he turned away from systematic method toward chance, openness, and improvisation — he was reading John Cage. He kept writing about design for the rest of his life. His later book *Designing Designing* came out in 1991. He chaired the Design Research Society from 1971 to 1973 and died in 2022, at ninety-four. Alexander died the same year.

So: the two people most responsible for the idea that design could be made systematic both publicly backed away from it within about a decade of proposing it.

Hold onto that. Their objection — that method was being substituted for judgment, and that you cannot study designing separately from doing it — is the same objection Natasha Jen is going to make in about an hour, in 2017.

## 5 · The rebuild

**[Slide — 1982–1992]**

The field didn't die. It got rebuilt on better foundations.

**1982. Nigel Cross publishes "Designerly Ways of Knowing"** in the journal *Design Studies*. Cross is a British design researcher at the Open University. His argument is that design is its own form of knowledge. Not a weak science, not a weak humanities. Its own thing, with its own way of working.

**1983. Donald Schön publishes *The Reflective Practitioner*,** Basic Books. Schön was an American philosopher who taught at MIT. He describes what he calls reflection-in-action — you learn by doing the thing and letting the situation talk back to you. He calls it a conversation with the situation. That's a much better description of how design actually works than any five-step diagram.

**1992. Richard Buchanan publishes "Wicked Problems in Design Thinking"** in the journal *Design Issues*, volume 8, number 2. Buchanan is an American design scholar, at Carnegie Mellon and later Case Western.

I want you to notice that title and that date. The phrase "design thinking" enters the literature here, in a peer-reviewed design journal, in 1992. Sixteen years before the *Harvard Business Review* article that most people think invented it.

## 6 · Then it gets a price tag

**[Slide — 1991–2009]**

Meanwhile, a second track.

**1972. Robert McKim publishes *Experiences in Visual Thinking*** at Stanford. McKim ran the product design program there. Rolf Faste took that work and made it teachable. David Kelley, who taught alongside them, carried it into practice.

**1991. IDEO is founded** in Palo Alto, from a merger of three firms: David Kelley Design, Bill Moggridge's ID Two, and Mike Nuttall's Matrix Product Design.

**1999. ABC's *Nightline* airs "The Deep Dive"** on the thirteenth of July. IDEO redesigns a shopping cart in five days, on camera. We're going to watch it twice.

**2005. The d.school opens at Stanford.** The same year, the UK Design Council publishes the Double Diamond.

**2008. Tim Brown publishes "Design Thinking" in the *Harvard Business Review*,** June issue. Brown was IDEO's chief executive. He follows it in 2009 with a book, *Change by Design*.

I'd argue 2008 is the year this stops being a way of working and becomes a product with a price. The *Harvard Business Review* is not a design magazine. It's read by people who buy consulting.

**[Slide — Brown's definition]**

Here's Brown's definition, from that article. I'm going to read the whole thing, because the shape of the sentence matters.

"Design thinking can be described as a discipline that uses the designer's sensibility and methods to match people's needs with what is technologically feasible and what a viable business strategy can convert into customer value and market opportunity."

Look at where that sentence ends. It ends in market opportunity. Simon's sentence ended in preferred.

Both are definitions of design. They point in different directions, and the difference is the whole argument of today.

**[Slide — two projects, one name]**

Which gets me to the thing I most want you to leave with this morning.

There are two different projects with the same name.

The academic one asks: what kind of knowledge is design? Is it a science, is it a craft, is it something else? That's Simon, Rittel, Buchanan, Cross, Schön. Sixty years of argument about the nature of design problems. That tradition produces descriptions. It's trying to explain what designers already do.

The business one asks a different question: how do we make this repeatable by people who aren't designers? That tradition produces procedures. Steps, diamonds, colored sticky notes, a certificate at the end.

Both are real questions. But a description of how expertise works is not a recipe for people who don't have it. And the second one is the one that got sold.

## 7 · The Deep Dive

**[Deep Dive, first viewing]**

We're going to watch about twelve minutes of a television program from 1999. ABC's
*Nightline* went into IDEO and filmed them redesigning a shopping cart in five days. It
aired on the thirteenth of July, 1999, and it is probably the single most effective piece
of design publicity ever made.

Watch it straight the first time. Two things to write down.

One: the brainstorming rules. They put them up on the wall and they're good rules, and
you'll be using them in a few weeks. Defer judgment. Encourage wild ideas. Build on the
ideas of others. Stay focused on the topic. One conversation at a time. Be visual. Go for
quantity.

Two: there's a line in it — "enlightened trial and error succeeds over the planning of the
lone genius." Hold onto that. It's an argument I'm going to come back to at the end of the
morning.

**[Deep Dive, second viewing]**

Now we're going to watch some of the same footage again, with different instructions.

Who is in the room? Count them. Notice who talks and who doesn't.

Who is *not* in the room? Where is the person who pushes a cart around a store every day?
Where's the person who collects them out of the parking lot in the rain? Where's the
person who has to manufacture the thing?

Five days. Whose deadline is that? Is that the problem's deadline, or the television
crew's?

And at the end of it, is there any evidence the cart actually works? What would that
evidence even look like?

I want to be fair here. This film is not dishonest. Everything in it happened. It just
shows you the fun part — the part with the sticky notes and the fast talking — and skips
the part you're going to spend seven weeks on.

## 8 · The case for it

**[Slide — the case]**

Before the critique, I want to give this its due, because four critics in a row with no defense is a pile-on and you'll smell it.

Four things design thinking actually did.

It got people who aren't designers to talk to users at all. Before this, decisions about what to build were made in conference rooms by people who had never met anyone who would use the thing. That is a low bar and clearing it mattered.

It made prototyping cheap and normal. Build it badly and find out — that idea moved out of art school and into hospitals, banks, and city governments.

It got design into the room where budgets are decided. Whatever you think of the *Harvard Business Review*, that article is the reason a hospital has a design team.

And it's teachable. Real design judgment is tacit and takes about a decade to build. Design thinking made a version you can hand to somebody in a weekend.

That last one is the argument for it and the argument against it in the same sentence. What makes it portable is what makes it thin. You can't get twenty years of judgment into five steps. You can get something into five steps. How much is what we're arguing about for the rest of the semester.

## 9 · The critique

**[Slide — Jen]**

In 2017, Natasha Jen gave a talk at the 99U Conference called "Design Thinking is Bullshit." Jen is a partner at Pentagram, which is the most established design firm in the world. This is not an academic sniping at industry. This is the top of commercial practice saying the commercial product is thin.

Her argument, briefly.

It's become a religion. There's a fixed liturgy, there are evangelists, and there are no heretics.

The Post-it note has become its emblem, and she means that as an insult. Process made visible is not the same as process made rigorous.

Two things are missing: evidence and expertise.

And her question, which is the whole talk — where's the criticism?

That's a designer's word. Critique. Somebody looks at the work and says whether it's any good and why. Jen's point is that design education has always had that at its center, and the packaged version quietly dropped it.

Notice this is the same objection Alexander made in 1971 and Jones made through the 1970s. Method substituting for judgment. Forty-six years apart, same complaint.

**[Slide — Iskander]**

Two people go further than Jen, because Jen is arguing about craft and these two are arguing about power.

In September 2018, Natasha Iskander published an article in the *Harvard Business Review* titled "Design Thinking Is Fundamentally Conservative and Preserves the Status Quo." Iskander is a professor of urban planning and public service at New York University. This is the most important reading for this particular course.

Her argument is that the empathy phase takes existing conditions as given. It brackets out politics and power. And its bias toward speed and delight favors solutions that don't disturb the arrangement. You end up making a bad situation more pleasant.

**[Slide — hold it against our site]**

Hold that against where we're going.

The Hilton Baltimore Inner Harbor is owned by the City of Baltimore. The city borrowed three hundred and one million dollars to build it. Taxpayers have covered about a hundred and forty million in losses. The workers there struck in 2024 and won a contract.

If you walk into that building, empathize with a housekeeper for eleven minutes, and come back with a nicer cart, you have done exactly what Iskander describes.

That is the failure mode of this course. I want you to be able to name it by lunch.

**[Slide — the crit frame]**

Two more, quickly.

Lee Vinsel, a historian of technology at Virginia Tech, published "Design Thinking Is a Boondoggle" in 2017. He's blunter than the others: it's innovation theater, universities buy it for the brochure, and the claims about results aren't backed by evidence. That's worth hearing precisely because we are in a university, being taught this.

And Maggie Gram published "On Design Thinking" in *n+1*, issue thirty-five, in 2019. It's the best-written history of this whole arc if you want to go deeper.

These four questions stay on the board all session.

Who is the audience, and what is being sold?

What gets left out?

Where is the research, the failure, the politics?

How does design get packaged, and for whom?

## 10 · Why this matters right now

**[Slide — why now]**

You could reasonably ask why we're spending a morning on an argument that peaked around 2017.

Three reasons.

First, it's the working language of the field you're about to enter. You will be interviewed by people who use these words. You need to speak it fluently whether or not you believe it.

Second, the business that sold it has contracted sharply. In October 2023, IDEO's chief executive announced cuts of a hundred and twenty-five jobs, about a quarter of the company. Counting earlier cuts that year, they lost roughly a third of their staff in 2023, and closed their Munich and Tokyo offices. Since 2020 the firm has lost about half its headcount. *Fast Company* covered it under the headline that the era of design thinking was ending. You are not inheriting the hype. You're inheriting what came after it.

Third, and this is the one I actually care about. Generating a lot of options quickly used to be the hard, expensive part, and it's the part design thinking was best at. That is now nearly free. A machine will give you forty concepts in a minute.

So what's left is the part that was always the weak spot in the packaged version: knowing which problem is worth solving, and being able to judge whether an answer is any good. Evidence and expertise. Which is precisely what Natasha Jen said was missing in 2017.

The critique got sharper, not weaker. That's why we're doing this now.

## 11 · What we keep

**[Slide — you can build the conditions]**

So let me be clear about what we're keeping, because I don't want you leaving here thinking it's all garbage.

Every critique today lands on the branded product. None of them land on "find out what's actually going on before you decide what to make." Jen isn't against research; she wants more rigor. Iskander isn't against listening; she wants you to listen to power too. Vinsel isn't against design; he's against the brochure.

We keep the practice and throw away the packaging. Which means from here on, nobody in this room says "we did a design thinking." You say what you actually did: I interviewed four people, and here's what I got wrong the first time.

And here's the thing the whole semester rests on, which is why the course is called what it's called.

You can build the conditions for a good idea. That part is real. Put people in a room who don't normally talk to each other. Go look at the thing instead of guessing about it. Make twenty bad prototypes so the twenty-first has somewhere to stand. Defer judgment for an hour. None of that is snake oil. It works, and it works reliably enough to put on a schedule.

What it cannot do is supply the idea.

A process amplifies what you brought into the room. Bring nothing and it amplifies nothing, and you end up with a wall covered in Post-its and no idea on it.

That's Jen's real complaint, underneath the jokes. People started doing the steps instead of doing the work, and the steps look enough like the work that nobody notices for a while.

So we're going to run the process all semester. Research, synthesis, prototyping, critique, in order, on a real building with real people in it. The process is not what will make your project good. What you notice at the Hilton will make it good. The process means you'll notice more of it, and that you can show me.

**[Slide — weak-link / strong-link]**

One last thing, and then we break.

Research is a weak-link problem. The quality of the whole thing depends on your worst input. One leading question, one interview where you weren't really listening, and everything built on top of it inherits the damage. So slow down. Be careful. That's where rigor belongs.

Prototyping is a strong-link problem. The quality depends on your best output. Make twenty prototypes, nineteen of them can be bad, and it costs you nothing, because nobody builds on the bad ones. So speed up. Be fearless. Being precious is what kills you there.

Most design anxiety is rigor spent in the wrong place. Being careful about an ugly sketch, and careless about an interview.

Photograph that one.

## 12 · What ethnography actually is

**[Slide — ethnos + graphein]**

The word first. Ethnography comes from two Greek words: *ethnos*, a people, and *graphein*,
to write. It literally means writing people down.

That matters more than it sounds. The output of this method is a written account. Not a
photograph, not a video, not a feeling you had standing in a lobby. Which is why we spend
the back half of this afternoon on how to write things down, and why I'll be strict about it.

**[Slide — Malinowski]**

This morning was about the argument. This afternoon is about the method, and the method
comes from anthropology, so let's start there rather than with the design version.

Bronisław Malinowski published *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* in 1922. The founding
move of modern fieldwork is in that book: stop reading other people's reports about a
place, go and live there, learn the language, and stay long enough that people forget to
perform for you. He was there for years.

That last part is the part design keeps forgetting. The first twenty minutes of any
observation are worthless, because everyone can see you.

**[Slide — Boas and Hurston]**

The American line runs through one person. Franz Boas, 1858 to 1942, at Columbia.

Boas gave us cultural relativism — the idea that you understand a practice in its own
terms, rather than by ranking it against your own. That's the intellectual move this
entire afternoon depends on, and design ethnography almost never credits it.

He trained a remarkable set of students. Margaret Mead. Ruth Benedict. And Zora Neale
Hurston, who studied anthropology under him at Barnard, and whose book *Mules and Men*,
published in 1935, collected folklore in Florida and Louisiana — in her own community.

I mention Hurston specifically because she's the counter-case to Malinowski. He was an
outsider trying to become less strange to the people he studied. She was already inside,
and she wrote about what that changed — what people told her that they wouldn't have told
a stranger, and what they wouldn't tell her precisely because she wasn't a stranger.

You're going to be outsiders at the Hilton. But some of you have worked service jobs, and
that changes what you'll notice and what you'll be told. Pay attention to that.

**[Slide — where the method comes from, honestly]**

And I'm not going to introduce this method without saying where it came from.

Anthropology grew up in the same decades as European empire. A great deal of early
fieldwork happened in colonized territories, sometimes with colonial administrations as
the patron or the audience for the report. Studying people who were not in a position to
refuse was, for a long stretch, the normal condition of the discipline.

It has spent about a century reckoning with that, and the reckoning produced most of the
ethics we'll cover. You're inheriting the method and the reckoning at the same time. If
anyone ever tells you research ethics is bureaucratic overhead, that's the history it's
overhead from.

**[Slide — emic and etic]**

Two words worth having. A linguist named Kenneth Pike coined them in 1954, from *phonemic*
and *phonetic*.

Emic is the insider's categories — how the people in a place carve up their own world.

Etic is the outsider's categories — the ones you walked in with.

If a housekeeper says "the mornings are the worst," that's emic. If you write down "morning
workflow inefficiency," that's etic.

You need both. The failure mode is using yours and believing they're theirs.

**[Slide — the wink]**

The single most useful idea in this field comes from Clifford Geertz, in an essay called
"Thick Description," published in 1973.

Two boys contract the eyelid of their right eye. *(Do it.)*

In one of them it's an involuntary twitch. In the other it's a wink — to a friend, about
something, a conspiracy, a signal.

Now photograph both. The photographs are identical. A camera cannot tell them apart. Any
description that stops at "rapid contraction of the right eyelid" has recorded everything
and understood nothing.

Thin description is what happened. Thick description is what it meant, and to whom, and
how you know.

**[Slide — thin and thick, on our site]**

Here's what that looks like at the Hilton.

On the thirty-first you'll watch someone at the front desk pause for four seconds before
answering a guest.

Thin: she paused.

Thick: she paused because the system takes four seconds to load, and she has learned to
fill that gap with eye contact so the guest doesn't feel ignored — which means she is
doing unpaid design work to cover for bad software, roughly two hundred times a shift.

You cannot get to the second one by watching harder. You get there by asking her.

**[Slide — be honest about the theft]**

And I want to be honest about what we're doing.

An anthropologist spends eighteen months. You are going to spend five days, spread across
a semester. Design took anthropology's methods and left behind the timescale, the language
learning, the reflexivity, and most of the ethics apparatus.

I'm not going to pretend that's fine. What I'll say is this: knowing you're doing a
compressed, partial version makes you careful. Believing you've "done ethnography" because
you filled a wall with Post-its makes you dangerous.

That's the same argument Natasha Jen made this morning, pointed at research instead of
process.

**[Slide — Writing Culture]**

One more idea before the methods, and it's the one that changes how you take notes.

In 1986, James Clifford and George Marcus edited a collection called *Writing Culture*.
The argument, roughly: an ethnographic account is constructed. Somebody chose what to put
in, in what order, in whose words. It's partial and it's rhetorical, and the person who
wrote it is present in it whether they admit it or not.

This is called the reflexive turn, and the practical version is simple. You are not a
camera. Who you are changes what people show you and what they say. So write that down too
— write down where you were standing, what you'd just been told, what you expected to see.

**[Slide — how it got into design]**

So how does any of this end up in a design studio?

Mostly through one person. Lucy Suchman finished a PhD in social and cultural anthropology
at Berkeley in 1984, and she was working at Xerox PARC — the research lab that produced
the graphical interface, the mouse, and the laser printer.

She videotaped people trying to use a photocopier. Not novices — accomplished people, in
their own workplace, unable to make a machine do what they wanted. That study is generally
called the first corporate ethnography.

In 1987 she published *Plans and Situated Actions* with Cambridge University Press. Her
argument was that people don't execute plans. They improvise, moment to moment, from
whatever is actually in front of them. The copier had been designed on the assumption that
users follow steps in order, and that assumption is why it was unusable.

That's the whole case for this method in one example. You could not have found that by
asking people what they do. They'd have told you they follow the instructions. You had to
watch.

After that it moves fast: Doblin develops AEIOU in 1991, and Hugh Beyer and Karen
Holtzblatt publish *Contextual Design* in 1998.

## 13 · Four methods

**[Slide — four methods]**

Four. Not twenty. You can hold four.

**Participant observation.** Be in the space, on its terms, long enough to stop being
interesting. Sit through the first twenty minutes.

And the rule that matters most: you are looking for the ordinary, not the remarkable.
Students always record the weird thing. The weird thing happened once. The pattern that
happens two hundred times is where the design problem actually lives.

**[Slide — AEIOU]**

**AEIOU.** Developed at Doblin in 1991. This is the scaffold for when you're standing in a
lobby with a notebook and no idea what to write down. Five letters.

A — Activities. What are people doing? Goal-directed sequences.

E — Environments. What's the space like? Light, noise, temperature, layout.

I — Interactions. Between people, and between people and systems. Where's the friction?

O — Objects. What's present? What's being used, misused, improvised with?

U — Users. Who's here? Roles, not demographics. Who has authority?

Run the letters. It always gives you something to write.

**[Slide — traces]**

**Traces.** This is where designers beat anthropologists, because you can read made things.

Desire paths — the worn dirt line where nobody built a sidewalk.

Wear patterns — which door handle is polished, which corner of the counter is worn down.

Workarounds — the doorstop, the taped-over sensor, the propped-open fire door.

And hand-written signs, which are the richest signal in any building.

Every hand-lettered sign taped to a wall is a fossil of a failure. Somebody had a problem
often enough that they went and got a Sharpie. "PLEASE DO NOT PUT TRAYS HERE." "DOOR
STICKS — PUSH HARD." "ASK AT DESK." Somebody designed that space, and then reality filed a
complaint in marker.

Photograph every single one you see at the Hilton.

**[Slide — the grand tour question]**

**Semi-structured interviews.** Not a survey. Not a chat. A prepared set of topics, asked
in whatever order the conversation wants.

The opener that always works comes from James Spradley. It's called the grand tour
question.

"Walk me through a typical shift."

Not "what's frustrating about your job." That's asking a stranger to complain to someone
who might report it. "Walk me through a typical shift" gets you sequence — and the
frustrations arrive on their own, attached to the moment they happen in.

**[Slide — follow-ups and failure modes]**

Then the follow-ups, which are the actual craft.

"What happened right before that?"

"Can you show me?" — that's the best one in the list.

"Is that how it usually goes, or was that unusual?"

"Who else deals with this?"

And silence. Count to five. They'll keep talking. You will find this unbearable and it
works every single time.

Now the failure modes, which matter more than the techniques.

Leading questions. "Doesn't it get frustrating when the elevator's slow?" You just put the
answer in their mouth, and now you can't unhear it. Remember research is a weak-link
problem — that one question can poison what the whole team understands.

Solutioning at people. "What if there were an app?" You're not there to pitch.

Interviewing your idea instead of the person. If you already know what you want to make,
you will hear agreement everywhere.

And the five whys, used badly. It's a real technique. It is also, four whys deep, an
interrogation of somebody who needs to get back to work. Two whys and gratitude.

## 14 · How to write it down

**[Slide — jottings and fieldnotes]**

This is the part that determines whether week five works, so I'm giving it real time.

In four weeks we sit in this room and try to synthesize everything this class collected.
That session is only as good as the notes. I have watched teams lose an entire site visit
because six people wrote "front desk seemed busy" and nothing else. You cannot code a vibe.

The vocabulary comes from Emerson, Fretz and Shaw, *Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes*,
which is the standard text.

Jottings happen in the space. Fragments, quotes, times, sketches. Nobody writes prose
while standing in a lobby.

Fieldnotes are what you write afterward, expanding those jottings into full description
while you can still see it in your head.

And the twenty-four hour rule, which I mean literally. Full fieldnotes within twenty-four
hours. Not "when I get to it." Memory for this kind of detail collapses overnight — you
keep the gist and you lose every specific, and the specifics are the entire value. On
Hilton days we will write notes on the light rail home. That's not a suggestion, that's
the schedule.

**[Slide — the two-column split]**

Two columns. Observation on the left, commentary on the right.

On the left: what a camera would have caught. On the right: what you think it means, what
it reminds you of, what to ask next.

Three rules.

One. Never let the columns merge. The moment interpretation gets written down as
observation, you can't recover the original.

Two. Verbatim quotes go in quotation marks, and everything else is paraphrase. If you
didn't catch it exactly, don't quote it. A fake quote is worse than no quote.

Three. Time-stamp constantly. Duration is data. "Waited six seconds" is worth more than
"waited a while," and you cannot reconstruct it later.

Three more things. Draw — plans, sections, arrows showing how people move. You're design
students; that's your advantage over a sociologist. Record what's absent: the chair nobody
sits in, the entrance nobody uses. And write down what surprised you, and that it
surprised you. Surprise is the sound your assumptions make when they break, and it fades
within a day.

## 15 · What coding is

**[Slide — coding]**

Just enough for this afternoon to make sense. The real version is week five.

You'll come back with hundreds of fragments. Coding is how a pile of fragments becomes
something you can act on. It comes from grounded theory — Glaser and Strauss, 1967 — and
it's three passes.

Open coding. Go through your notes and tag each distinct observation with a short label
for what it's about. No categories decided in advance. The labels come from the material.

In vivo codes. Where you can, use the person's own words as the label. If a housekeeper
says "the mornings are the worst," then the code is *the mornings are the worst*. Not
"morning workflow inefficiency." Two reasons: it keeps their framing instead of
substituting yours, and it stays specific enough to argue with.

Axial coding. Group the codes. What clusters? What's the relationship between clusters?
That's the affinity wall — it comes from Jiro Kawakita's KJ method, and you'll see it
called affinity diagramming everywhere in design.

**[Slide — the rule for the wall]**

And the rule for the wall, which is not negotiable.

One observation per note. In the person's own words where you have them. No interpretation
on the note itself.

The interpretation happens when you move the notes around. If the interpretation is
already written on the card, you have pre-decided the answer and the wall is theater.

Which is Jen's Post-it critique, answered. A wall of Post-its that just says what you
already believed is exactly the ritual she was attacking. A wall of specific, verbatim,
uninterpreted observations is a tool. Same Post-its. The difference is entirely in what's
written on them.

Alright. Outside.
