## The scaling question — asked, then re-asked correctly (2026-08-26)

**First framing (wrong):** can "signs signaling on substrates" broaden into a general
definition of *all* design — products, buildings — under some larger "form-givers" umbrella?

**Kristian's correction, same day, and the framing that matters:** the goal is not a theory
of all design. It is a **medium-agnostic definition of graphic design** that covers his own
working model — *"I think of myself as a graphic designer, even if I am making a chair or
recycling plastic; it's all for signaling the meaning I want to convey, not necessarily
about the actual stuff produced."*

That inverts the answer. Recorded so the wrong version doesn't get re-derived:

1. **The chair objection was bad and is withdrawn.** A chair's form denotes chairness and
   connotes comfort, austerity, status — that is straightforwardly signaling, and it is
   exactly Krippendorff's product semantics and Barthes on the Citroën. Those were cited as
   a *risk* of over-extension when they are in fact the supporting literature. Same for
   buildings.

2. **The definition doesn't need to scale to all design, and shouldn't try.** It needs to be
   agnostic about *output* while staying specific about *intent*. It already is. Nothing in
   "signs signaling on substrates" names paper, screens, or two dimensions.

3. **The discriminator is intent, not medium.** Graphic design is not a category of objects.
   It is what you are doing when the purpose of the work is *to signal a meaning* — whatever
   the substrate turns out to be. A chair made to be sat in is product design. The same chair
   made to signal something is graphic design with a chair for a substrate. Both intentions
   can run on one object without contradiction, because they are different intentions, not
   competing classifications.

4. **Function is therefore not flattened** — the earlier objection's real failure. The
   definition never claimed a chair is *only* signs. It claims that when your intent is
   signaling, signaling is the part you are operating on. The chair still has to hold weight.
   You're just not the one claiming that as your contribution.

5. **The skeptic's objection, and its answer.** *Everything* signals — so does intent do any
   work, or does all making become graphic design? The line: every object carries semiotic
   residue; not every object is **made in order to** signal. Author's purpose, not the
   object's inevitable connotations. Worth having ready, because it's what gets raised in
   the room.

6. **This is why "form-givers" is the wrong umbrella after all** — for a reason better than
   the one first given. Form-giving names the *output* side (someone gives a thing its form).
   Kristian's model is explicitly *not about the actual stuff produced*. Form-giver is a
   definition by output, aimed at a practice defined by intent. Wrong axis entirely — and
   also, as noted before, silent on ideology.

7. **What this does for the automation argument** — it makes it much stronger, and this
   should go in the lecture. Define yourself by output and Canva takes the posters. Define
   yourself by intent and the practice is portable to any substrate, which is precisely the
   escape route the essay is looking for. The definition isn't just more accurate; it's the
   thing that makes the argument work.

8. **This is also the retroactive justification for half the workshop menu.** `3P: People
   Processing Plastic`, `Spontaneous Lamp`, the Drawdown diptychs — these stop being
   departures from graphic design and become graphic design with unusual substrates. Worth
   saying out loud in the pitch sheets, which currently don't claim it.

## Then what is product design? (2026-08-26)

Kristian's follow-up: *"would product design be when you are considering the affordances
first, not the communication aspects?"*

**Yes — and keeping that on the same axis is what makes this a real taxonomy** rather than
a boundary dispute. Both disciplines get defined by *which intent you're operating under*,
not by what the object is. Same move, applied twice. Affordance is solid ground for it:
Gibson's term, taken into design by Norman's *The Design of Everyday Things*.

**But the two are nested, not parallel, and the asymmetry is the interesting part.**

Norman added the word **"signifier"** in the 2013 revised edition precisely because
affordance alone doesn't do the job — an affordance nobody can perceive is inert. A flat
plate signifies *push*; a handle signifies *pull*. The door works because the affordance
has been **made legible**, and making it legible is signaling.

So: affordance-first design still requires a signaling layer to function. Signaling-first
design doesn't require an affordance layer in the same way. Which means **product design
contains an irreducible graphic-design layer** — the part where the affordance is made
readable.

That's the formal version of the claim: *"I think of myself as a graphic designer, even if
I am making a chair."* You're not borrowing from another discipline. You're working the
layer that discipline can't do without.

A chair, then, has three things going on, and they are stances toward one object rather
than competing classifications:

1. **Structure** — it holds weight. Engineering. Nobody's aesthetic claim.
2. **Affordance, made legible** — its form tells you where and how to sit. Norman's
   signifier. Product design's intent, doing graphic design's work.
3. **Signaled meaning** — comfort, austerity, status, ecology, whatever you're saying.
   Graphic design's intent outright.

Layer 3 is where the Substrates definition lives. Layer 2 is where it's shared.

**Know before presenting this:** Klaus Krippendorff's *The Semantic Turn: A New Foundation
for Design* (2006) argues nearly this position from the product side — "design is making
sense of things," products constituted by meaning rather than function. You have a serious
ally in the literature and you are not first. Cite him rather than getting cited at. Same
neighborhood as Blauvelt's relational design, already in the Notion bibliography.

## The overlap is the real thing (2026-08-26)

Kristian, on the door plate: *"is this really communication design or graphic design then?
like is that PART of thinking about how all designs often are a venn diagram intersection
of what think of themselves as unique, specialized design mediums… this is the overlap
between graphic design, product design, architectural design?"*

**Correction to the section above.** "Product design contains an irreducible graphic-design
layer" was a land grab. Architecture makes the identical claim about wayfinding, industrial
design makes it about product semantics, interaction design makes it about UI. Every
discipline annexes the overlap and calls it a sub-department of itself.

**The Venn framing is more honest — but it still concedes too much,** because it treats the
three disciplines as the real categories and the intersection as derivative. Your own
definition says the opposite. Drop medium from the definition and the picture inverts:

> The **intent** is the real category. The "disciplines" are historical bundles of
> *intent + medium + toolset + guild*. Graphic design got paper and screens. Product
> design got objects. Architecture got buildings. Signaling intent ran across all three
> the entire time — which is why the overlap keeps needing to be rediscovered and renamed.

**This is empirically checkable, and it checks out: the overlaps already have professional
bodies.** If the overlap were incidental, it wouldn't have institutions.

- **SEGD** — the Society for Experiential Graphic Design. The graphic ∩ architecture
  overlap, with a name, a membership, and a conference. *Already in your archive:*
  `sad2021tw/tiddlers/MM_ MM050 (020260700).tid:821`, an SEGD paper on environmental
  graphic design changing perceptions of divided communities.
- **Product semantics** (Krippendorff) — the graphic ∩ product overlap.
- **Interaction design** — graphic ∩ product ∩ computation.

Three disciplines that supposedly have distinct specialized media keep independently
founding organizations in the same intersection. That's what you'd expect if the
intersection were the actual practice and the disciplines were the accident.

**You already answered the "graphic or communication design?" half — in 2019.**

- `SAD2021.txt:331` — *"what can **communication design (meaning what can signs on
  substrates)** do to get carbon out of the air"*. You glossed communication design **as**
  signs on substrates, explicitly, in a parenthetical.
- `SAD2019.md:179` — same sentence, first version.
- `SAD2019.md:332` — *"what would make me excited to be a 'communication designer' or what
  would make me excited and proud to be 'graphic designing'?"*
- `SAD2019.md:430` — *"that's not graphic design though; that's not really communication
  design"* — using them as near-synonyms while sensing neither fits.

So: the field already attempted this fix with a rename. "Communication design" escapes the
*medium* bundle — graphic means marks, communication doesn't — but it still doesn't get you
to a chair. Yours does, because it drops medium rather than swapping it.

**The cost, which should be said out loud rather than discovered later.** Dissolving the
disciplines is intellectually clean and professionally expensive. "I'm a graphic designer"
is what gets you hired, what a department chair understands, and what a workshop pitch has
to say in its first line. The honest position and the working identity are in tension. That
tension is probably *material for the lecture* rather than a problem to solve in it — it's
the same bind the automation argument describes, arriving from the other direction.

## DECIDED 2026-08-26

**The broad definition is the 2021 line.** Confirmed by Kristian, verbatim:

> how do you translate your interests and desires and intentions into things — these
> things can be posters, websites, apps, books, zines, dances, songs, flags, chairs,
> gardens, whatever.

Not *form-giving* — form-giving is silent on ideology, and ideology is the point.
Not *problem-solving* — already rejected in the 2021 entry itself.

Structure that follows:

- **Genus — "translating intention into things."** Scales to chairs, gardens, buildings,
  dances, because it was written that way. Carries agenda/ideology at its center, which
  is what connects it to `workshop-form-content-context/` and the sustainabilitist
  material. Sits directly beside Simon's "changing existing situations into preferred
  ones."
- **Species — "signs signaling on substrates."** What the genus looks like when the
  intention being translated is *a meaning to be signaled*. **Agnostic about the output,
  specific about the intent** — the substrate can be paper, a screen, a chair, reground
  HDPE, a room, a garden. It is a subset by purpose, not by medium.

The two levels share one logic, which is why they fit: both put the intention first and
treat the produced object as downstream. The 2021 line says design is intention becoming a
thing; Substrates says graphic design is the case where the intention is to signal. Same
move, one level apart.

That structure is the spine of the "What is design?" lecture. Substrates does not get
rewritten to be broader — it was never narrow in the way it looked. It gets a floor put
under it and the medium assumption stripped out of the prose, which currently still leans
on print-and-pixels examples.
