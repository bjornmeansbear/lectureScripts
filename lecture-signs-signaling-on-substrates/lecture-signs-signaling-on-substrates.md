# Signs Signaling on Substrates.

**Redefining Graphic Design: From Visual Communication to Cultural Praxis**

Graphic design has long been understood as the art and practice of visual communication. Traditionally, it has been defined as the craft of creating visual content to convey messages, solve problems, and communicate ideas through the use of typography, imagery, and composition. This definition has served the field well for decades, guiding designers in their pursuit of creating visually appealing and effective communication materials.

However, as we stand at the cusp of a new era marked by rapid technological advancements and shifting cultural landscapes, it's becoming increasingly clear that this traditional definition falls short. It fails to capture the evolving scope of design and the rich cultural contexts in which it operates. To address this limitation, I propose a new, more comprehensive definition of graphic design: "signs signaling on substrates."

**Signs Signaling on Substrates: A New Paradigm**

This new definition encompasses the full range of design practices, from traditional print media — books, magazines, posters, packaging — to emerging digital and multisensory experiences — apps, interactive projections, multi-sensory exhibitis. Let's break down this definition to understand its components and implications:

1. Signs: At the heart of this definition lies the semiotic concept of "signs." Signs are any forms that convey meaning, ranging from words and images to sounds and textures. They can be simple and concrete or vague and abstract. In graphic design, signs are the building blocks of communication, used to create logos, icons, typography, and other design elements.
2. Signaling: This term refers to the active process of communication — the way signs are combined, arranged, and presented to convey a message, idea, or meaning. Graphic designers are skilled in the art of signaling, using composition, color, hierarchy, and other design principles to create effective and engaging communication. However, in this new context, signaling extends beyond traditional visual methods to encompass a full spectrum of sensory and experiential communication techniques. This can include tactile elements (textures, materials, interactive components), olfactory signals (scents, smells), auditory elements (sounds, music, silence), kinetic aspects (movement, gestures, performance), and even temporal dimensions (changes over time, sequences, rhythms). In this expanded view, signaling becomes a multi-dimensional practice that engages all senses and modes of human perception.
3. Substrates: Traditionally, these are the physical or digital materials on which signs are presented. From paper to pixels, substrates provide the canvas for graphic design, shaping the possibilities and constraints of communication. Extending this, a substrate could be more abstract substrates like culture, memory, consciousness, or sensory perception. The substrate could be auditory (sound waves hitting ear drums), visual (light waves striking retinas), tactile (textures felt by fingertips), or even conceptual (ideas taking root in minds). In digital spaces, the substrate might be the user's attention span or their patterns of online behavior. In environmental design, the substrate could be public space itself or the collective social consciousness of a community. These expanded notions of substrates help us think beyond traditional surfaces to consider how design signals across multiple dimensions of human experience.

This broader definition allows us to appreciate graphic design as a dynamic interplay of signs, signals, and substrates, with designers as the skilled manipulators of these elements. It reflects the evolving nature of the field and provides a more inclusive framework for understanding the full range of design practices.

**The Automation Challenge: Beyond Technical Mastery**

As we embrace this new definition, we must also confront a significant challenge facing the field: the increasing automation of design tasks. As artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies advance, many of the technical skills that have long been the domain of graphic designers are being replicated by automated systems.

This shift poses a critical question: If all we focus on is technical mastery, will designers lose their role to automated machines? The answer, quite possibly, is yes. Tools like Canva and Adobe Express are already filling gaps where many graphic design jobs used to lie, democratizing basic design skills and making them accessible to non-designers.

However, this challenge also presents an opportunity. It pushes us to reconsider the role of the designer and to focus on aspects of design that cannot be easily automated. As we move beyond post-modernism and pluralism, the skills that have served designers well in the past are no longer sufficient or uniquely valuable.

**A Holistic Approach: Integrating Semiotics, Ecology, and Cultural Studies (&more?)**

To remain relevant and impactful in this changing landscape, designers must look beyond technical skills and embrace a more holistic approach to their practice. This approach involves integrating knowledge and methodologies from diverse fields such as semiotics, ecological studies, anthropology, and cultural studies. Let's explore how these areas can inform and enrich graphic design practice:

1. Semiotics: The study of signs and symbols is fundamental. Understanding semiotics provides designers with a deeper grasp of how meaning is created and interpreted across different cultures and contexts. As Umberto Eco emphasized, the interpretation and context of signs are crucial in shaping their meaning. By mastering semiotic principles, designers create more nuanced and culturally attuned work.
2. Ecological Concerns: As our world grapples with environmental challenges, designers have a responsibility to consider the ecological impact of their work. This involves not only choosing sustainable materials and production methods but also designing communications that promote environmental awareness and sustainable behaviors. By integrating ecological thinking into their practice, designers can contribute to creating a more sustainable future.
3. Anthropological and Cultural Studies: To create truly impactful design, we must understand the cultural contexts in which our work will exist. Anthropological approaches can help designers gain insights into diverse cultural practices, beliefs, and values. This knowledge can inform the creation of designs that are not only visually appealing but also culturally relevant and respectful.

**Towards Contextual Cultural Production**

By integrating these diverse fields of study, we can transform graphic design from a purely visual discipline into a practice of contextual cultural production. This approach positions designers as cultural interpreters and innovators, capable of creating work that resonates deeply across diverse contexts and mediums.

In this new paradigm, the role of the designer expands significantly:

1. Cultural Mediator: Designers become bridges between different cultural contexts, translating ideas and messages in ways that are meaningful and relevant to diverse audiences.
2. Meaning Maker: Rather than just creating visually appealing artifacts, designers focus on crafting meaningful experiences that contribute to cultural discourse.
3. Systems Thinker: Designers consider the broader systems and contexts in which their work exists, creating designs that are adaptable and responsive to changing circumstances.
4. Ethical Practitioner: With a deeper understanding of cultural and ecological impacts, designers take on the responsibility of creating work that is not only effective but also ethically sound and socially beneficial.

**Praxis: Putting Theory into Practice**

To truly embody this new approach to graphic design, we must focus on praxis—the process of putting theory into practice. This involves several key shifts in how we approach design education and professional practice:

1. Interdisciplinary Education: Design education must expand beyond technical skills to include robust training in semiotics, cultural studies, ecology, and other relevant fields. This broader education will equip designers with the tools to navigate complex cultural landscapes.
2. Contextual Design Processes: Rather than starting with aesthetics, designers should begin their process by deeply understanding the cultural, social, and ecological contexts of their projects. This might involve ethnographic research, stakeholder interviews, or ecological impact assessments.
3. Collaborative Practice: Designers should seek out collaborations with experts from other fields—anthropologists, ecologists, sociologists—to create more holistic and impactful work.
4. Ethical Framework: Developing a strong ethical framework should be a core part of design practice, guiding decisions about what projects to take on and how to approach them.
5. Adaptive Design: Create designs that are robust enough to convey their intended meaning across various contexts, yet flexible enough to allow for personal interpretation and connection.

**Conclusion: Shaping the Future of Design**

As we redefine graphic design as "signs signaling on substrates" and embrace a more holistic, culturally attuned approach, we position the field to remain relevant and impactful in an increasingly automated world. By expanding our toolkit to more intentionally include semiotics, ecological thinking, and cultural studies, we transform designers from mere technicians into cultural interpreters and innovators.

This evolution in design thinking is not just about staying relevant; it's about making design more generous, culturally attuned, and resilient against obsolescence. It's about recognizing the power of design to shape how people see and understand the world around them.

As we train the next generation of designers, we must equip them with the skills to navigate an increasingly complex and automated world. This new approach to graphic design serves as a hedge against irrelevance and positions designers at the forefront of cultural and technological evolution.

In conclusion, by embracing this broader view of graphic design, we can create more impactful, culturally attuned work that reflects the richness and complexity of the human experience. As we move forward, let us remember that the true power of graphic design lies not just in creating aesthetically pleasing visuals, but in crafting meaning and context—in essence, shaping culture.
