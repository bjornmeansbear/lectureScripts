# Design for the Future Today — examples & references

Working notes for the lecture. Two kinds of material here:

1. **Vision-redraws-the-line examples** — the same structural move as the iPhone slide
2. **Practitioners** — speculative design / design fiction people worth citing

Already in the talk: Joseph Voros (the cone), Stuart Candy, Dunne & Raby, Julian Bleecker, Parsons & Charlesworth, Katie Patrick, Curry Hackett, Angela Oguntala, Buckminster Fuller.

---

## 1. Visions that cut the projected line

The shape: a named person or group holds a preferable future for an object that sits out in the possible or preposterous band; they marshal resources and culture toward it; once it lands, the old projected line is cut and a new one is drawn. What is *probable* afterward is permanently different.

### Douglas Engelbart — the 1968 "Mother of All Demos"

Best pairing with the iPhone. His goal was stated in 1962 as *augmenting human intellect* — a preferable future, not a product. The December 1968 demo showed the mouse, hypertext links, windows, video conferencing, and two people editing one document in real time, at a moment when computers were batch-processing machines owned by institutions. Like the iPhone keynote, the demo was partly theater — a leased microwave link and improvised hardware making something work just long enough to be seen. Everything about how we use computers descends from that room.

### Masaru Ibuka / Sony Walkman, 1979

He wanted to listen to opera on long flights. Engineers objected that a tape machine that couldn't record and had no speaker was simply broken; Sony's own sales people expected a flop. What it actually created was a *behavior* — private audio in public space — and that redrawn line runs through the iPod, AirPods, and podcast culture.

### Stewart Brand, 1966 — "Why haven't we seen a photograph of the whole Earth yet?"

Buttons and a pestering campaign aimed at NASA, for an image that did not exist, on the belief that the image itself would reorganize how people understood the planet. *Earthrise* (1968) and *Blue Marble* (1972) became the icon of the entire environmental movement. **Use this one** — it lands directly on the Fuller / Spaceship Earth section already in the talk, and the "object" is only a picture.

### Richard Stallman — GNU (1983), the GPL (1989)

Free software was treated as ideological nonsense. Note for a design audience: the designed object here is substantially *a license* — a legal artifact. Now effectively all computing infrastructure runs on it. Good counterweight to Jobs, because the mobilization wasn't capital.

### Wikipedia, 2001

"An encyclopedia anyone can edit" was preposterous enough that the serious project was Nupedia, the expert-reviewed version — which failed. Wikipedia cut off the projected future in which knowledge is made by credentialed experts and sold to you.

## The ones that complicate it — probably the most valuable slides

### Malcom McLean and the shipping container, 1956

A trucking man asking why you would unload the truck at all. Ports, unions and shipbuilders resisted. A corrugated steel box then restructured global trade, offshoring, and the shape of every port city on earth.

**The wrinkle: preferable for whom?** It gutted dockworker unions and hollowed out waterfronts. Preferable futures have losers, and the cone doesn't tell you who they are.

### Alan Kay — the Dynabook, 1972

A tablet computer for children, as a learning instrument. Cardboard mockups and a paper. Kay's line is the famous one: *the best way to predict the future is to invent it.* But Kay has said plainly that the iPad is **not** the Dynabook, because it fails the educational premise that was the entire point.

So: **the artifact arrived and the preferable future didn't.** This is the sharpest thing available to say to seniors about degree projects — the object can get built and the vision still lose.

### Buckminster Fuller as counterexample

Dymaxion house, Dymaxion car, geodesic domes. Enormous vision; the projected line mostly did *not* move. Worth one sentence, because visions fail — and that is not an argument against having one.

## 2. Reframing the iPhone slide

Stronger as a timeline than as a date claim, because it shows the vision circulating in the culture for two decades while the possible band slowly widens to meet it:

- **1987** — Apple publishes the *Knowledge Navigator* concept video for Sculley's Educom keynote: a folding touchscreen tablet, video calls, a conversational agent. Literally a design fiction, in Bleecker's sense, made by the company that eventually shipped the thing. Jobs isn't even there — he's at NeXT.
- **1999** — Jobs points the company at it, without the technology or the acquisitions to build it yet.
- **2005–06** — the capability finally arrives: multitouch, batteries, processors.
- **2007** — the keynote demo, held together with backstage string.
- **after** — projected line cut and redrawn.

Why this is better for students: none of them have Jobs's resources. All of them can publish the fiction.

## 3. Practitioners worth adding

### Dunne & Raby — already in the talk, worth sharpening

**Technological Dreams Series: No. 1, Robots (2007).** Four objects, now in MoMA's collection. Each does an ordinary domestic job. What is designed is not the job — it is the *temperament* the object brings to it.

MoMA's framing, worth quoting whole:

> What will our future interactions with robots be like? Will robots be obedient or defiant? Will our relationships with them be intimate, or equal? Will they take care of us or will we take care of them? Dunne and Raby explore the range of these dynamics, which have already become part of our lives, whether with Roomba vacuums or Alexa speakers. The robots — some independent, others needy, neurotic, and timid — are individuals with distinct personalities and quirks and behaviors that emerge over time.

- **Robot 1, "Ring"** — runs the home's computer systems. You never interact with it. Its one quirk: it cannot tolerate electromagnetic interference, so when a TV or radio or phone comes on it moves to the quietest part of the room. The interaction is *spatial* — its ring-shaped centre is a low-EM zone pleasant to sit in, so you put your furniture inside it. You arrange the living room around wherever the robot decided to go.
- **Robot 2, "Neurotic One"** — home security, run on anxiety. The moment someone enters it turns to face them and analyses them with its many eyes. Approach it and it gets more agitated. You interact by learning not to startle it.
- **Robot 3, "Sentinel"** — guards your data behind a retinal scan. Not the instant beep of film biometrics: it demands prolonged eye contact before it is satisfied. You sit and stare into it. Security as an intimate ritual.
- **Robot 4, "Needy One"** — extremely smart, physically helpless, dependent on you to carry it anywhere. Its voice evolves over time, developing its own language while keeping traces of human speech. You move it, and you listen to it drift away from you.

The design question moves from *what does a robot do* to *what is it like to live with one*. The answer is: difficult. These are not servants. Robot 3 makes you prove yourself. Robot 4 makes you pick it up. Robot 1 ignores you.

**What they are for — the slide that earns its place.** Look at the four jobs: home server, security camera, password manager, smart assistant. That is Nest, Ring, 1Password, Alexa. In 2007 they named the exact four product categories that arrived. MoMA's own text says it — "whether with Roomba vacuums or Alexa speakers."

The fiction was right about function and deliberately wrong about affect. The talk already reaches for this, comparing them to "the kind of HomePod things we've ended up with from Google and Amazon and Apple." Same jobs, opposite posture.

The argument for students: mainstream design makes technology invisible, frictionless, servile. These are needy, visible, demanding. If a machine has no needs, you never have to think about it — and not thinking about it is how a surveillance device ends up on a kitchen counter. Robot 2 is a security camera you cannot forget is watching, because it visibly flinches. Alexa's smoothness *is* the design decision that lets you forget.

**From Dunne & Raby's own project text** (worth having in full — it is sharper than any summary):

> Over the coming years, robots are destined to play a significant part in our daily lives — not as super smart, functional machines, nor as pseudo life forms, but as **technological cohabitants**. But how will we interact with them? What new interdependencies and relationships might emerge? These objects are meant to spark a discussion about how we'd like our robots to relate to us: **subservient, intimate, dependent, equal?**

Three details from that text worth knowing, beyond what is above:

- **Robot 2 inverts the design process.** "In the future products/robots might not be designed for specific tasks or jobs. Instead they might be given jobs based on behaviours and qualities that emerge over time." The neurosis came first; the security job was assigned to it afterwards. "Home security makes good use of this robot's neurosis."
- **Robot 3 is also a furniture question.** It "asks what new forms of furniture might evolve in response to future technological developments" — same thread as putting a chair inside Robot 1's ring.
- **Robot 4 is the darkest line in the project.** "Neediness is designed into very smart products to maintain a feeling of control." The helplessness is deliberate, so the owner still feels in charge of something smarter than they are. In 2026, in front of students who use AI assistants daily, this is the line that will land hardest. It is in the script.

Credits, if a slide needs them: video by **Noam Toran**, robot sound design by **Scanner**, computer modelling by **Graeme Findlay**, commissioned by **Jan Boelen** at **Z33**. In the permanent collections of MoMA and Fnac, Paris.

**Keep the self-correction in the delivered version.** In the recording the line runs: robots "that humans can wield control over" — then, immediately, *"control is maybe not the right word."* That hesitation is the project's thesis. Dunne & Raby designed the absence of control; all four require the human to accommodate the object. Do not edit it out. It is the moment the idea arrives.

Practical: the objects are catalogued individually at MoMA, so there are citable images. The project also exists substantially as a **film** of people interacting with them — the video does more work than photographs of the objects.

### Nick Foster — "The Future Mundane" — flagged for next time

Designer, Royal Designer for Industry, Near Future Laboratory with Bleecker, and for years Head of Design at Google X. He argued for mundane futures from inside the moonshot factory.

**"The Future Mundane"** (Core77, October 2013). Opens by going after designers who "grab at cinematic aesthetics without a plot," producing work that is "banal, twee and idealistic to the point of fantasy." Three principles:

1. **"The Future Mundane is filled with background talent."** Do not design for heroic super-users. Design for "those innumerable, un-named characters of Hollywood, the extras."
2. **"The Future Mundane is an accretive space."** New things do not replace old ones. "This stuff piles up, favela-like. Humans are covetous, sentimental and resourceful; they cling to things."
3. **"The Future Mundane is a partly broken space."** The future holds "countless partly broken realities: WiFi passwords, connectivity, battery life, privacy and compatibility."

**Why it earns a slot next time — two specific jobs:**

**It fixes the opening.** The talk currently opens on utopian *or* dystopian. Foster's third principle says the real future is neither: partly broken, patched, annoying. That is a better third option than the two now on offer, and it lands in the first five minutes.

**It explains the workshop's central finding.** `facilitator-guide.md` in `workshop-designing-for-the-future/` reports that broad prompts about "the future" come back as generic sci-fi, and half the room gets the same chrome-and-neon city. That is Foster's "cinematic aesthetics without a plot." He named the failure mode in 2013; Midjourney industrialised it. Hand students the diagnosis before they hit the wall, then let them hit it anyway.

Assignable: also delivered as a dConstruct 2015 talk with free audio and video, so it can be a listen rather than a read.

- [Core77 essay](https://www.core77.com/posts/25678/the-future-mundane-25678)
- [dConstruct 2015 audio](https://archive.dconstruct.org/2015/mundane)

### The Star Fleet Technical Manual (1975) — Bleecker's origin object

The book behind the Bleecker slide. Worth naming precisely, because the talk currently calls it "the fan fiction, the fan made book," and the real story is better.

**Franz Joseph** (Franz Joseph Schnaubelt) was a professional aerospace draftsman who began drawing Enterprise blueprints because his daughter was a Star Trek fan. Ballantine published it as a licensed trade book in 1975 and it sold like an actual reference manual — not an underground fan production, a commercial one. A companion *Booklet of General Plans* held the ship blueprints.

What makes it the origin object for design fiction is that it never breaks character. It is written as an in-universe document: stardated, issued under Star Fleet Command letterhead, containing uniform regulations, ship schematics, blueprints for the communicator and phaser and tricorder, the Federation banner — and a section of graphic standards, symbols, insignia, typography.

**It is a brand manual for a world that does not exist, sold in bookstores.**

The teaching point: this is 1975. Bleecker's design fiction essay is 2009. The practice preceded the theory by thirty-four years, and it was invented by a draftsman doing straight technical documentation for a fictional future. If the lecture shows this, show interior spreads — the graphic standards pages — not the cover. The cover looks like merchandise; the interior looks like work.

Slots into the timeline running alongside the iPhone material: Kay's Dynabook paper (1972), this (1975), Knowledge Navigator (1987).

**Full scan, free:** <https://archive.org/details/star-trek-star-fleet-technical-manual> — readable in the browser, downloadable as PDF (~45MB). So this can be shown in class rather than described, and students can be pointed at it directly.

The Archive's own contents summary is worth quoting on a slide, because the range is the argument:

> Gathers diagrams of spaceships, transporters, control stations, equipment, medical instruments, weapons, shuttlecraft, uniforms, insignia and fleet headquarters, and includes **Federation maps and treaties**.

Treaties. Anyone can draw a spaceship. Somebody sat down and drafted the diplomatic paperwork of a government that does not exist.

### ENO (Gary Hustwit, 2024) — the idea and the artifact are the same object

<https://www.hustwit.com/eno>

Hustwit — Helvetica, Objectified, Urbanized, Rams — made a documentary about Brian Eno that is generated fresh at every screening. Built with creative technologist **Brendan Dawes** under their outfit **Anamorph**: software sequences scenes and transitions out of hundreds of hours of unseen Eno footage, unreleased music, and new interviews. Roughly **52 quintillion possible versions**. Premiered Sundance 2024, screened as live events. Billed as "a feature film that's never the same twice."

Why it earns a slot over most of the other examples:

**Form matches subject.** A film about the man who pioneered generative music, made generatively. The method is the argument.

**The distribution is part of the design.** You cannot stream it, because there is no fixed version to stream. Streaming would collapse it into one film and kill the premise. That is a decision about how the thing exists in the world — exactly what students are being asked to think about, and the part they will skip.

**It cut a line.** A film was a fixed object. Every print identical. True since cinema began. Not true any more.

**Students know Hustwit.** Helvetica and Objectified are probably already assigned somewhere in their program.

Not in the script, but available: Eno co-founded the Long Now Foundation with **Stewart Brand** and coined the phrase "the Long Now." Since Brand's whole-Earth campaign is already in section 4, Eno is on the same thread rather than a new name. Eno's **Oblique Strategies** deck is also a sibling to Candy's "The Thing From The Future" — a designed object whose job is to change what you can think.

Unverified, worth checking before it goes on a slide: the generative engine is often referred to as **Brain One**, an anagram of Brian Eno. The hustwit.com page credits Anamorph and does not use that name.

### Faulkner — "The past is never dead. It's not even past."

*Requiem for a Nun* (1951). Sits in section 2 of the script, after the concrete cases and before the cone: a 1910 building still in daily use, cars lasting twenty or thirty years, the 1970s still present in 2026 in housing stock, plumbing, road layout, tax code. Foster's accretive space, compressed to one line. The new never fully eclipses the old.

**Do not use it twice.** It is quoted once in section 2, and then section 9 pays it off without restating it — Curry Hackett's alternative African American pasts are the same claim about a past that refuses to stay past, and the audience will already be carrying the line. Saying it again would flatten both.

**Worth knowing what it actually means.** In Faulkner the line is about the American South and the inescapability of historical guilt — race, slavery, the past as unpaid debt. Reading it as "old buildings are still standing" is a real drain on it. That is a legitimate extension and it works, but given the talk already engages Prado & Oliveira, Hackett, and Imarisha, the material reading and the historical one are doing the same structural work: what came before decides what futures are available. The second reading is available for free if wanted.

### Superflux (Anab Jain & Jon Ardern) — the biggest omission

**"Mitigation of Shock"** builds a full-scale London apartment in 2050 under climate collapse: improvised food growing, recipes for scarcity, an inhabitable near-future room you walk into. Bleecker's "archaeologist of the future" made literal — and it's climate work, which is the throughline. See also Anab Jain's talk, "Why We Need to Imagine Different Futures."

### Fixing the canon's politics

The Jobs slide already flags the "powerful white male" problem. The speculative design canon has the same problem, and naming it beats inheriting it.

- **Walidah Imarisha & adrienne maree brown, *Octavia's Brood* (2015).** Imarisha's *"all organizing is science fiction"* is the best single quote available for this lecture — and it comes from organizing, not design school.
- **Luiza Prado & Pedro Oliveira, "Cheat Sheet for a Non- (or Less-) Colonialist Speculative Design" (2014).** A direct critique of Dunne & Raby for defaulting to a white, European, middle-class future. Pairs with Curry Hackett.
- **Arturo Escobar, *Designs for the Pluriverse* (2018).** Sits on the New Design Commons material.

### Tony Fry — defuturing

***Design Futuring* (2008).** The line, as delivered:

> Unsustainable options remove potential futures… A species goes extinct and every future containing it goes with it. Tony Fry calls this defuturing.

Extinction is the anchor because nobody argues with it. The same shape transfers to a drained aquifer, a paved watershed, a material nobody can separate back out.

Three things worth explaining alongside it:

- **It is cumulative and it does not come back.** This is what separates defuturing from ordinary bad design. A bad logo is bad. A material nobody can un-mix is a permanently smaller cone. Skip the irreversibility and students hear "be more responsible" and file it with everything else.
- **Nobody intends it.** Defuturing is the default output of normal, competent,` well-meaning professional practice. Good intentions do not exempt a project, because the mechanism runs on effects.
- **The time asymmetry.** You spec a material in an afternoon. It is in the ground for five hundred years. A designer's decisions routinely outlast the designer by centuries, and almost nothing in design education takes that seriously. This is the one that lands on seniors, because it is about them.

**How to draw it.** Don't build a second diagram — draw it as damage to the cone they just learned. The payload is the *area lost*.

Keep the full Voros cone as a dashed ghost outline ("what was possible from here"). Draw the actual available cone converging inside it. Shade the gap.

```
        /  ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~  \      <- ghost: what was possible
       /  ############### 	    <- foreclosed
      /  ---------------
now --O=================>       <- projected
      \  ---------------
       \  ###############
        \  _ _ _ _ _ _  /
```

Better than simply inverting the cone: an inverted cone implies futures reach zero, which reads as extinction and lets students file it under doom.

**The order of loss is the argument.** Bands get eaten from the outside in — preposterous first, then possible, then plausible. So the end state of defuturing is not nothing. It is the projected line and nothing else.

*You lose the alternatives before you lose the future.*

That lands on the closing line already in the talk — "to keep that from being the only way." Defuturing is the process by which the moderno-techno-capitalist future becomes the only one. Same enemy, now drawn.

**Then the synthesis slide.** Once there is a direction, every example in the talk becomes a vector:

- Engelbart, Brand's photograph, Stallman, Wikipedia, the Dynabook — **widened** it
- Extraction, lock-in, disposability — **narrow** it
- The shipping container — **both**, which is why it is the honest example

And the degree-project question stops being "is this a good idea" and becomes **"which way does this point?"** A better prompt than the cone gives on its own.

Build notes: animate across three slides (full cone -> ghost appears -> collapse to line) so the loss is watched rather than described, and leave the shaded region unlabeled until the third beat.

### Parsons & Charlesworth (Jessica Charlesworth & Tim Parsons) — seven built slides, no script text

Seven slides exist in the deck (`4.3 Jessica Charlesworth and Tim Parsons`) and the script has never mentioned them. Their line, on every one: *"an art and design studio that develops tangible worlds as discursive tools for critically appraising urgent issues."*

Kristian's read, 2026-09-02: same vein as Dunne & Raby, but **more performative**, and a wider range of tools — drawing, sketching, models, full-size functioning designs, film. He particularly wants the multispecies sustainability work.

**Multispecies Inc.** is a fictional company making tools for "multispecies living," and they act as its de facto employees — designing, prototyping and publishing on its behalf since 2022, with real interviews with ecologists and scholars feeding it. Dunne & Raby make objects and put them in a vitrine; P&C staff an organization in public.

The sharpest thing on their project page is that it is built to fail on purpose:

> it will highlight the inevitably human-centric nature of design, in that, even while attempting to place other species first, the protagonists are constantly thwarted by the limits of human knowledge and imagination

They designed a company whose thesis is that the company cannot succeed. Same shape as the shipping-container wrinkle above — a preferable future with the problem baked in.

**Future Climate Souvenirs** does something §1 has no example of. Their framing: the climate crisis "through humor and speculation rather than doomsday scenarios." *Future Climate Souvenirs of New York* is six patches and a baseball cap from future nature reserves and ecotourist sites. Not utopian, not dystopian — a gift shop. Foster gives §1 *mundane* as the third option; this gives it *funny*, and it is merchandise from Bleecker's archaeology. A 2025 Chicago Architecture Biennial edition exists with a kiosk stand.

Also **Catalog for the Post-Human** (Venice Architecture Biennale 2021) — ClickbaitWear, Mycopops, the NootDial, plus an infomercial and a trade-fair stand. That is the range of media.

**One real fact worth carrying:** the project responds partly to the **zoöp**, an actual company structure giving non-human life a voice in governance. Real, not fiction. Same move as the Stallman note above — the designed object is a legal instrument — pointed at ecology.

**Where they go: end of §7, right after Superflux.** The section then escalates cleanly, all three already built as slides:

- Bleecker's IKEA catalog — an artifact
- Superflux's apartment — a space you walk into
- Parsons & Charlesworth — an organization you can transact with

Each rung bigger than the last, and it hands into §10, because P&C have already crossed from speculating to operating — the move seniors are being asked to make.

They also give §5 something it lacks. Sustainable, circular, decarbonized, decolonized — none of those is *non-human-centric*, which is what Multispecies Inc. asks about.

**Open question, blocking the draft.** Kristian says "full size actually functioning designs." The site shows objects, prototypes, an infomercial, a trade-fair stand, a biennial kiosk — but not which pieces actually work versus which are convincing props. That distinction is the whole Dunne & Raby contrast, since the robots are non-functional props for debate. Answer it before writing the passage.

## Loose ends from the 2026-09-02 session

**Credit the cone properly.** The band definitions on the slides are close to verbatim from Epaminondas Christophilopoulos, ["Special Relativity Theory Expands the Futures Cone's Conceptualisation of the Futures and The Pasts"](https://jfsdigital.org/articles-and-essays/vol-26-no-1-september-2021/special-relativity-theory-expands-the-futures-cones-conceptualisation-of-the-futures-and-the-pasts/), *Journal of Futures Studies* 26(1), Sept 2021 — which is in the are.na channel. Compare slide `2 Future Cone 09` against the paper's gloss on Voros: identical. Given every other slide carries `TYPE:`/`IMAGE:` and a licence URL, the band slides want a Voros (2017) credit via this. The paper also gives the fuller lineage: **Charles Taylor, 1990** ("cone of plausibility," built for geopolitical scenario planning), then Hancock and Bezold, then Voros's seven-band version in 2003. The script says Voros ← Dunne & Raby ← Candy, which is the design-world path, not the origin.

**Blauvelt's other connection, unused.** *Defuturing the Image of the Future* notes that world's fairs staged Indigenous people in "living dioramas" as the past against which the future was measured. That is Blauvelt, Whyte and Prado & Oliveira converging on one exhibition format. Probably too heavy to open §8 with, but it is the strongest connective tissue available if §8 and §9 should ever be one argument.

**The Lost Cause sentence, §9.** What separates Hackett from revisionism is that his work is openly fiction; speculative history claiming to be documentation is propaganda. The honesty about being invented is what does the ethical work. Undecided whether it earns a line or the room gets there alone.

**Still unbuilt: the mirrored cone.** §3 now describes a double cone — past cone below, future cone above, now as the pinch point — and the deck does not draw it. One added frame in the existing `2 Future Cone` build.

Gary Hustwit and his generative documentary ‘Eno’, and Brian (Eno) are a kind of futurist of the best sort — they make the worlds they imagine and do so materially — and shape culture far more than the futurists who think getting a certificate in Foresight is good enough to call yourself one.
