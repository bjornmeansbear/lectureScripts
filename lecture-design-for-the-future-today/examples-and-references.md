# Design for the Future Today — examples & references

Working notes for the lecture. Two kinds of material here:

1. **Vision-redraws-the-line examples** — the same structural move as the iPhone slide
2. **Practitioners** — speculative design / design fiction people worth citing

Already in the talk: Joseph Voros (the cone), Stuart Candy, Dunne & Raby, Julian
Bleecker, Parsons & Charlesworth, Katie Patrick, Curry Hackett, Angela Oguntala,
Buckminster Fuller.

---

## 1. Visions that cut the projected line

The shape: a named person or group holds a preferable future for an object that
sits out in the possible or preposterous band; they marshal resources and culture
toward it; once it lands, the old projected line is cut and a new one is drawn.
What is *probable* afterward is permanently different.

### Douglas Engelbart — the 1968 "Mother of All Demos"

Best pairing with the iPhone. His goal was stated in 1962 as *augmenting human
intellect* — a preferable future, not a product. The December 1968 demo showed the
mouse, hypertext links, windows, video conferencing, and two people editing one
document in real time, at a moment when computers were batch-processing machines
owned by institutions. Like the iPhone keynote, the demo was partly theater — a
leased microwave link and improvised hardware making something work just long
enough to be seen. Everything about how we use computers descends from that room.

### Masaru Ibuka / Sony Walkman, 1979

He wanted to listen to opera on long flights. Engineers objected that a tape
machine that couldn't record and had no speaker was simply broken; Sony's own
sales people expected a flop. What it actually created was a *behavior* —
private audio in public space — and that redrawn line runs through the iPod,
AirPods, and podcast culture.

### Stewart Brand, 1966 — "Why haven't we seen a photograph of the whole Earth yet?"

Buttons and a pestering campaign aimed at NASA, for an image that did not exist,
on the belief that the image itself would reorganize how people understood the
planet. *Earthrise* (1968) and *Blue Marble* (1972) became the icon of the entire
environmental movement. **Use this one** — it lands directly on the Fuller /
Spaceship Earth section already in the talk, and the "object" is only a picture.

### Richard Stallman — GNU (1983), the GPL (1989)

Free software was treated as ideological nonsense. Note for a design audience:
the designed object here is substantially *a license* — a legal artifact. Now
effectively all computing infrastructure runs on it. Good counterweight to Jobs,
because the mobilization wasn't capital.

### Wikipedia, 2001

"An encyclopedia anyone can edit" was preposterous enough that the serious
project was Nupedia, the expert-reviewed version — which failed. Wikipedia cut
off the projected future in which knowledge is made by credentialed experts and
sold to you.

## The ones that complicate it — probably the most valuable slides

### Malcom McLean and the shipping container, 1956

A trucking man asking why you would unload the truck at all. Ports, unions and
shipbuilders resisted. A corrugated steel box then restructured global trade,
offshoring, and the shape of every port city on earth.

**The wrinkle: preferable for whom?** It gutted dockworker unions and hollowed
out waterfronts. Preferable futures have losers, and the cone doesn't tell you
who they are.

### Alan Kay — the Dynabook, 1972

A tablet computer for children, as a learning instrument. Cardboard mockups and
a paper. Kay's line is the famous one: *the best way to predict the future is to
invent it.* But Kay has said plainly that the iPad is **not** the Dynabook,
because it fails the educational premise that was the entire point.

So: **the artifact arrived and the preferable future didn't.** This is the sharpest thing available to say to seniors about degree projects — the object can get built and the vision still lose.

### Buckminster Fuller as counterexample

Dymaxion house, Dymaxion car, geodesic domes. Enormous vision; the projected line mostly did *not* move. Worth one sentence, because visions fail — and that is not an argument against having one.

## 2. Reframing the iPhone slide

Stronger as a timeline than as a date claim, because it shows the vision
circulating in the culture for two decades while the possible band slowly widens
to meet it:

- **1987** — Apple publishes the *Knowledge Navigator* concept video for Sculley's
  Educom keynote: a folding touchscreen tablet, video calls, a conversational
  agent. Literally a design fiction, in Bleecker's sense, made by the company that
  eventually shipped the thing. Jobs isn't even there — he's at NeXT.
- **1999** — Jobs points the company at it, without the technology or the
  acquisitions to build it yet.
- **2005–06** — the capability finally arrives: multitouch, batteries, processors.
- **2007** — the keynote demo, held together with backstage string.
- **after** — projected line cut and redrawn.

Why this is better for students: none of them have Jobs's resources. All of them
can publish the fiction.

## 3. Practitioners worth adding

### Dunne & Raby — already in the talk, worth sharpening

**Technological Dreams Series: No. 1, Robots (2007).** Four objects, now in
MoMA's collection. Each does an ordinary domestic job. What is designed is not
the job — it is the *temperament* the object brings to it.

MoMA's framing, worth quoting whole:

> What will our future interactions with robots be like? Will robots be obedient
> or defiant? Will our relationships with them be intimate, or equal? Will they
> take care of us or will we take care of them? Dunne and Raby explore the range
> of these dynamics, which have already become part of our lives, whether with
> Roomba vacuums or Alexa speakers. The robots — some independent, others needy,
> neurotic, and timid — are individuals with distinct personalities and quirks
> and behaviors that emerge over time.

- **Robot 1, "Ring"** — runs the home's computer systems. You never interact with
  it. Its one quirk: it cannot tolerate electromagnetic interference, so when a
  TV or radio or phone comes on it moves to the quietest part of the room. The
  interaction is *spatial* — its ring-shaped centre is a low-EM zone pleasant to
  sit in, so you put your furniture inside it. You arrange the living room around
  wherever the robot decided to go.
- **Robot 2, "Neurotic One"** — home security, run on anxiety. The moment someone
  enters it turns to face them and analyses them with its many eyes. Approach it
  and it gets more agitated. You interact by learning not to startle it.
- **Robot 3, "Sentinel"** — guards your data behind a retinal scan. Not the
  instant beep of film biometrics: it demands prolonged eye contact before it is
  satisfied. You sit and stare into it. Security as an intimate ritual.
- **Robot 4, "Needy One"** — extremely smart, physically helpless, dependent on
  you to carry it anywhere. Its voice evolves over time, developing its own
  language while keeping traces of human speech. You move it, and you listen to
  it drift away from you.

The design question moves from *what does a robot do* to *what is it like to
live with one*. The answer is: difficult. These are not servants. Robot 3 makes
you prove yourself. Robot 4 makes you pick it up. Robot 1 ignores you.

**What they are for — the slide that earns its place.** Look at the four jobs:
home server, security camera, password manager, smart assistant. That is Nest,
Ring, 1Password, Alexa. In 2007 they named the exact four product categories
that arrived. MoMA's own text says it — "whether with Roomba vacuums or Alexa
speakers."

The fiction was right about function and deliberately wrong about affect. The
talk already reaches for this, comparing them to "the kind of HomePod things
we've ended up with from Google and Amazon and Apple." Same jobs, opposite
posture.

The argument for students: mainstream design makes technology invisible,
frictionless, servile. These are needy, visible, demanding. If a machine has no
needs, you never have to think about it — and not thinking about it is how a
surveillance device ends up on a kitchen counter. Robot 2 is a security camera
you cannot forget is watching, because it visibly flinches. Alexa's smoothness
*is* the design decision that lets you forget.

**Keep the self-correction in the delivered version.** In the recording the line
runs: robots "that humans can wield control over" — then, immediately, *"control
is maybe not the right word."* That hesitation is the project's thesis. Dunne &
Raby designed the absence of control; all four require the human to accommodate
the object. Do not edit it out. It is the moment the idea arrives.

Practical: the objects are catalogued individually at MoMA, so there are citable
images. The project also exists substantially as a **film** of people
interacting with them — the video does more work than photographs of the objects.

- MoMA: [Robot 1](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/110124) ·
  [Robot 3](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/110126) ·
  [Robot 4](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/110127)
- [Galerie des Galeries project page](https://www.galeriedesgaleries.com/en/invites/dunne-raby/technological-dreams-series-no-1-robots)

### Near Future Laboratory — Bleecker's own studio

- **Nick Foster, "The Future Mundane" (2013).** The essay for the argument the
  talk already makes — that a future still contains everything ordinary from the
  present. Futures should be peripheral, full of ordinary people, and boringly
  maintained. Currently unattributed in the talk; this is the citation.
- **TBD Catalog (2014).** The canonical Near Future Laboratory artifact — a
  deadpan catalog of mundane near-future products. Purer version of the IKEA move
  already on the slides.
- **Nicolas Nova** — the ethnographic half of that practice. Useful bridge to the
  DI200 ethnographic research material.
- **Bleecker, "Design Fiction: A Short Essay on Design, Science, Fact and
  Fiction" (2009)** — the founding text, if one primary source is wanted.

### The Star Fleet Technical Manual (1975) — Bleecker's origin object

The book behind the Bleecker slide. Worth naming precisely, because the talk
currently calls it "the fan fiction, the fan made book," and the real story is
better.

**Franz Joseph** (Franz Joseph Schnaubelt) was a professional aerospace
draftsman who began drawing Enterprise blueprints because his daughter was a
Star Trek fan. Ballantine published it as a licensed trade book in 1975 and it
sold like an actual reference manual — not an underground fan production, a
commercial one. A companion *Booklet of General Plans* held the ship blueprints.

What makes it the origin object for design fiction is that it never breaks
character. It is written as an in-universe document: stardated, issued under
Star Fleet Command letterhead, containing uniform regulations, ship schematics,
blueprints for the communicator and phaser and tricorder, the Federation banner
— and a section of graphic standards, symbols, insignia, typography.

**It is a brand manual for a world that does not exist, sold in bookstores.**

The teaching point: this is 1975. Bleecker's design fiction essay is 2009. The
practice preceded the theory by thirty-four years, and it was invented by a
draftsman doing straight technical documentation for a fictional future. If the
lecture shows this, show interior spreads — the graphic standards pages — not
the cover. The cover looks like merchandise; the interior looks like work.

Slots into the timeline running alongside the iPhone material: Kay's Dynabook
paper (1972), this (1975), Knowledge Navigator (1987).

**Full scan, free:** <https://archive.org/details/star-trek-star-fleet-technical-manual>
— readable in the browser, downloadable as PDF (~45MB). So this can be shown in
class rather than described, and students can be pointed at it directly.

The Archive's own contents summary is worth quoting on a slide, because the range
is the argument:

> Gathers diagrams of spaceships, transporters, control stations, equipment,
> medical instruments, weapons, shuttlecraft, uniforms, insignia and fleet
> headquarters, and includes **Federation maps and treaties**.

Treaties. Anyone can draw a spaceship. Somebody sat down and drafted the
diplomatic paperwork of a government that does not exist.

### Superflux (Anab Jain & Jon Ardern) — the biggest omission

**"Mitigation of Shock"** builds a full-scale London apartment in 2050 under
climate collapse: improvised food growing, recipes for scarcity, an inhabitable
near-future room you walk into. Bleecker's "archaeologist of the future" made
literal — and it's climate work, which is the throughline. See also Anab Jain's
talk, "Why We Need to Imagine Different Futures."

### Fixing the canon's politics

The Jobs slide already flags the "powerful white male" problem. The speculative
design canon has the same problem, and naming it beats inheriting it.

- **Walidah Imarisha & adrienne maree brown, *Octavia's Brood* (2015).**
  Imarisha's *"all organizing is science fiction"* is the best single quote
  available for this lecture — and it comes from organizing, not design school.
- **Luiza Prado & Pedro Oliveira, "Cheat Sheet for a Non- (or Less-) Colonialist
  Speculative Design" (2014).** A direct critique of Dunne & Raby for defaulting
  to a white, European, middle-class future. Pairs with Curry Hackett.
- **Arturo Escobar, *Designs for the Pluriverse* (2018).** Sits on the New Design
  Commons material.

### Tony Fry — defuturing

***Design Futuring* (2008).** The line, as delivered:

> Unsustainable options remove potential futures… A species goes extinct and
> every future containing it goes with it. Tony Fry calls this defuturing.

Extinction is the anchor because nobody argues with it. The same shape transfers
to a drained aquifer, a paved watershed, a material nobody can separate back out.

Three things worth explaining alongside it:

- **It is cumulative and it does not come back.** This is what separates
  defuturing from ordinary bad design. A bad logo is bad. A material nobody can
  un-mix is a permanently smaller cone. Skip the irreversibility and students
  hear "be more responsible" and file it with everything else.
- **Nobody intends it.** Defuturing is the default output of normal, competent,
  well-meaning professional practice. Good intentions do not exempt a project,
  because the mechanism runs on effects.
- **The time asymmetry.** You spec a material in an afternoon. It is in the
  ground for five hundred years. A designer's decisions routinely outlast the
  designer by centuries, and almost nothing in design education takes that
  seriously. This is the one that lands on seniors, because it is about them.

**How to draw it.** Don't build a second diagram — draw it as damage to the cone
they just learned. The payload is the *area lost*.

Keep the full Voros cone as a dashed ghost outline ("what was possible from
here"). Draw the actual available cone converging inside it. Shade the gap.

```
        /  ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~  \      <- ghost: what was possible
       /  ############### 	    <- foreclosed
      /  ---------------
now --O=================>       <- projected
      \  ---------------
       \  ###############
        \  _ _ _ _ _ _  /
```

Better than simply inverting the cone: an inverted cone implies futures reach
zero, which reads as extinction and lets students file it under doom.

**The order of loss is the argument.** Bands get eaten from the outside in —
preposterous first, then possible, then plausible. So the end state of defuturing
is not nothing. It is the projected line and nothing else.

*You lose the alternatives before you lose the future.*

That lands on the closing line already in the talk — "to keep that from being the
only way." Defuturing is the process by which the moderno-techno-capitalist
future becomes the only one. Same enemy, now drawn.

**Then the synthesis slide.** Once there is a direction, every example in the
talk becomes a vector:

- Engelbart, Brand's photograph, Stallman, Wikipedia, the Dynabook — **widened** it
- Extraction, lock-in, disposability — **narrow** it
- The shipping container — **both**, which is why it is the honest example

And the degree-project question stops being "is this a good idea" and becomes
**"which way does this point?"** A better prompt than the cone gives on its own.

Build notes: animate across three slides (full cone -> ghost appears -> collapse
to line) so the loss is watched rather than described, and leave the shaded
region unlabeled until the third beat.
