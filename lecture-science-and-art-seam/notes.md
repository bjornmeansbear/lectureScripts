## What the outline claimed

Section III says science erases the discoverer and art preserves the maker. Section V says design sits in the middle. It never says how.

## The claim fails as written

[K] Science doesn't erase the scientist. Peer review builds foundations that can be trusted, and later work cites them, so there is a record of who laid which brick, and when.

[C] Priority fights (Newton and Leibniz), eponyms, and the Nobel all show that credit is the currency of science. What science drops is dependence. The theorem holds without its author in the room, but the Ninth Symphony doesn't exist without Beethoven.

## Who the credit reaches

[K] Outside a Nobel or similar, scientists are mostly known only to other scientists in or near their area. The biography lives inside the field.

[K] An artist, fashion designer, or architect might be known to the public because their work is a thing people can see and experience. The work is in public, so the name travels with it.

[C] Test: is the work itself what people encounter, or does it do something else for them? Scientists who get famous (Einstein, Sagan, Attenborough) usually do it by becoming the public encounter themselves.

[C] Most design is used in service of something else, so attention goes through it and the credit stays behind. Closer to science than to art on this point.

## The museum evidence

[K] We go to science museums to learn about science in everyday ways, not to see an individual scientist's work. We often go to art museums to see a specific work by a specific artist.

[K] Art museums also teach, so this is about what draws the visit, not what the museum does.

[C] Does it matter if it's the original? A replica pendulum teaches as well as the first one. A copy of the Mona Lisa isn't what anyone queues for.

[K] The Apollo capsule is a design object. It's in the Air and Space museum, which is as much about designed objects as about the science of flight.

[C] That sorts museums three ways: ideas (replica works, maker is a footnote), specific works (original matters, maker is the draw), and made objects with a function (original matters, and the science is part of what the object is). Design lives in the third.

## Conceptual work is the middle case

[K] Art and design can be conceptual, just as science is. A conceptual artwork that is text on a card feels like something from an office, not the magical object people expect from art.

[K] A scientific concept usually comes with rigorous writing and documentation. A lay person won't read it, but it looks like a lot of specialized work was generated.

[C] Call that visible apparatus. Conceptual shows often add it back with sketches, correspondence, and instructions. Design generates it as a normal part of the job (research, sketches, prototypes), and case-study pages show it.

[C] Sol LeWitt's wall drawings and Lawrence Weiner's sentences are made by other people from instructions. The original is a certificate. A font file or open-source design works the same way, where every copy is the original. This is where the commons work fits: attribution and citation do the job that the unique object does in the art world.

## Does the designer's self belong in a design?

[K] Yes. We should care about designers: who they are, and who built on the idea of a previous one.

[K] Design is often an intellectual property problem. Designers pretend not to be referencing things so they don't draw a problematic connection for legal reasons. And individual designers are often absorbed by the corporations they work for, so a design is from a company, not a person.

[K] The cases where that isn't true: Dieter Rams for Braun (though he was Braun's design leader), and Jony Ive for Apple, same situation. Ive is in a harder spot now that he's left and is working on his own. The new Ferrari still looks like an Apple Magic Mouse. Does Ive have a different visual language?

[K] Philippe Starck, Karim Rashid, and Michael Graves did become household names in some respects, through work with Target and similar. IKEA still documents the designer for each product when an outside designer or studio did the work.

[K] Contemporary design at Target is now tied to influencers and TV personalities more than to known designers. The Magnolia collections from the Gaineses are an example. They aren't designing the things so much as choosing the qualities and look of objects.

[K] Room & Board doesn't tell you who designed their things the way the older furniture companies built on modernist legends do. Those names earned cultural relevance. Do contemporary ones? Will they? Are corporations too important now?

[C] This is a different answer from science. Science has a visible biography because citing earlier work is rewarded. Design has no equivalent, and legal risk pushes it to hide its references, so the chain of who built on whom is missing.

[C] Where a name does survive in design, it's usually because the company wants the story (Rams, Ive) or the name sells (Graves, Starck). The self is kept as a brand.

[C] Possible claim for the talk: design's self gets suppressed by law and by corporate authorship, not by its method. If so, that's a policy problem, which fits your commons work.

## Both reasons are real

[K] The designer also disappears because of how design works. When a thing is functional or practical, it's much easier to erase the designer, and people mostly don't care who made it. The designer doesn't much care about being known for a sign either. The whole project can matter, though, such as one that improves wayfinding across an entire campus.

[C] So credit may attach to the system and not to the single object. One sign doesn't need a name, but the wayfinding system does. Science works the same way: a single measurement isn't credited, the theory is.

[K] Science thinks it explains reality in concrete, repeatable ways. Art and design also try to describe reality, or preferred realities. Design often does it in concrete ways too, but the output is metaphorical, semiotic, and form-based, not truth or fact.

[K] Keep the outline's line from section II: both are compression, "finding the economical form that holds something sprawling."

[K] Section III says science seeks the pattern that subtracts the particular, and art seeks the particular that implies the universal. But science keeps using simpler and simpler equations to express more and more of the universe. Are we not the same in that regard?

[K] Different kinds of thinking: abductive reasoning in art and design, deductive reasoning in science. (Same question as my note in section III of the outline.)

[K] Stendhal and plenty of others say things should be beautiful and simple. But that beauty is individual and taste-based.

[K] *How Buildings Learn* is a good example of a place where personal taste, opinion, experiment, and real survivable building have to fit together. So are all the chair things.

[C] Either could be the concrete case section V is missing. It says design sits in the middle but never shows how (see the top of these notes).

[K] On section V's claim that a society treating art and science as enemies can't design well: if physics and feeling are together, that's what makes great things. And the great things should be open.

[C] "Should be open" is the commons argument again, the same place "Conceptual work is the middle case" ends up.
