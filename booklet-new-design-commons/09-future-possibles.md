# Future Possibles

In January 2007 Steve Jobs stood on a stage and demonstrated a phone that didn't work. The features he showed were being hacked together backstage by a team of engineers, in sequence, so that each one would survive being shown once.

At that moment the iPhone was possible. Buildable. What nobody knew was whether anyone wanted one. The critics who said it didn't fit the future were describing the future correctly as it stood — nothing about how anyone lived in 2006 required that object.

Then it shipped, and the future they had described stopped existing.

That's the mechanism this whole booklet depends on, so it's worth being precise about it.

## The cone

Our ordinary picture of time is an arrow. There's a now. Behind it, a past that looks like a straight line because we're reading it backwards, knowing how it came out. Ahead of it, more arrow.

But we don't know what's ahead. So the future isn't a line, it's a range — and the further out you go, the wider the range gets. Not an arrow. A cone, opening away from you.

If you've studied relativity you've met this shape already. Any photon arriving where you are came from somewhere inside a cone of possible origins. If it keeps going, it enters a cone of possible destinations. Same geometry, run in both directions from a single point.

Joseph Voros graded the futures cone by how likely each region is from where we stand:

- **Projected** — what happens if we do nothing and keep going exactly like today.
- **Probable** — the things already underway that will most likely continue.
- **Plausible** — consistent with what we know, less certain the further out they sit.
- **Possible** — we can imagine a path there, we haven't built it.
- **Preposterous** — ridiculous, impossible, never going to happen. Except that things which were never going to happen do keep happening.

Then a category that runs across all of them: **preferable**. The futures you actually want. That's a different axis, and it's different for every person.

Nobody's preferable future is the projected one. Nobody wants today, extended forever.

## Moving the cone

The iPhone case shows what happens when something crosses from possible into actual. The old projected line truncates. A new cone opens from the new position, and things that used to be preposterous are now merely possible.

Every invention and every change to how a culture works nudges the cone. Arthur C. Clarke's version: any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic. Think of all the things a person in 1500 would have called fantasy that you used before breakfast.

This is why designers should care. You are not predicting the future. You are making objects that move the cone, and you're doing it whether or not you're paying attention to which direction.

## What this booklet has been arguing

Here is the turn.

Enclosure narrows the cone. Commoning widens it.

Every enclosure in this booklet forecloses options, and it's the same harm three times.

When your tools are rented, someone else decides what you can make. Adobe cut off Venezuela in 2019 out of caution about sanctions it turned out not to be subject to. Every designer in the country lost access to their work. Whatever those people were going to make next left the cone that morning, by someone else's decision. And the softer version runs constantly: eight people in the early 1980s made a set of choices about what desktop publishing is, and most of the range of things a design tool could be has been unavailable ever since. Not forbidden. Just never built.

When images and culture are enclosed, the material you can build from shrinks to what you can afford to license. Copyright now runs seventy years past a life, ninety-five for a corporation. Most of how humans have made culture is copying, changing, and remixing what came before. A law that works against that isn't neutral about the future; it makes some futures unbuildable and leaves the ones already owned.

When materials are enclosed, the cost lands on people who never agreed to carry it, and it lands on the ones with the least room to absorb it. The neighborhoods due south of Baltimore's incinerator have the city's highest rates of asthma, lung cancer, and heart disease. A child growing up there has a narrower cone than a child two miles north. That's not a metaphor. It's a shorter list of available lives.

A commons is a wider cone. That's the whole claim. Shared tools mean more kinds of things get built. Shared culture means more people can build from more material. Shared materials mean the cost of making doesn't foreclose someone else's options downstream.

## Doing the impossible

In 1978, at the Toward Tomorrow Fair in Amherst, Murray Bookchin gave a talk called "Utopia, not futurism." His argument was that doing the impossible is the most rational thing we can do — that in a crisis, the demand for realism is the unrealistic position, because the realistic options have already been shown not to work.

He was careful about one distinction, and it's the one worth keeping. Criticizing technocracy is not the same as rejecting technology. The problem isn't the tools. It's who owns them and what they're pointed at.

That's the futures cone as an ethic instead of a diagram. Deliberately aiming at the preposterous end isn't naive. It's what you do when the projected future is unacceptable and the probable one isn't much better.

## The test

Which leaves one usable question, and it's the thing I'd want someone to take from all of this.

Does the thing you're making exist in the future you'd prefer?

If it does, make it. If it doesn't, that's a reason not to — and a reason to go find out what does belong there.

You can ask it about a poster. You can ask it about a career.
