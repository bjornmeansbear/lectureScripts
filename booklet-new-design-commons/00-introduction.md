# A New Design Commons

In September 2022 I woke up to the news that Adobe had bought Figma for twenty billion dollars. A former student who works there wrote to me that morning:

> The tools we use influence what we make and dictate the people who have access to them. Having a diverse toolset to choose from is good. Even if Figma doesn't change much in the first few years, relying on just one company for design gives Adobe way too much power in dictating which people and how people design.
> — Amanda Yeh

Three things worry me about the tools I work with. They can be bought by the company I was trying to get away from. They can be switched off: in 2019 the US sanctioned Venezuela, Adobe wasn't sure whether Creative Cloud counted, and rather than find out the hard way it cut off every Venezuelan IP address. No designer in that country could open Illustrator. Adobe turned out to be wrong about the sanctions. That didn't give anyone their week back.

And the people who make my tools are interested in something other than my work. For a while the Photoshop and Illustrator splash screens carried ads for *Rings of Power*, along with downloadable templates so you could make your own Rings of Power–style designs. A commons where the sharing exists to sell you something else.

We don't own these tools. We are granted access to them. Paying the rent on time doesn't guarantee the access.

That is enclosure. It is the same move as running barbed wire across the Great Plains or walling in an English pasture, done to the tools of a discipline.

## Three enclosures

This booklet is about three of them.

Our **tools** are enclosed. Rented, remotely revocable, and built on aesthetic assumptions we absorb without noticing. Mr. Keedy calls the result a Global Style — an affected modernism now adorning cultural institutions everywhere. I don't think a healthy commons produces that kind of sameness. If Silicon Valley runs on softened corporate modernism, and its tools are built on that assumption, and we are all told to use those tools, then we have no way to make except inside that frame.

Our **images and culture** are enclosed. Copyright now runs seventy years past the death of the person who made the thing, ninety-five years for a corporation. Most of how humans have made culture involves copying, changing, and remixing what came before. Copyright law works against exactly that.

Our **materials** are enclosed. The cost of extraction and waste gets pushed somewhere else, onto people who never agreed to carry it. The neighborhoods due south of Baltimore's trash incinerator have the highest rates of asthma, lung cancer, and heart disease in the city.

Each one takes something that was shared, puts a wall around it, and charges for the gate.

## What a commons is

A shared resource, available to everyone, managed for collective benefit. That is roughly Wikipedia's definition and it is good enough.

For most of human history this is how making worked. Someone found a solution and it proliferated. It was copied by the people nearby, improved, copied again. Art worked this way. Architecture worked this way. Music, fashion, gardening, blacksmithing, language. This is vernacular design — common design by common people.

We still all copy each other. It's just a secret now, and getting caught can be a legal problem. Whether it's a problem depends a great deal on the direction of the copying.

> My interest in the commons is grounded in a desire for the conditions necessary to promote social justice, sustainability, and happy lives for all. As simple as that.
> — Massimo De Angelis

The economist Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize for studying real commons — fisheries, water systems, forests — and found that the usual warning, the tragedy of the commons, gets the diagnosis backward. Commons don't fail because they're shared. They fail when someone ignores the self-governing rules that make the sharing work. Enclosure is that kind of rule-breaking: taking from the shared pool and putting nothing back.

## Why this matters for designers

Ellen Lupton, on *Thinking With Type*, in a 2006 aTypI lecture:

> My book was never intended for experts. It is a book for everyone, because I believe that everyone on earth needs typography and can benefit from working with letterforms at the highest level.

Widen that from typography to design. Everyone on earth needs design and would benefit from working with design tools and content at the highest level. That requires a more shareable, accessible, equitable distribution of recipes and resources than we have.

If we are in the business of cultural production and of passing on better possible futures, then the commons is our problem to work on.

Which is the argument this booklet makes, in three parts and one conclusion: enclosure narrows the range of futures available to us. Commoning widens it.
