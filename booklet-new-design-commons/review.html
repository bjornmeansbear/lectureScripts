A New Design Commons — booklet outline

Eight essays plus an introduction. One idea each. Short enough that each is also a video-essay script and a lecture module.

The argument

The introduction (A New Design Commons) already states it: intellectual enclosure produces "things that limit our future possibles." The booklet takes that seriously as a structure — three enclosures, three commons, and one shared warrant.

  1. Tools are enclosed (Adobe, licensing, rent-not-ownership) → the libre commons
  2. Images and culture are enclosed (copyright, Getty, the myth of individual genius) → the semiotic commons
  3. Materials and ecology are enclosed (extraction, waste, externalized cost) → the ecological commons

And in every case the warrant is the same: enclosure narrows the cone of future possibles; commoning widens it. That's essay 09, and it's why it goes last rather than first.

Part Four exists because the first three don't add up to an answer. Everything in parts one to three is something one designer does alone — change your fonts, change your images, learn Inkscape. That's ethical consumption against a structural problem. Teaching is the lever that scales: a brief reaches everyone who takes the class, every year, and shapes what a cohort thinks the defaults are before they have a job telling them otherwise. Without part four, the introduction's promise — how we might fight this enclosure — isn't kept.

How this directory works

Source files stay where they are. Nothing is copied in here. Each essay lists its sources in frontmatter and, once written, supersedes them — at which point the superseded drafts get a one-line pointer at the top saying which essay replaced them. No deletions.

This means the outstanding merge decisions (which WhatIsSGD draft wins, which of the three "Open Source Design" revisions is real) get resolved by writing the essay, not adjudicated in advance.

A New Design Commons

Introduction · drafting

On 15 September 2022, Adobe announced it was buying Figma for twenty billion dollars.acquire A former student who worked there at the time wrote to me that morning:

The tools we use influence what we make and dictate the people who have access to them. Having a diverse toolset to choose from is good. Even if Figma doesn't change much in the first few years, relying on just one company for design gives Adobe way too much power in dictating which people and how people design.

Fifteen months later the deal was dead. Adobe and Figma called it off in December 2023, saying there was no clear path to approval from the European Commission and the UK Competition and Markets Authority.terminate Adobe paid the termination fee and walked away.

I was concerned about the merger, Figma was sort of the most fun tool I had used in a while, and they generally seemed to have an "open" sort of feeling. I was worried that Adobe owning them would wreck that. And then, the thing I was worried about didn't happen. But, let's be exact about why. And why I'm still worried.

It wasn't stopped by designers. Nobody at Adobe or Figma asked us if this was a good idea or desired. It was stopped by regulators in two jurisdictions, on competition grounds. For fifteen months the future of a tool that a large part of this discipline opens every morning sat with a small number of people in Brussels and London.

That's the condition to notice. The powerlessness the deal revealed.

Three things worry me about the tools I work with, and the Figma deal touched all of them.

They can be bought.

They can be switched off. With the trend toward cloud tools, access is something a company grants day to day. In 2019 the US sanctioned Venezuela. Adobe wasn't sure whether allowing Creative Cloud access counted as part of the sanctions, and rather than find out the hard way, it cut off every Venezuelan IP address.venezuela No designer in the country could open Illustrator or InDesign or After Effects or Acrobat. Adobe later determined the sanctions didn't apply to their tools. That didn't give anyone their week back.

And the people who make my tools are not really interested in helping me make my work. They're interested in selling access to their digital neighborhoods to as many people as possible. They're interested in transactions, with anyone.

In August 2022, a month before the Figma news, Adobe announced a partnership with Amazon Studios: six Adobe Express templates and a set of Photoshop templates themed on The Rings of Power, plus commissioned artwork from six digital artists "for community inspiration."lotr Users found it in the apps before they found the announcement.lotr-reddit

Adobe is not against sharing. Adobe runs a commons — templates, tutorials, community files, all free to take. It exists to sell Adobe subscriptions, and that month, Amazon subscriptions too.

What this all really reveals is this: We no longer own our tools. We are granted access to them. We pay rent, and whether we pay it on time doesn't guarantee our access.

At the end of the day what this is is plain old enclosure. It is the same move as running barbed wire across the Great Plains or walling in an English pasture. We've allowed our tool makers to now enclose the tools (and even the files) of our disciplines.

What follows

Three enclosures, and the same shape every time: something shared gets a wall around it, and someone charges for the gate.

Tools. Rented, remotely revocable, and built on aesthetic assumptions we absorb without noticing.

Images and culture. Copyright runs seventy years past the death of the person who made the thing, ninety-five years for a corporation, here in the US. Most of how humans have made culture involves copying, changing, and remixing what came before. The law works against exactly that kind of cultural commons.

Materials. Textiles, closures, whole processes are patented now — technologies in themselves, licensed rather than shared. And the costs of extraction and waste get pushed somewhere else, onto people who never agreed to carry them. The neighborhoods due south of Baltimore's trash incinerator have the highest rates of asthma, lung cancer, and heart disease in the city.

Then a fourth part, which is the one that matters if any of this is going to move: teaching. A designer changing their own tools changes one practice. A brief changes everyone who takes the class, every year, for as long as you teach it.

What a commons is

A shared resource, available to everyone, managed for collective benefit. That is roughly Wikipedia's definition and it is good enough.

For most of human history this is how making worked. Someone found a solution and it proliferated. It was copied by the people nearby, improved, copied again. Art worked this way. Architecture worked this way. Music, fashion, gardening, blacksmithing, language. This is vernacular design — common design by common people.

We still all copy each other. It's just a secret now, and getting caught can be a legal problem. Whether it's a problem depends a great deal on the direction of the copying.

My interest in the commons is grounded in a desire for the conditions necessary to promote social justice, sustainability, and happy lives for all. As simple as that.

— Massimo De Angelis

The economist Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize for studying real commons — fisheries, water systems, forests — and found that the usual warning, the tragedy of the commons, gets the diagnosis backward.ostrom Commons don't fail because they're shared. They fail when someone ignores the self-governing rules that make the sharing work. Enclosure is that kind of rule-breaking: taking from the shared pool and putting nothing back.

Why this matters for designers

Ellen Lupton, on Thinking With Type, in her 2006 aTypI lecture Univers Strikes Back:lupton

My book was never intended for experts. It is a book for everyone, because I believe that everyone on earth needs typography and can benefit from working with letterforms at the highest level.

Widen that from typography to design. Everyone on earth needs design and would benefit from working with design tools and content at the highest level. That requires a more shareable, accessible, equitable distribution of recipes and resources than we have.

If we are in the business of cultural production and of passing on better possible futures, then the commons is our problem to work on.

Which is the argument this booklet makes: enclosure narrows the range of futures available to us. Commoning widens it.

Free as in Freedom

One — The Tools Commons · drafting

Richard Stallman worked at MIT's AI Lab in the early 1980s, back when researchers passed code around the way you'd hand someone a wrench. The lab's Xerox printer kept jamming. Stallman had fixed this kind of thing before — the old printer software sent a notice to everyone with a job in the queue, so whoever most needed the printer working went and fixed it. The new printer's source code was not available. Xerox wouldn't share it.

He quit, and set out to build free versions of everything, starting with an operating system he named GNU.

The detail he kept returning to wasn't the refusal. It was that the refusal was impersonal:

If he had refused me his cooperation for personal reasons, it would not have raised any larger issue. I might have considered him a jerk, but no more. The fact that his refusal was impersonal, that he had promised in advance to be uncooperative, not just to me but to anyone whatsoever, made this a larger issue.

A jerk is a person. A non-disclosure agreement is a policy. Stallman was arguing with a policy.

Free means free

The word causes trouble in English, so say it the way the Free Software Foundation says it: free as in free speech, not free as in free beer. Libre, not gratis.

You can charge money for libre software. People do. The point isn't that nothing costs anything. The point is that nobody stops you from doing what you want with the thing once you have it, the way nobody stops you from repainting a chair you bought.

Stallman wrote four freedoms:

  • Run the program however you want, for any purpose.
  • Study how it works and change it. This requires access to the source.
  • Redistribute copies, so you can help other people.
  • Distribute your modified versions, so the community gets your changes too.

Translated for people who make things rather than software:

  • Access to the recipe. What raw materials does this take, and can everyone get at them?
  • The ability to remix and redistribute, with credit.
  • An end to predatory vendor lock-in.
  • More collaboration.

Copyleft is infectious

The GNU Public License has a condition attached. Build on GNU-licensed code and your thing has to carry the same license. Use the commons, feed the commons.

Infectious openness. Freedom as a virus.

Not everyone liked that. Eric Raymond watched Linus Torvalds and a scattered crowd of volunteers build the Linux kernel faster than any closed company was building anything, wrote "The Cathedral and the Bazaar" about it, and worried that a viral license would frighten off businesses that wanted to build on open code without giving anything back. The MIT license he helped popularize asks only for credit. One step short of the public domain.

It worked, in the sense that most commercial software today quietly runs on open code its makers never have to share. Including software sold by Adobe. Whether that counts as the commons winning is a question worth sitting with.

Three myths

You lose control of your work. You don't. What gets added to or removed from a project stays governed by whoever maintains it. Open doesn't mean unowned.

Open is unsafe. Linux runs a large fraction of the world's infrastructure. Thousands of people reading the same code find more problems than a small team can. Many eyes make light work.

Everything is free as in gratis. The recipe is free. The ingredients may not be. Anyone can have the plans for a house. Lumber still costs money.

Why it's a design argument

Stallman's own summary is more interesting than most of the arguments made on his behalf:

Basically, free software combines capitalist, socialist and anarchist ideas. The capitalist part is: free software is something businesses can use and develop and sell. The socialist part is: we develop this knowledge, which becomes available to everyone and improves life for everyone. And the anarchist part: you can do what you like with it.

Dunne and Raby argue that all design is ideological — the process is informed by values, based on a specific worldview. If the status quo is a proprietary set of tools and an enclosed set of images, then designing alternatives to it means finding tools and images that aren't part of it. That's not a purity test. It's the practical requirement of a critical practice.

Ostrom's Law is the version I keep coming back to: a resource arrangement that works in practice can work in theory.

It works in practice. You're reading this on infrastructure that proves it.

The Libre Designer

One — The Tools Commons · drafting

I got here through a budget problem.

I was designing publications with no art budget. Not a small art budget — none. No stock photos, no hiring a photographer. So I went looking, and found public domain and Creative Commons images scattered across the internet. That was a practical fix for a practical constraint, and it turned into an ideology later.

The other route in was Drupal, in 2006. People all over the world building modules together, free to download, free to use however I wanted. Later, thinking about sustainability, I read How Buildings Learn and noticed that Stewart Brand's account of vernacular architecture — small fixes, copied down the block, adapted over time — described how Drupal had evolved. Same process, different material.

The libre designer is a character I play. A utopian device, useful for showing another way. I don't fully live there. I'm writing this on an Apple laptop.

Start with the pictures

Changing where your images come from is the easiest move available, for four reasons. It works with the tools you already have — nothing to install or reconfigure. You don't need anyone's permission. Often you don't even need new sources, just a different way of searching. And it clears a pile of copyright and contract problems you'd otherwise be carrying.

Places to start: Openverse, Flickr Commons, RawPixel's public domain section, the Public Domain Review, Archive.org and the Prelinger Archive inside it, the Noun Project, Undraw.

There's a design argument for this beyond cost and risk. Working from public domain material means you can't search "diverse office team high-fiving" and take the first result. Old material doesn't carry this month's visual clichés. It makes you build a meaning instead of borrowing one, and the work ends up looking like it came from somewhere.

Then the type

A magnitude harder than images, but not much.

Some libre fonts are already on your machine. Adobe Fonts carries part of the Google Fonts library, so you can switch a few on without leaving the software you have. Google Fonts has gone hard at variable fonts, which means most newer families arrive with nine weights and italics. The old complaint that libre type means one thin weight and no italics is mostly out of date. Raleway launched from The League of Moveable Type with a single weight, got forked, and the version on Google Fonts now has nine weights, italics, and a display cut.

Where to look: Velvetyne, The League of Moveable Type, Open Font Library, UseModify, Badass Libre Fonts by Womxn.

The part designers underuse: you can get the source files. If the family you like is missing a character or a weight, download the UFOs and draw it. If a client needs a custom face, start from the closest libre thing and change it. SIL's fonts carry enormous character sets because SIL makes language materials for communities nobody else typesets for. That's a different set of priorities producing a different kind of quality.

Then the software

This is the real chasm.

Frame it right or it won't work: these are not replacements. They are alternatives. They do things differently and you can arrive at the same place — a well-made graphic object.

Start with tools that have no proprietary equivalent, so you're gaining something instead of giving something up. Drawbot. Nodebox. Processing. OBS. There's no Adobe app that does what these do.

Then the rest, when you're ready: Inkscape, Scribus, GIMP, Krita, Blender, Darktable, FontForge, Audacity, LibreOffice. ImageMagick and Ghostscript run in the background and in the terminal — hand ImageMagick a PDF and get back a folder of JPGs.

File formats are the sleeper argument

SVG is an open standard that works in print and on the web. Illustrator reads it. Figma reads it. It's also XML, so a text editor reads it.

That holds across the ecosystem. A Scribus document is XML. You can open it in a code editor, see what's happening, add a page by hand, save it, and reopen it in Scribus. Try opening an .ai file the same way.

This is what you're buying: the ability to fix your file without the company that made it.

Machines, and the ones you already have

GNU/Linux runs on almost anything, from a Raspberry Pi to a mainframe. That means an old laptop that can't handle a current version of Windows is often perfectly good with a current Linux on it. My old ThinkPad is useless for contemporary Windows and completely usable otherwise.

You should be able to fix your computer. You should be able to change it. That's getting harder everywhere, and Apple is the worst offender — proprietary parts, trackpads that Linux kernels struggle to talk to, security chips that make the newest models nearly impossible to liberate.

And there's a design point buried here. A designer should have some say in how their studio works. On Linux you pick the desktop environment, the icons, the interface type. You can design the thing you design inside of.

Liberate the practice

Images, type, tools, machine. Then the work itself.

  1. Share your process. Successes and failures, and probably the failures more. Name who you took from.
  2. Share your source files. Documentation is better. Files alone still let someone open them and learn.
  3. Use text-editable formats where you can. Easiest to work with, easiest to version, easiest to preview on the real system.
  4. Collaborate. Design keeps insisting on the singular visionary. That's partly design history's fault.
  5. Donate. Work that didn't get used can go to the public domain instead of a dead folder.
  6. Contribute. Make examples for a type designer whose font you use. Build a site for a project that needs one. Answer design questions in an issue tracker.

Why designers don't

Won't get credit. Pride. Not wanting anyone to see how the sausage is made. File formats. Tooling. Fear of design by committee. Greed, sometimes — I might sell this. Mostly, no particular desire to.

None of these survive much scrutiny, and the benefits are good enough that staying out looks foolish.

Here's the part I'd rather leave you with. Desktop publishing was shaped by about eight people in the early 1980s, for a small Macintosh, and most of those decisions are still sitting in your dock. Designers are supposed to be the people who make interfaces. Almost all of us use the same software on the same machines, which gives us a very narrow sense of what an interface can be.

Mr. Keedy gets at the same thing from the other side. He named the "Global Style" — modernist tropes recycled without the ideology that produced them, in a register he describes as "I'm not going to try and impress you with skill, craft, technique, originality, or complexity, because that's been done already."keedy His sharpest observation is about the grid. In classic modernism the grid was drawn, argued over, visible on the page. In the Global Style it lives in the invisible coordinates of the software — the x/y axes, the pixel, the code. The aesthetic arrives feeling like nature instead of like a decision somebody made.

Eight people made those decisions. You can go and use something they didn't build.

There are tools out here that don't exist on a Mac. That alone is worth the trip.

Everything Is a Remix

Two — The Semiotic Commons · drafting

For most of human history, making things was communal. Somebody found a solution. It proliferated. The people nearby copied it, improved it, passed it on. Over time the improvements accumulated into something none of them could have made alone.

Art worked this way. Architecture worked this way. Industrial design, music, fashion, gardening, science, technology, language, blacksmithing — all of it.

This is vernacular design. Common design by common people.

You can still see it. Adobe dwellings in the Southwest and Cape Cod cottages in the Northeast are both the same process running in different weather: a technique found that answers local conditions cheaply and reliably, duplicated by neighbors, varied through imitation and experiment, forking into site-specific specializations. Nobody drew the master plan. The buildings are the record of everyone's edits.

Then it mostly stopped. Mass production made copying an industrial act instead of a neighborly one. Copyright expanded until derivative work became a legal exposure. And modernism's one-to-one semiotics made universal solutions the goal, which is the opposite of a regional variation.

The secret we all keep

We still copy each other constantly. It's just covert now, and when you copy too much or in the wrong way it stops being a craft question and becomes a legal one.

Whether it's a problem depends on the direction of the copying.

A student quoting a corporation is theft. A corporation quoting a student is research. Same act, different direction, completely different consequence. Any account of the commons that doesn't start there is nostalgia.

Where it survived

The vernacular didn't disappear. It reappeared among computer scientists, under three names — free software, libre, open source — and started running the same loop at speed. Share, iterate, copy whatever works best.

Thousands of people around the world built the Linux kernel together. That community had advantages no in-house engineering team could match: the product shipped constantly and was used widely, which subjected it to more scrutiny than any internal QA process could produce, and let fixes go out several times a day.

The reward system is the part designers should look at hardest. People contributing to these projects are not being paid. They do it out of need, curiosity, and pride. Recognition and reputation are the return. Motivation comes from the community.

To solve an interesting problem, start by finding a problem that is interesting to you.

— Eric Raymond

Stallman started building GNU because a printer wouldn't work and Xerox wouldn't show him the code. Pragmatism is at the heart of the practice.

Open source design has no style

There's no open source look. No visual guidelines, no signature. It's infrastructure, not an aesthetic — a way of enabling non-hierarchical, community-sourced collaboration that leaves no trace on the surface of the work.

Mozilla's Photon Design is the clearest case. Grid tools, color palettes, icons, motion principles, typography, built by contributors across the globe. It gave Firefox developers a cohesive toolkit and a consistent identity at the same time. It also made the thing genuinely accessible: Firefox runs in over eighty languages, and the diversity of the contributor base is why. Screen reader support got attention because people who needed it were in the room.

Smaller versions of this are available to anyone. Share data, assets, process. Show the mistakes. Show the technique. Ask a client whether the unused assets can go out into the world instead of into a dead folder. Libraries of icons, palettes, and layouts can be built and improved with almost no extra effort, out of work that already exists.

Garth Braithwaite's manifesto is short enough to quote whole:

I will:

find opportunities to design in the open

share my design experiences; both the good and the bad

find time for meaningful projects

openly participate in design discussions

work with other designers by choice

improve my toolbox

The honest objection

This is a privileged practice. It needs a computer, a baseline of technical competence, free time, energy, and the willingness to share. Not everyone has those.

Worth saying anyway, because the alternative model has its own problem. Social design usually installs an outside team as facilitator, arbiter, and organizer of somebody else's community, and often never checks whether the intervention worked. Open source cuts that role out. The power to name problems and propose solutions stays with the people who have them, and feedback comes from the user base, so change is constant and proportional to need.

Communities are not short of creativity. The solutions have usually already been described by someone living there. What's missing is time, resources, and access to infrastructure.

Keep the trail

None of this requires giving up authorship. It should make lineage easier to see, not harder. If you've credited your remixing properly, there's nothing to worry about in copying, combining, and transforming.

So stop hoarding. The old work, the good work that never ran, the syllabi, the projects. Share-alike them. The lineage doesn't need to be a secret.

What if we re-conceive the archive as a point of origin, as a birthplace for new works and a rebirthing venue for old works?

— Rick Prelinger

Sustainable Design Doesn't Exist

Three — The Ecological Commons · drafting

In grad school I had a stack of books on my desk about sustainability and design, and I was not getting through them. No sane person has time to read all of it. That was the problem I actually had, and the project came out of it: could I make the ideas in that stack easier to get at?

I started by mapping connections between them. Which ideas repeated. Which principles showed up in three books written decades apart. It turned into a zine — an essay per principle, written by me, each one pointing back at where I'd taken it from, so a reader could get the idea and then know where to go for more.

Then I looked at what I'd made and noticed I had only photographed the books that were already sitting on my desk.

Why not use the books.

The piece

For the exhibition, the books were the books. My actual copies, on strings, connected to screen-printed aphorisms — the long essays compressed down to single lines.

The constraints produced the form:

  • The definitions were screen printed on found paperback novels from a book share, on the front matter, so the novels stayed readable and went back to the book share afterward.
  • The embroidery floss connecting everything was cut in the longest pieces possible, to maximize what could be reused after.
  • The whole thing assembled in the gallery and came apart into its constituent parts when the show closed.

I'd call it a poster. It does what a poster does — it's posted in a public place and it has something clear to tell you. It also sits in a strange place between poster and sculpture, which is a useful place for a graphic design object to be in a gallery.

Four answers

That piece was my last answer to the question I'd been asking the whole time in grad school: what does sustainable graphic design look like? Across the work I'd made, four answers had emerged.

It looks the same. Good design is good design. Pick better materials and processes and nothing has to change visually.

It looks eco-friendly. Environmentalism on the sleeve. Kraft paper, plant motifs, natural dyes, visible reuse.

It looks innovative. Sustainability brings new tools, new technology, new social arrangements, so the visuals should be new too.

It doesn't exist.

The fourth one

That last answer started out as despair. Everything I make is waste. Anything I make requires unmaking something else. I'm turning the world into ephemeral garbage, and calling it a practice.

That's a real position and plenty of people stop there.

What changed it was noticing that the piece I'd just finished didn't exist either — in a different sense. It was ephemeral. It reused things that already existed. It left no trace. It came apart and went back into circulation. The books went home. The floss got used again.

So "sustainable design doesn't exist" stopped being a verdict and turned into a prompt. Can I make a designed object with no footprint? One so embedded in the systems that produce it that there's nothing left over?

That question generates work. The despair version doesn't generate anything.

What it's for

The pivot matters beyond one grad school project, because it's the move that makes the rest of this part of the booklet possible.

If sustainable design is a style, then it's a look you can apply, and it can be faked. If it's an absence — a thing that leaves nothing behind, that stays inside its own cycles — then it's a property of how the thing was made and what happens to it after, which is much harder to fake and much more interesting to design toward.

It also stops the question being about materials alone. An object fully embedded in the systems that create it is a description of a commons: nothing extracted that isn't returned.

Design for the Welfare of All Life

Three — The Ecological Commons · drafting

Sustainability gets drawn two ways, and the difference between them decides everything that follows.

The common one is the triple bottom line: three circles, social, economic, ecological, and you aim for the overlap in the middle. It's a workable practical model. It has one bad property — you can sit in the economic circle, ignore the other two, and still describe yourself as doing sustainability. Each circle is available on its own.

The better one is nested: economy inside society inside nature. Economic systems don't exist apart from social ones. Societies don't exist apart from nature. Draw it this way and every decision is a decision made inside nature. You can't accidentally leave a kind of sustainability out, because there aren't separate kinds.

The first is sustainability in practice. The second is sustainability as a worldview.

Name what you're sustaining

The key to doing something about sustainability is that you first have to say what it is that you want to sustain.

— John Ehrenfeld, Flourishing (2013)

Sustainability is always in relation to something. You sustain something. The word does no work on its own.

Look at the definitions in circulation and none of them finish the sentence. The Brundtland Commission, 1987, still the most quoted:

Sustainable development [is] development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Then the variations: equity over time, living within limits, leaving things better than you found them, improving human life without foreclosing other systems. The golden rule extended forward in time. All of them true. None of them says what's being sustained.

Which matters, because you can pick badly. Sustain the status quo and "sustainable design" means keeping this going. That's a coherent reading of the word and it's the one most corporate sustainability programs are quietly using.

Ehrenfeld answers his own question: he wants to sustain that all humans and other life should flourish.

Bruce Mau got somewhere similar with Massive Change: "our project is the welfare of all life as a practical objective."

Put that into design and the definition falls out:

Sustainable graphic design is graphic design in support of all life flourishing. Graphic design for the welfare of all life.

Which is a different job

All life flourishing is not the goal of business, of culture, or of design as currently practiced. Defined this way, sustainable graphic design is doing something other than ordinary cultural production.

Across Western art and design history, new goals and new tools have gone along with new aesthetics. If the objective changes, the way you decide things changes, and eventually the way things look changes.

Take picking a typeface. Normally that's taste — closer to choosing a style of house than to solving anything. Now give it an objective. Less ink is better, so pick the lighter face. Less paper is better, so pick the condensed one. Combine them: the thinnest, most condensed, lowest-coverage face wins. Push it far enough and you arrive somewhere ludicrous.

The absurdity is instructive. It's a real criterion, it critiques a decision that's usually arbitrary, and it produces a visual language you wouldn't have chosen. It also doesn't obviously serve the welfare of all life, which tells you resource math alone isn't the whole answer.

There are people working on this seriously. Ryman Eco is a big decorative serif drawn with inline elements knocked out of the letterforms — it reads as filled at text size while using about a third of the ink, and becomes ornamental when set large. On the web, subsetting a typeface to the characters you actually use makes the page load faster and take less energy to serve. Performance and ecology point the same direction there.

Ideological, therefore critical

All design is ideological, the design process is informed by values based on a specific world view.

— Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby, Design Noir (2001)

Dunne and Raby argue for critical design: design that provides a critique of the prevailing situation through objects embodying alternative social, cultural, technical, or economic values. Design works for this because people already know how to read designed objects. An interface, a poster, a piece of packaging is a more familiar window than an art piece or an experimental score.

With the welfare of all life as the worldview, sustainable graphic design isn't only design that helps people and the planet. It's design critical of the existing social, cultural, technical, and economic arrangements — because a lot of those arrangements are what's preventing the flourishing.

Green Acres, and getting it wrong

Green Acres: Artists Farming Fields, Greenhouses, and Abandoned Lots was a book I made with the curator Sue Spaid for the Contemporary Arts Center in Cincinnati. The artists in it used farming as their practice. I wanted the book to be as ecologically inventive as the work in it.

My idea was to stage the contrast between small art farms and industrial agriculture, visually. Aerial Midwestern farm photography at the section breaks. Decorative elements pulled from the logotype, referencing field furrows. A page grid built on township squares — the same tiny farm squares visible in the aerial shots.

Nobody gets it. No reader has ever looked at that book and understood something new about industrial agriculture because of the page grid. It's too abstract to function as critique, and it's an inert way to be critical of anything.

What actually made the book more sustainable was mundane: print on demand, recycled unbleached paper. Small runs, so no warehouse of unsold copies waiting to be pulped.

Otherwise it's a well-made modernist book. Which raises the question the rest of this part of the booklet is about: if my design conforms to every standard of good modernist layout, and the only thing I changed was the paper stock, in what sense is it sustainable graphic design?

Signs Signaling Sustainability

Three — The Ecological Commons · drafting

The principles of "good" modernist design are so embedded in culture that they are the principles of "good" graphic designing.

— Jerome Harris

We judge design using criteria inherited from the systems we say we want to critique. Contemporary formal goodness came out of Western art traditions between the late nineteenth and mid twentieth centuries, and those traditions have not so far produced all life flourishing. Usually the opposite.

You can watch it happen in real time. Every company rebranding right now arrives at humanist geometric sans-serif type. Modernism everywhere, all of it competent, none of it distinguishable. That's fine as far as it goes. The problem is that when it's the only measure of formal goodness we have, it quietly rules out solutions that might be better for reasons the measure can't see.

So what other criteria are available?

Beauty is a rendition of values

To call a work of architecture or design beautiful is to recognize it as a rendition of values critical to our flourishing. A transubstantiation of our individual ideals in a material medium.

— Alain de Botton, The Architecture of Happiness

De Botton says beauty is your values, materialized. He also happens to say flourishing, which is the same word Ehrenfeld chose for what we're sustaining.

Put the two together. If the value critical to our flourishing is all life, then design that renders that value is beautiful design. Sustainable design is beautiful design.

The inverse is the part with teeth. If your design doesn't account for the welfare of all life, then whatever it looks like on the outside, it's ugly. Edwin Datschefski calls this "the hidden ugliness of traditional products."

What's useful about this as an aesthetic direction is that it doesn't tell you what anything should look like. It tells you what the thing has to say. It indexes values rather than taste, and your values are probably broader than your taste.

A design is sustainable and beautiful when its form declares that humans and all other life should flourish.

The obvious objection

Beauty is in the eye of the beholder. Hume: "Beauty in things exists merely in the mind which contemplates them." Stendhal: "There are as many styles of beauty as there are visions of happiness."

This is not a new problem and I'm not going to solve it. Both things are true at once — beauty is plural, and a design that ignores the welfare of all life is ugly — because the second claim isn't about style. It's about whether the values are there. Two people can disagree completely about how a thing should look and agree about what it should be for.

The better question isn't what sustainable graphic design looks like. It's what values your design signals out to the world, and how it makes some part of sustainability tangible.

That's available on any project, at any scale.

Four that do it

The Amager Bakke vapor ring. Copenhagen's waste-to-energy plant is clean enough that its stack emits only CO2 and water vapor, and clean enough to be a public park with a ski slope on it. The proposal was for the stack to puff a visible smoke ring for every ton of CO2 captured. It was never built, for complicated reasons. It's still the clearest answer I've seen to a hard problem: how do you make an invisible thing visible enough to think about? (Bjarke Ingels Group)

Reverberation crosswalks. Brightly painted crosswalks and intersections in Baltimore. Paint on asphalt, which makes this graphic design. You cannot fail to notice them, which is the entire safety argument. The neighborhood around the intersection becomes more walkable the day it's painted. It's cheap, fast, easily replicated, and adaptable to whatever region, culture, and materials are at hand. Usually made with the school or neighborhood that will use it. (Graham Coreil-Allen)

solar.lowtechmagazine.com. A website run on solar power. Images are bitmapped, which keeps them high resolution at low file size and lets them be recolored per context. The site goes down when the battery does. It asks whether we need constant connection, and demonstrates that aesthetic choices correspond to physical resources even in the digital sphere. (Kris De Decker and Marie Otsuka)

The DC High Water Mark Project. Sculptural stanchions in the hundred-year floodplain around Washington, marking both where floods have reached and where they're projected to reach. The high black one is the future. You stand next to it and see the water without the flood having to happen. Without seeing your house or your park submerged, even symbolically, you can't picture the alternative. (Curry J. Hackett / Wayside Studio)

Two more worth having: Tattfoo Tan's S.O.S. merit patches, earned through actual sustainability certifications and worn on gray coveralls. And the Cradle to Cradle books themselves — the first printed as a technical nutrient, plastic pages with reclaimable ink, made to re-enter production; The Upcycle printed as a biological nutrient, paper and ink and binding made to decompose. The book performs its own argument. (Braungart and McDonough, designed by Paul Sahre)

Greenwashing

Which is why I stopped looking for a sustainable style.

If there's a single visual trope that means sustainability, it can be used without the ideology that produced it. Kraft paper and a leaf mark, wrapped around anything.

Around 2006 several computer companies started putting bamboo shells on laptops. ASUS did it. The idea that a bamboo enclosure makes a computer stop being a resource extraction and labor problem is funny, and it worked on people.

A trope can be stolen. A value has to be enacted, and it shows up in what the object does rather than in how it's finished.

What I actually think now

There is a central quality which is the root criterion of life and spirit in a man, a town, a building, or a wilderness. This quality is objective and precise, but it cannot be named. It is never twice the same, because it always takes its shape from the particular place in which it occurs.

— Christopher Alexander

I spent a long time hung up on how sustainable things should look. I wanted them to have their own aesthetic, something identifiable.

What I've landed on is that the look matters less than the values underneath it. Anything that promotes the flourishing of nature's interconnected systems will come out formally correct. It doesn't need a particular style or material or typeface handed down from outside. It takes its shape from the place it happens in.

A wild garden. A biodegradable book. A thriving farmers market.

Teaching the Commons

Four — The Lever · drafting

Everything in this booklet so far is something one designer can do alone. Change where your images come from. Change your fonts. Learn Inkscape. Share your files.

That's worth doing and it isn't enough. One designer using Scribus does not move Adobe. A structural problem answered with individual purchasing decisions is the same logic as recycling your own yogurt cups while the incinerator runs. It makes you feel better and it leaves the system intact.

There is a bigger lever available, and if you teach, you already have your hands on it.

What a syllabus does

A project brief reaches twenty people a year. It reaches them at the moment they are deciding what normal looks like, before they have a job telling them otherwise. Do it for ten years and you have shaped several hundred practitioners' sense of what the default tools are.

That's slow. It's also the mechanism by which every current default was installed. Nobody decided the discipline would run on Adobe. It got taught that way, for thirty years, until it stopped looking like a decision.

Defaults are not neutral and they were not inevitable. They were taught. They can be taught differently.

What that looks like in a brief

Require libre fonts. Not as a limitation, as an expansion. A student using a Google Font or a Velvetyne face can use it however they want, forever, with no licensing conversation. They can also open it. If it's missing a weight or a character, they can draw it. If they want a custom face for an identity project, they can fork the closest thing and change it. That last part is the actual pedagogy — you cannot open Helvetica and see how it was made.

Constrain the image sources. Public domain and Creative Commons only. This does more work than the licensing hygiene suggests. It means nobody can search "diverse team collaborating" and take the first result. Old material doesn't carry this month's clichés, so students have to build a meaning instead of borrowing one, and the work comes out looking like it came from somewhere.

Generate the source material together. At Nebraska I spent three semesters working with Byron Anway on a Color and Composition assignment built this way: instead of everyone searching the internet separately for reference, the class produced a shared pool of imagery and worked from it. The copyright conversation and the creativity conversation turned out to be the same conversation. What changed wasn't the output. It was that the material had a known provenance, and everyone could see it.

Share the student files, with permission. A class that publishes its source files is a class that can be learned from by the next one. It also teaches the thing that's hardest to teach in a critique: that the finished artifact is the smallest part of the work.

Use version control. Not because designers need Git. Because a history of changes makes the process visible, and visible process is the whole argument.

Share-alike your own teaching materials. This is the one that costs you something, so it's the honest test.

Are you really concerned with someone taking your syllabi or projects? What if you just Share-Alike them? Don't keep them enclosed.

I wrote the copyright primer for the semiotic workshop so that someone could run it without me in the room. That was the point. A workshop that only works when I show up is a workshop I've enclosed.

Access is the plainest argument

Requiring libre tools increases the number of students who can fully participate.

A student who can't afford the software after graduation loses the practice they just spent four years building. A student on an old laptop can run Linux and Inkscape and keep working. A student in a country under sanctions keeps their files. None of this is hypothetical; the Venezuela example in the introduction happened to working designers with deadlines.

Every proprietary requirement in a syllabus is a small toll, and the people who feel it are never the ones who'd tell you.

What I'm not claiming

Art schools have Adobe site licenses. Students need to be hireable, and the job ads say InDesign. A studio class that refuses proprietary tools on principle can hand its students a real disadvantage, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

I don't teach full abandonment and I don't practice it. I'm writing this on an Apple laptop, and I still open InDesign when a client needs an InDesign file.

The goal is not purity. The goal is that a student graduates knowing there was a choice — that the tools they use were selected, by someone, for reasons, and that other tools exist with different assumptions baked into them. A designer who has only ever used one set of tools cannot tell which of their instincts are theirs.

Teach one libre tool. Require one libre font. Run one project entirely from the public domain. That's enough to break the appearance of inevitability, which is the only thing holding the default in place.

The multiplier

An individual practice change affects one practice. A curriculum change affects every student who passes through it, and some of them will teach.

That's the difference between opting out of enclosure and actually contesting it.

Future Possibles

The Warrant · drafting

In January 2007 Steve Jobs stood on a stage and demonstrated a phone that didn't work. The features he showed were being hacked together backstage by a team of engineers, in sequence, so that each one would survive being shown once.

At that moment the iPhone was possible. Buildable. What nobody knew was whether anyone wanted one. The critics who said it didn't fit the future were describing the future correctly as it stood — nothing about how anyone lived in 2006 required that object.

Then it shipped, and the future they had described stopped existing.

That's the mechanism this whole booklet depends on, so it's worth being precise about it.

The cone

Our ordinary picture of time is an arrow. There's a now. Behind it, a past that looks like a straight line because we're reading it backwards, knowing how it came out. Ahead of it, more arrow.

But we don't know what's ahead. So the future isn't a line, it's a range — and the further out you go, the wider the range gets. Not an arrow. A cone, opening away from you.

If you've studied relativity you've met this shape already. Any photon arriving where you are came from somewhere inside a cone of possible origins. If it keeps going, it enters a cone of possible destinations. Same geometry, run in both directions from a single point.

Joseph Voros graded the futures cone by how likely each region is from where we stand:

  • Projected — what happens if we do nothing and keep going exactly like today.
  • Probable — the things already underway that will most likely continue.
  • Plausible — consistent with what we know, less certain the further out they sit.
  • Possible — we can imagine a path there, we haven't built it.
  • Preposterous — ridiculous, impossible, never going to happen. Except that things which were never going to happen do keep happening.

Then a category that runs across all of them: preferable. The futures you actually want. That's a different axis, and it's different for every person.

Nobody's preferable future is the projected one. Nobody wants today, extended forever.

Moving the cone

The iPhone case shows what happens when something crosses from possible into actual. The old projected line truncates. A new cone opens from the new position, and things that used to be preposterous are now merely possible.

Every invention and every change to how a culture works nudges the cone. Arthur C. Clarke's version: any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic. Think of all the things a person in 1500 would have called fantasy that you used before breakfast.

This is why designers should care. You are not predicting the future. You are making objects that move the cone, and you're doing it whether or not you're paying attention to which direction.

What this booklet has been arguing

Here is the turn.

Enclosure narrows the cone. Commoning widens it.

Every enclosure in this booklet forecloses options, and it's the same harm three times.

When your tools are rented, someone else decides what you can make. Adobe cut off Venezuela in 2019 out of caution about sanctions it turned out not to be subject to. Every designer in the country lost access to their work. Whatever those people were going to make next left the cone that morning, by someone else's decision. And the softer version runs constantly: eight people in the early 1980s made a set of choices about what desktop publishing is, and most of the range of things a design tool could be has been unavailable ever since. Not forbidden. Just never built.

When images and culture are enclosed, the material you can build from shrinks to what you can afford to license. Copyright now runs seventy years past a life, ninety-five for a corporation. Most of how humans have made culture is copying, changing, and remixing what came before. A law that works against that isn't neutral about the future; it makes some futures unbuildable and leaves the ones already owned.

When materials are enclosed, the cost lands on people who never agreed to carry it, and it lands on the ones with the least room to absorb it. The neighborhoods due south of Baltimore's incinerator have the city's highest rates of asthma, lung cancer, and heart disease. A child growing up there has a narrower cone than a child two miles north. That's not a metaphor. It's a shorter list of available lives.

A commons is a wider cone. That's the whole claim. Shared tools mean more kinds of things get built. Shared culture means more people can build from more material. Shared materials mean the cost of making doesn't foreclose someone else's options downstream.

Doing the impossible

In 1978, at the Toward Tomorrow Fair in Amherst, Murray Bookchin gave a talk called "Utopia, not futurism." His argument was that doing the impossible is the most rational thing we can do — that in a crisis, the demand for realism is the unrealistic position, because the realistic options have already been shown not to work.

He was careful about one distinction, and it's the one worth keeping. Criticizing technocracy is not the same as rejecting technology. The problem isn't the tools. It's who owns them and what they're pointed at.

That's the futures cone as an ethic instead of a diagram. Deliberately aiming at the preposterous end isn't naive. It's what you do when the projected future is unacceptable and the probable one isn't much better.

The test

Which leaves one usable question, and it's the thing I'd want someone to take from all of this.

Does the thing you're making exist in the future you'd prefer?

If it does, make it. If it doesn't, that's a reason not to — and a reason to go find out what does belong there.

You can ask it about a poster. You can ask it about a career.
