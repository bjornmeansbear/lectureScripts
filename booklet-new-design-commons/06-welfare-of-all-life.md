# Design for the Welfare of All Life

Sustainability gets drawn two ways, and the difference between them decides everything that follows.

The common one is the **triple bottom line**: three circles, social, economic, ecological, and you aim for the overlap in the middle. It's a workable practical model. It has one bad property — you can sit in the economic circle, ignore the other two, and still describe yourself as doing sustainability. Each circle is available on its own.

The better one is **nested**: economy inside society inside nature. Economic systems don't exist apart from social ones. Societies don't exist apart from nature. Draw it this way and every decision is a decision made inside nature. You can't accidentally leave a kind of sustainability out, because there aren't separate kinds.

The first is sustainability in practice. The second is sustainability as a worldview.

## Name what you're sustaining

> The key to doing something about sustainability is that you first have to say what it is that you want to sustain.
> — John Ehrenfeld, *Flourishing* (2013)

Sustainability is always in relation to something. You sustain *something*. The word does no work on its own.

Look at the definitions in circulation and none of them finish the sentence. The Brundtland Commission, 1987, still the most quoted:

> Sustainable development [is] development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Then the variations: equity over time, living within limits, leaving things better than you found them, improving human life without foreclosing other systems. The golden rule extended forward in time. All of them true. None of them says what's being sustained.

Which matters, because you can pick badly. Sustain the status quo and "sustainable design" means keeping this going. That's a coherent reading of the word and it's the one most corporate sustainability programs are quietly using.

Ehrenfeld answers his own question: he wants to sustain *that all humans and other life should flourish.*

Bruce Mau got somewhere similar with Massive Change: *"our project is the welfare of all life as a practical objective."*

Put that into design and the definition falls out:

**Sustainable graphic design is graphic design in support of all life flourishing. Graphic design for the welfare of all life.**

## Which is a different job

All life flourishing is not the goal of business, of culture, or of design as currently practiced. Defined this way, sustainable graphic design is doing something other than ordinary cultural production.

Across Western art and design history, new goals and new tools have gone along with new aesthetics. If the objective changes, the way you decide things changes, and eventually the way things look changes.

Take picking a typeface. Normally that's taste — closer to choosing a style of house than to solving anything. Now give it an objective. Less ink is better, so pick the lighter face. Less paper is better, so pick the condensed one. Combine them: the thinnest, most condensed, lowest-coverage face wins. Push it far enough and you arrive somewhere ludicrous.

The absurdity is instructive. It's a real criterion, it critiques a decision that's usually arbitrary, and it produces a visual language you wouldn't have chosen. It also doesn't obviously serve the welfare of all life, which tells you resource math alone isn't the whole answer.

There are people working on this seriously. Ryman Eco is a big decorative serif drawn with inline elements knocked out of the letterforms — it reads as filled at text size while using about a third of the ink, and becomes ornamental when set large. On the web, subsetting a typeface to the characters you actually use makes the page load faster and take less energy to serve. Performance and ecology point the same direction there.

## Ideological, therefore critical

> All design is ideological, the design process is informed by values based on a specific world view.
> — Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby, *Design Noir* (2001)

Dunne and Raby argue for critical design: design that provides a critique of the prevailing situation through objects embodying alternative social, cultural, technical, or economic values. Design works for this because people already know how to read designed objects. An interface, a poster, a piece of packaging is a more familiar window than an art piece or an experimental score.

With the welfare of all life as the worldview, sustainable graphic design isn't only design that helps people and the planet. It's design critical of the existing social, cultural, technical, and economic arrangements — because a lot of those arrangements are what's preventing the flourishing.

## Green Acres, and getting it wrong

*Green Acres: Artists Farming Fields, Greenhouses, and Abandoned Lots* was a book I made with the curator Sue Spaid for the Contemporary Arts Center in Cincinnati. The artists in it used farming as their practice. I wanted the book to be as ecologically inventive as the work in it.

My idea was to stage the contrast between small art farms and industrial agriculture, visually. Aerial Midwestern farm photography at the section breaks. Decorative elements pulled from the logotype, referencing field furrows. A page grid built on township squares — the same tiny farm squares visible in the aerial shots.

Nobody gets it. No reader has ever looked at that book and understood something new about industrial agriculture because of the page grid. It's too abstract to function as critique, and it's an inert way to be critical of anything.

What actually made the book more sustainable was mundane: print on demand, recycled unbleached paper. Small runs, so no warehouse of unsold copies waiting to be pulped.

Otherwise it's a well-made modernist book. Which raises the question the rest of this part of the booklet is about: if my design conforms to every standard of good modernist layout, and the only thing I changed was the paper stock, in what sense is it sustainable graphic design?
