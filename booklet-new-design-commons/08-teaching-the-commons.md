# Teaching the Commons

Everything in this booklet so far is something one designer can do alone. Change where your images come from. Change your fonts. Learn Inkscape. Share your files.

That's worth doing and it isn't enough. One designer using Scribus does not move Adobe. A structural problem answered with individual purchasing decisions is the same logic as recycling your own yogurt cups while the incinerator runs. It makes you feel better and it leaves the system intact.

There is a bigger lever available, and if you teach, you already have your hands on it.

## What a syllabus does

A project brief reaches twenty people a year. It reaches them at the moment they are deciding what normal looks like, before they have a job telling them otherwise. Do it for ten years and you have shaped several hundred practitioners' sense of what the default tools are.

That's slow. It's also the mechanism by which every current default was installed. Nobody decided the discipline would run on Adobe. It got taught that way, for thirty years, until it stopped looking like a decision.

Defaults are not neutral and they were not inevitable. They were taught. They can be taught differently.

## What that looks like in a brief

**Require libre fonts.** Not as a limitation, as an expansion. A student using a Google Font or a Velvetyne face can use it however they want, forever, with no licensing conversation. They can also open it. If it's missing a weight or a character, they can draw it. If they want a custom face for an identity project, they can fork the closest thing and change it. That last part is the actual pedagogy — you cannot open Helvetica and see how it was made.

**Constrain the image sources.** Public domain and Creative Commons only. This does more work than the licensing hygiene suggests. It means nobody can search "diverse team collaborating" and take the first result. Old material doesn't carry this month's clichés, so students have to build a meaning instead of borrowing one, and the work comes out looking like it came from somewhere.

**Generate the source material together.** At Nebraska I spent three semesters working with Byron Anway on a Color and Composition assignment built this way: instead of everyone searching the internet separately for reference, the class produced a shared pool of imagery and worked from it. The copyright conversation and the creativity conversation turned out to be the same conversation. What changed wasn't the output. It was that the material had a known provenance, and everyone could see it.

**Share the student files, with permission.** A class that publishes its source files is a class that can be learned from by the next one. It also teaches the thing that's hardest to teach in a critique: that the finished artifact is the smallest part of the work.

**Use version control.** Not because designers need Git. Because a history of changes makes the process visible, and visible process is the whole argument.

**Share-alike your own teaching materials.** This is the one that costs you something, so it's the honest test.

> Are you really concerned with someone taking your syllabi or projects? What if you just Share-Alike them? Don't keep them enclosed.

I wrote the copyright primer for the semiotic workshop so that someone could run it without me in the room. That was the point. A workshop that only works when I show up is a workshop I've enclosed.

## Access is the plainest argument

Requiring libre tools increases the number of students who can fully participate.

A student who can't afford the software after graduation loses the practice they just spent four years building. A student on an old laptop can run Linux and Inkscape and keep working. A student in a country under sanctions keeps their files. None of this is hypothetical; the Venezuela example in the introduction happened to working designers with deadlines.

Every proprietary requirement in a syllabus is a small toll, and the people who feel it are never the ones who'd tell you.

## What I'm not claiming

Art schools have Adobe site licenses. Students need to be hireable, and the job ads say InDesign. A studio class that refuses proprietary tools on principle can hand its students a real disadvantage, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

I don't teach full abandonment and I don't practice it. I'm writing this on an Apple laptop, and I still open InDesign when a client needs an InDesign file.

The goal is not purity. The goal is that a student graduates knowing there was a choice — that the tools they use were selected, by someone, for reasons, and that other tools exist with different assumptions baked into them. A designer who has only ever used one set of tools cannot tell which of their instincts are theirs.

Teach one libre tool. Require one libre font. Run one project entirely from the public domain. That's enough to break the appearance of inevitability, which is the only thing holding the default in place.

## The multiplier

An individual practice change affects one practice. A curriculum change affects every student who passes through it, and some of them will teach.

That's the difference between opting out of enclosure and actually contesting it.
