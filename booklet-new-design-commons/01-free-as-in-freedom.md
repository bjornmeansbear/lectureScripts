# Free as in Freedom

Richard Stallman worked at MIT's AI Lab in the early 1980s, back when researchers passed code around the way you'd hand someone a wrench. The lab's Xerox printer kept jamming. Stallman had fixed this kind of thing before — the old printer software sent a notice to everyone with a job in the queue, so whoever most needed the printer working went and fixed it. The new printer's source code was not available. Xerox wouldn't share it.

He quit, and set out to build free versions of everything, starting with an operating system he named GNU.

The detail he kept returning to wasn't the refusal. It was that the refusal was impersonal:

> If he had refused me his cooperation for personal reasons, it would not have raised any larger issue. I might have considered him a jerk, but no more. The fact that his refusal was impersonal, that he had promised in advance to be uncooperative, not just to me but to anyone whatsoever, made this a larger issue.

A jerk is a person. A non-disclosure agreement is a policy. Stallman was arguing with a policy.

## Free means free

The word causes trouble in English, so say it the way the Free Software Foundation says it: free as in free speech, not free as in free beer. Libre, not gratis.

You can charge money for libre software. People do. The point isn't that nothing costs anything. The point is that nobody stops you from doing what you want with the thing once you have it, the way nobody stops you from repainting a chair you bought.

Stallman wrote four freedoms:

- Run the program however you want, for any purpose.
- Study how it works and change it. This requires access to the source.
- Redistribute copies, so you can help other people.
- Distribute your modified versions, so the community gets your changes too.

Translated for people who make things rather than software:

- Access to the recipe. What raw materials does this take, and can everyone get at them?
- The ability to remix and redistribute, with credit.
- An end to predatory vendor lock-in.
- More collaboration.

## Copyleft is infectious

The GNU Public License has a condition attached. Build on GNU-licensed code and your thing has to carry the same license. Use the commons, feed the commons.

Infectious openness. Freedom as a virus.

Not everyone liked that. Eric Raymond watched Linus Torvalds and a scattered crowd of volunteers build the Linux kernel faster than any closed company was building anything, wrote "The Cathedral and the Bazaar" about it, and worried that a viral license would frighten off businesses that wanted to build on open code without giving anything back. The MIT license he helped popularize asks only for credit. One step short of the public domain.

It worked, in the sense that most commercial software today quietly runs on open code its makers never have to share. Including software sold by Adobe. Whether that counts as the commons winning is a question worth sitting with.

## Three myths

**You lose control of your work.** You don't. What gets added to or removed from a project stays governed by whoever maintains it. Open doesn't mean unowned.

**Open is unsafe.** Linux runs a large fraction of the world's infrastructure. Thousands of people reading the same code find more problems than a small team can. Many eyes make light work.

**Everything is free as in gratis.** The recipe is free. The ingredients may not be. Anyone can have the plans for a house. Lumber still costs money.

## Why it's a design argument

Stallman's own summary is more interesting than most of the arguments made on his behalf:

> Basically, free software combines capitalist, socialist and anarchist ideas. The capitalist part is: free software is something businesses can use and develop and sell. The socialist part is: we develop this knowledge, which becomes available to everyone and improves life for everyone. And the anarchist part: you can do what you like with it.

Dunne and Raby argue that all design is ideological — the process is informed by values, based on a specific worldview. If the status quo is a proprietary set of tools and an enclosed set of images, then designing alternatives to it means finding tools and images that aren't part of it. That's not a purity test. It's the practical requirement of a critical practice.

Ostrom's Law is the version I keep coming back to: a resource arrangement that works in practice can work in theory.

It works in practice. You're reading this on infrastructure that proves it.
