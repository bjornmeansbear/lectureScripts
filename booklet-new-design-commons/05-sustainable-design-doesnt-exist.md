# Sustainable Design Doesn't Exist

In grad school I had a stack of books on my desk about sustainability and design, and I was not getting through them. No sane person has time to read all of it. That was the problem I actually had, and the project came out of it: could I make the ideas in that stack easier to get at?

I started by mapping connections between them. Which ideas repeated. Which principles showed up in three books written decades apart. It turned into a zine — an essay per principle, written by me, each one pointing back at where I'd taken it from, so a reader could get the idea and then know where to go for more.

Then I looked at what I'd made and noticed I had only photographed the books that were already sitting on my desk.

Why not use the books.

## The piece

For the exhibition, the books were the books. My actual copies, on strings, connected to screen-printed aphorisms — the long essays compressed down to single lines.

The constraints produced the form:

- The definitions were screen printed on found paperback novels from a book share, on the front matter, so the novels stayed readable and went back to the book share afterward.
- The embroidery floss connecting everything was cut in the longest pieces possible, to maximize what could be reused after.
- The whole thing assembled in the gallery and came apart into its constituent parts when the show closed.

I'd call it a poster. It does what a poster does — it's posted in a public place and it has something clear to tell you. It also sits in a strange place between poster and sculpture, which is a useful place for a graphic design object to be in a gallery.

## Four answers

That piece was my last answer to the question I'd been asking the whole time in grad school: what does sustainable graphic design look like? Across the work I'd made, four answers had emerged.

**It looks the same.** Good design is good design. Pick better materials and processes and nothing has to change visually.

**It looks eco-friendly.** Environmentalism on the sleeve. Kraft paper, plant motifs, natural dyes, visible reuse.

**It looks innovative.** Sustainability brings new tools, new technology, new social arrangements, so the visuals should be new too.

**It doesn't exist.**

## The fourth one

That last answer started out as despair. Everything I make is waste. Anything I make requires unmaking something else. I'm turning the world into ephemeral garbage, and calling it a practice.

That's a real position and plenty of people stop there.

What changed it was noticing that the piece I'd just finished didn't exist either — in a different sense. It was ephemeral. It reused things that already existed. It left no trace. It came apart and went back into circulation. The books went home. The floss got used again.

So "sustainable design doesn't exist" stopped being a verdict and turned into a prompt. Can I make a designed object with no footprint? One so embedded in the systems that produce it that there's nothing left over?

That question generates work. The despair version doesn't generate anything.

## What it's for

The pivot matters beyond one grad school project, because it's the move that makes the rest of this part of the booklet possible.

If sustainable design is a style, then it's a look you can apply, and it can be faked. If it's an absence — a thing that leaves nothing behind, that stays inside its own cycles — then it's a property of how the thing was made and what happens to it after, which is much harder to fake and much more interesting to design toward.

It also stops the question being about materials alone. An object fully embedded in the systems that create it is a description of a commons: nothing extracted that isn't returned.
