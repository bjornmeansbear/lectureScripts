# Three Chairs, One Argument

*Circular economy and bioregionalism, made concrete through objects everyone already recognizes*

*Written fresh for this repo — not drawn from the Cambridge University Press essay this idea originated in, pending a check on what that contract actually restricts. Same throughline, same real objects, new words.*

*Related, and not previously linked from here: the published [Chair-ness](https://a.wjerk.shop/case-study-chairness.html) case study on a.wjerk.shop. Check it before extending this file — see `SURFACES.md`.*

A chair is a good teaching object because everyone already has a relationship to one. Compare a few real, well-documented chairs side by side, and the differences in how each one sources, uses, and disposes of material stop being abstract — they're right there in the object.

## The Navy Chair — circularity as a closed loop

Emeco's Navy Chair was designed in 1944 to a US Navy spec: it had to survive a torpedo blast belowdecks and keep working. The result is aluminum, built in 77 manufacturing steps, and sold with a lifetime warranty. What makes it a circular-economy case study rather than just a durable-goods one is the material choice specifically: aluminum can be melted down and recast indefinitely without losing structural quality, unlike most plastics, which degrade with each reuse cycle. Emeco has leaned into this directly, including runs made significantly from recycled and reclaimed aluminum. The chair's durability and its recyclability are the same design decision, not two separate virtues — a material that can go back into the loop cleanly is worth building to last, because "last" and "return to raw material" aren't in tension here.

## The Thonet No. 14 — efficiency as the constraint

Michael Thonet's Chair No. 14, from 1859, is built from six steam-bent beechwood parts, a handful of screws, and two nuts — no glue, no ornament beyond what the bending technique itself produces. The famous stat: unassembled, 36 chairs shipped in a single cubic meter crate. It sold in the millions across Europe in the 19th century, largely because the flat-pack shipping efficiency made it cheap to distribute at scale. This isn't a story about recycled material — it's a story about a single material constraint (steam-bent solid wood, minimal parts) generating an entire logistics and manufacturing system around it. Bioregional in a specific sense too: beechwood was a regionally abundant material in the areas Thonet's factories operated in, not shipped in from elsewhere to meet a design that could have used anything.

## Where this could go

Three real chairs, three different relationships to material — a closed aluminum loop, a wood-and-logistics constraint, and (pending your input) a local Baltimore case. That's a real, teachable structure: circularity isn't one strategy, it's several, and a chair make the differences legible fast. Could work as:

- A short lecture addition to Spontaneous Lamp or 3P, grounding the "what does circular design actually look like" question in precedent before students build their own.
- Its own short case-study handout across the whole circular economy pairing.
- Eventually, once the CUP question is resolved, a pointer back to the fuller published essay for anyone who wants the deeper version.
