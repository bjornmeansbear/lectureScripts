# SPONTANEOUS LAMP

*A circular economy workshop: build what you need from what's already around, engineered to come apart again*

> Working title, matching the existing project name. Alternates at bottom.

---

## The pitch

Each act of making is an act of unmaking. Every design that solves a problem creates one — new material pulled out of the world to make something that, eventually, becomes waste. Spontaneous Lamp works against that: a five-gallon bucket, foraged bamboo, loose pebbles and broken bricks, a clip-on light, an extension cord, assembled into a lamp because a lamp was needed right then. Built so it can stop being a lamp as easily as it started — when the need passes, the parts go back to being a bucket, a rock, a bamboo pole, instead of staying stuck together as something only useful once.

Students find a real, immediate need somewhere in the room or building, forage the materials to answer it — no new purchases — and build something that works. Then they take it apart again, on purpose, and sort the parts back into what they actually are.

---

## Day one — find the need, forage the materials

Short lecture: circular economy principles, "waste equals food" (nature's own material cycle applied as a design prompt), and the Spontaneous Lamp precedent — six built so far, ad hoc, at school and at home, each one solving a real and specific need. The throughline back to Sustainabilitism: this is the same question The Sustainabilitist Principles asked in 2009, just built instead of printed — what if a design didn't have to exist forever?

Students identify something the room or building actually needs — light, a shelf, a way to prop something open, whatever surfaces — then forage the materials to build it, from campus, the neighborhood, wherever's nearby. No new purchases. Start building.

## Day two — build it, use it, take it apart

Finish the object and put it to its actual use. Then, deliberately, take it apart — sort the components back into raw materials, the same categories they started as. Close with a group critique: what worked as circular design, what didn't, and what would it take for this to become a habit instead of a one-time exercise.

## Who it's for

Undergraduate studio, any level — no special background needed. Graduate seminar. 12–20 students. The lightest-logistics workshop on this menu: no special equipment, no safety gear beyond common sense, no power requirements beyond what the room already has.

## What they leave with

An object that solved a real, immediate need, built entirely from found material. Direct, physical experience with design-for-disassembly — not just designing something, but designing its unmaking too. A working answer to "what does circular design actually look like" that doesn't require industrial equipment to get to.

## What you need

- One to two days — this is flexible; a single afternoon can cover forage-build-use, a second day adds real reflection time before disassembly
- Any studio space
- Access to a "material commons" — a spot on or near campus where found material can actually be gathered (a surplus bin, a scrap pile, a walk around the neighborhood)
- Basic hand tools: wire cutters, tape, whatever fits what gets found

## Fee

$2,000–$3,000 plus travel and materials. Single-day version available.

## Prior versions

- An ongoing, informal solo project — six lamps built so far, at school and at home, for colleagues along the way. Real precedent for the *idea*, but never run as a group workshop with participants before — the two-day structure here is entirely new. ## Notes to self

- This is the lightest-logistics, easiest-to-book workshop on the whole menu — but also has the least group-workshop track record. Worth a trial run before the first real pitch, same as Open Source Design and Form/Content/Context.
