# 3P: PEOPLE PROCESSING PLASTIC

*A workshop on turning trash plastic into designed objects, live, in public*

> Working title, matching the existing project name. Alternates at bottom.

---

## The pitch

Plastic is one of the most recyclable materials there is, and almost none of it actually gets recycled — it's treated as too low-value to bother with, so it ends up as trash, then as poison. 3P is a traveling, palletized processing station: a home oven rebuilt into a plastic melter, a hand-built grinder, a shop press. Found HDPE goes in. Designed objects come out, made in public, by whoever's in the room.

Students grind, melt, and mold real found plastic into small objects — start to finish, in front of an audience if possible. The making is the argument: plastic isn't trash, it's underused material, and the tools to prove that fit on a pallet.

**This one needs real buy-in.** The equipment is hand-built and semi-improvised — motor torque, temperature control, mold pressure are all things a semester-long class has time to work through with a trusted team, not guarantees. This works best with participants who want to actually troubleshoot alongside the machinery, not just watch a smooth process happen. Pitch it as part of the experience, not a hidden risk.

---

## Day one — the station and the critique

Short lecture: why plastic ends up as waste despite being one of the most reusable materials available, and how [Precious Plastic](https://preciousplastic.com/), an open-source hardware project, made small-scale plastic recycling buildable by anyone. Set up the station — oven, grinder, press — and start collecting and sorting found plastic on-site.

Students begin grinding and melting a first batch, working out mold-box dimensions and material volumes by hand, the way the original station was built: ad hoc, from whatever's on hand.

## Day two — molding, in public

Students press, mold, and finish their objects. If the setting allows, the second day runs as a public performance, not a closed studio session — passersby watch plastic get reprocessed into something new, and the process itself becomes the pitch for why this material shouldn't be disposable.

Close with a group critique: what worked as a material, what didn't, and what it would take to keep a small recycling operation like this running past the two days.

## Who it's for

Fairly advanced undergraduate studio, junior or senior — product design, industrial design, or sculpture background helpful but not required. Graduate seminar. 12–16 students, smaller than the other workshops given the equipment and safety supervision involved. Works best with a group genuinely willing to troubleshoot, not just spectate.

## What they leave with

At least one finished object, made from plastic that would otherwise be waste. Hands-on experience with a real, small-scale circular manufacturing process — including its rough edges. A working answer to "what does circular design actually look like," made rather than argued.

## What you need

- Two full days
- Ventilated shop space, not a standard classroom — this involves heat, fumes, machinery, and 240V power
- The 3P station itself (oven, grinder, press) shipped or built on-site from Precious Plastic's open plans; needs advance logistics planning, unlike the other workshops on this menu
- Safety gear: gloves, eye protection, closed-toe shoes, possibly more depending on location
- A steady supply of found HDPE, sourced locally in advance — or make collecting it part of the class's own prep work

## Fee

$3,500–$5,000 plus travel, shipping, and materials — higher than the other workshops given equipment logistics. Ask about a lighter single-day version: critique and small-batch processing only, no full object production, still real learning about recycling and mold-making.

- Ongoing public performances and processing events — follow [@people_processing_plastic](https://www.instagram.com/people_processing_plastic/) on Instagram.

This is real, tested classroom material, not a proposal — but it's never been packaged as a standalone traveling workshop before.
