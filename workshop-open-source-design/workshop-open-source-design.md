# THE LIBRE DESIGNER

*A workshop on open source, the commons, and refusing to rent your tools*

> Working title, matching the existing [case study](https://a.wjerk.shop/case-study-libre-designer.html) on a.wjerk.shop. Alternates at bottom.

---

## The pitch

The libre designer is a utopian character — a way of working where nothing you depend on can be locked away from you. Free and libre open source tools, openly licensed fonts and images, and a design practice built on the same logic that has always driven culture: copy, improve, share.

Students design something real using only F/LOSS tools and openly licensed material, post their work-in-progress files publicly instead of privately, and give and receive critique through the files themselves — the way open source communities actually work, not the way design studios usually do.

---

## Day one — enclosure and precedent

Short lecture. Rented tools are a risk, not an inconvenience: Adobe cut off Venezuela's Creative Cloud access in 2019 to comply with US sanctions, and paying the rent turned out not to guarantee anything. The word is "libre," not "free" — the point was never to give design away for nothing, it's to not be locked into a tool, format, or ecosystem you can't get back out of.

Then the precedent, which is older and more design-specific than students expect. Donald Knuth, offended by the photo-typeset proofs of his own book in the 1970s, wrote his own typesetting system rather than accept them. That was TeX, and alongside it Metafont — one master set of instructions that draws a whole family of typefaces, decades before anyone said "variable font." He gave both away. Around that: Stewart Brand on vernacular architecture, Stallman's Four Freedoms, Raymond's *Cathedral and the Bazaar*, and Garth Braithwaite's Open Source Design Manifesto.

Students then pick a stack — Inkscape, GIMP, Blender, Scribus, HTML/CSS, or something with no proprietary equivalent at all like Processing or DrawBot — and start building with openly licensed fonts and imagery. Work-in-progress files go up on a shared repo immediately, not at the end.

## Day two — the issue queue

Instead of a normal critique, students open issues against each other's files, the way open source projects surface problems, and try to actually fix something in someone else's project rather than just comment on it.

This was the real class's method and it was harder than expected. Surface feedback comes easily. Opening someone else's files and understanding their decisions well enough to repair something takes real time, and only a few people in any group get there. Saying that out loud at the start is part of the workshop.

Everyone finishes their piece, licenses it CC BY-SA to match the terms of what they built it from, and the group compiles the work into one shared publication, laid out and printed with the same F/LOSS tools. We close on friction: format limits, unfamiliar tooling, the client who needs an InDesign file. The goal isn't full adoption. It's having more tools available, and noticing how much of "good design" is the accumulated habit of a few programs built by a handful of people in the 1980s.

## Who it's for

Undergraduate design studio, any level. Graduate design seminars. 12–20 students. Needs laptops that can run the chosen tools, and a shared GitHub space — or just accounts, and I'll add everyone to one repo on the day.

## What they leave with

A finished piece made entirely outside the Adobe ecosystem, with its working files public and licensed back to the commons it drew from. Direct experience of file-based critique instead of verbal-only feedback. Working basics of Git and GitHub, which are useful well beyond design. And a practical conversation about licensing and openness as they apply to real clients, not just to principle.

## What you need

- Two days
- A computer lab, or students' own laptops with F/LOSS tools installed or installable
- A shared GitHub org or repo, set up beforehand if possible
- Internet access for sourcing fonts and images, and for publishing the result

## Fee

Weekend workshop. $2,000–$3,000 plus travel and materials. Single-day version available — lecture plus a compressed build, no issue-queue round.

Students worked in F/LOSS tools all term, posted files to GitHub, critiqued through issue queues, and made real print and signage with MICA's library from public-domain archive images.
