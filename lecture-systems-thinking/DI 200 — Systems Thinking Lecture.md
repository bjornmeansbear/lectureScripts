## 1. Opener — What the Cart Video Didn't Show (\~15 min)

- Revisit the IDEO shopping cart clip (already watched). They do touch maintenance, a little.
- But what they were really focused on was how the cart works differently in the store. It doesn't ultimately change shopping — it just changed the cart.
- Are they considering what happens when these things wait outside? In the rain, the snow, the heat, the cold?
- What happens when people try to steal them — like frequently happens in Baltimore?
- Are they adjustable to height? They didn't seem to really worry about any of those things.
- Then Laura Kampf: she took a broken shopping cart and turned it into a chair. Her point — very little maintenance actually happens on these carts. Even something as simple as a stuck wheel doesn't get fixed.
- **Two-part takeaway:**
  - **The world wears it down:** shopping carts are out in the world, get damaged, and don't get fixed — no matter how well or interestingly you designed them.
  - **The system moves on:** the IDEO cart is from the late '90s — almost three decades ago. What is shopping now, with Instacart, Uber Eats, and all these apps? Should there even be a different kind of cart now? The system changed around the object.
- Exercise: still of the cart on screen; class calls out everything off-camera until the cart is tiny in the middle.

**Turn:** The cart is a concrete object. It's very simple, and it was very apparent there's this much larger system around it. Almost all of your projects are much more complicated — or at least they're in this hotel, which is much more complicated in terms of the system. So let's try to zoom out and see that in the context of all your projects.

## 2. The Stance — Assume the Hidden (\~10 min)

**From my practice:**

- Web projects: "We want a web store." Okay — how's inventory tracked? How are you already doing these things? Or does the website have to take over doing all of them?
- MICA admissions, graduate catalog: the idea is often "we just need to make a giant catalog to send everybody." But is that really the best way to engage everybody? Are there other things actually important to a person, rather than just tons of pictures of pretty work? If they're a designer, how do they know which of our many design programs is really for them? Is reading about them the right way? Can we show them — audio, video? What are the other ways to enlighten and engage someone?

* Systems thinking isn't a later step; it's how you look from minute one.
* Every observation carries an invisible "…and behind this."
* Anticipating the system early, even incorrectly, turns blanks into questions someone can answer.
* Connection: sacrificial prototype (Wk4), at system scale. Strong-link idea: guesses are cheap, only corrected ones carry forward.
* The Hilton story: they even asked for something simpler — "let's get a crab mural in the lobby." Then we zoomed out and said, well, what are you really trying to do? Now we ought to zoom out even more: if we do one of these things, how does it actually get maintained and supported?
* *Frame — at the Hilton: the brief says a lobby installation that promotes Baltimore. But who is it for, who keeps it running, and what's it competing with? Zoom out — your future: every client will hand you an object. Your first move is always to ask what system it lives in.*
* **Keep reiterating — wicked problems & placements (Buchanan):** the perspective of the person analyzing the problem shapes the problem. Any client has their placement, which affects what they're asking for. Zooming out to see the interlinking systems and services — what are we actually trying to provide this person? — gives us other placements, so we can perhaps offer back a different pitch in response to what we get asked.

## Definitions (\~10 min)

- **Service:** value delivered through an interaction over time, made together with the person receiving it. You can't stockpile a check-in — it only exists when it happens.
- **System:** elements, the interconnections between them, and a purpose (Donella Meadows). The hotel, the city, the conference circuit.
- **Service design:** designing the whole experience and the operations behind it — touchpoints, people, processes, both sides of the line of visibility. Signature tool: the blueprint.
- **Systems design:** shaping the structures, rules, flows, and feedback loops that drive behavior. Bigger, slower, rarely in your full control.

**"You can design a service. You can only intervene in a system."**

## 3. Tool — The Service Blueprint & the Line of Visibility (\~10 min)

- Layers: guest actions / frontstage staff / line of visibility / backstage / support processes.
- Hilton legal drew the line for us: front-of-house only.
- Read the backstage through traces: staff doors, carts, cleaning times, conference badges.
- Unanswerable questions become marked assumptions, not dead ends.
- Example: Ella's kids' art class looks like a lobby activity but runs on event sales, staffing, liability, scheduling.
- *You put your idea into the blueprint, and the blueprint helps you see the systems at play around and inside your service. Two uses: diagnose the service as it exists, or drop in your proposal and see what it demands from the layers below. And for a client: it shows you're not just making something up — you're thinking about how it fits into how they actually do things. Note: our Observations table already tags front stage / back stage / threshold.*
- **The goal:** we haven't been able to get these questions answered — so let's think through it anyway. Who else will we need to talk to? What else will we need to ask, and know? Who would we need to loop in to find out who would actually be in charge of this? What desk would manage it?

**Support processes at the Hilton — who sits under the line:** Hilton doesn't own the hotel, it only manages it (asset-light). The Baltimore Hilton was built with public money as a city convention hotel. So at least three parties: the owner (the building has to pay off), Hilton (brand standards and fees), and the city (convention traffic downtown). A lobby installation has to satisfy all three — and that may explain why legal is so cautious. Brainstorm: why would a hotel brand not own its hotels?

## 4. Exercise — The Margin Map (\~30 min)

- Move from the class-wide cart exercise into small groups (3–4). Each student puts their current prototype in the center of a large sheet.
- Fill the margins: who makes it, maintains it, refills it, says no to it, is affected outside the revolving door.
- Mark each item: observed / guessed / unknown (?).
- Group works each map in turn: what broader systems does this prototype need to think about, engage with, and plan for? Add hidden actors the maker missed.
- Three prompts for each map:
  - **Think about:** who is affected but never touches it?
  - **Engage with:** who has to say yes, or take part, for it to work?
  - **Plan for:** what happens over time — maintenance, wear, the next conference?
- Rewrite their HMW so the system is visible in the question.
- Add a staff/backstage lane under their journey map.
- *Materials: butcher paper, sticky notes, Sharpies, wall space. Timing: see Day Plan.*

**Observations tied to project clusters:**

- Maps out to Baltimore → no Baltimore identity in the lobby or menu.
- Wayfinding inside the hotel → "numerous turns and corridors with limited wayfinding cues"; parking elevators hard to locate; buildings joined by skyway to the convention center.
- Play / kids → guest working at the children's hospital (hotel partnership); live-sports projector put up because guests liked it, later taken down.

## 5. Tying to the Course Learning Outcomes (\~5 min)

- Prototyping & visualization → the margin map
- Ethnographic methods → reading backstage traces
- Actionable prompts → system-visible HMWs
- Team decisions → swapping maps
- Iterative prototyping & feedback loops → every assumption is a test

**Why this matters:** we're not using systems thinking as "just come up with big-picture ideas in a cool way." We're doing this, we're iterating — and we're even iterating the process — so the process helps us get to better solutions that aren't just about one side or the other. They try to account for multiple user journeys in a single question.

## 6. The Home Stretch — "Is What We Have Enough To…?" (\~10 min)

- **This week:** …understand the system around our prototype?
- **Next week:** …get useful feedback from non-designers?
- **Week after:** …change direction based on that feedback?
- **Then:** …make a persuasive argument?
- **Pitch week:** …show all seven outcomes, even without a finished object?
- Documentation proves the process: evidence → HMW → prototypes → what each taught → open questions.
- Suggested final slide: "What we assumed and couldn't verify."

**Working without another site visit** (no return to the Hilton until final presentations):

- **Stand-ins for hidden people:** classmates role-play housekeeping, sales, the city — and push back on each prototype.
