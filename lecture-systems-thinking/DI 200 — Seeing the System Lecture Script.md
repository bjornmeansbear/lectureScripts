## 1. The cart — 9:00

\[Title: Seeing the System\]

Today we're talking about systems and services. So we're going to jump backwards to the very first week: IDEO and the shopping cart.

In that video we get to see them do the design thinking process live for a news crew, and they apply it to the shopping cart. They take the user perspective of a few different kinds of shoppers, and that affects some tiny aspects of the cart. They're thinking about materials and maintainability.

But at the end of the day, what we watch them do is redesign the shopping cart. They don't really ask how shopping is different. It doesn't ultimately change shopping. It just changed the cart. We never zoom out very far.

\[Laura Kampf\]

Then we watched Laura Kampf take a broken shopping cart and turn it into a chair. She did get at this larger system. She's outside the world of the shopping carts. She's not at a store. She's out in the world, and she's always seeing shopping carts — broken, abandoned, left behind, or used for something else. That's a zoom out. We see the larger system the carts live within.

The world wears these things down. They're outside, in the weather. And she points out that even tiny things like stuck wheels often never get fixed, because it's more work than a store has time or employee effort for.

\[What's not been considered\]

So what didn't IDEO talk about? Theft — which frequently happens in Baltimore. Different heights of users. Repair. Rain, snow, heat, cold.

And there's something else. IDEO wasn't envisioning a world where carts get used by somebody else to buy multiple people's worth of groceries. Instacart. Uber Eats. That changes things even further. The system moved on around the object.

\[Shrinking cart — exercise\]

So if the cart is our center, we have to start thinking about how we zoom out and out and out, and see all the network of stuff around it. Take something as simple as a shopping cart. Think how quickly we can fill up the world. What's the first ring of stuff around the cart?

*(Let them call out. Fill the screen until the cart is tiny.)*

The cart is simple. And look at all this stuff we were able to think about. Our projects live in the lobby of a hotel. ✱ If a cart fills a wall, imagine the hotel.

## 2. Assume the hidden — 9:35

\[Thesis slide\]

This is the main thing to take away from today. Systems and services deal in these more abstract, hidden, invisible realms. We have to train our brains and our processes to see them, and then reveal them.

\[Web store\]

I used to build a lot of websites. Often I'd get an ask like, oh, we need a web store, or we want a blog. And that reveals all these other questions. Do you already have an inventory tracking system, or is the website going to be your inventory tracking? Do you have credit card processing? A client list? Does the website have to take all this over, or is it just the front end, with everything else already happening behind the scenes on services you already use?

We're frequently having to see what else attaches to the question you're being asked by a client.

\[Grad catalog\]

I used to do a lot of work for graduate admissions at MICA. Similar thing. Oh, we need a new graduate catalog. Okay — is it obvious what the difference is between the graduate programs, from looking at it versus reading it? Is a giant thick book of images and text actually the right way? Who else needs to be tied into editing and collecting the information? What could we show from an audio or visual perspective instead — and how is that still connectable in a print piece?

\[Crab mural\]

So let's zoom out on our Hilton project, to see these systems and reveal the opportunities for a service, not just a thing. The Hilton project started as kind of a running joke. It started as a call: hey, can you do a crab mural? We zoomed out and asked, well, what are they actually trying to do? We decided they're trying to get Baltimore into the lobby. Now we get to zoom out again from our ideas that answer that how might we question.

\[Placements\]

Remember wicked problems, and the perspective of the person analyzing the problem. Any client has their placement, which affects what they're asking for. Zooming out to see these interlinking systems and services — what are we actually trying to provide this person? — gives us other placements, so we can offer back a different pitch in response to what we get asked.

Every client will hand you an object. Your first move is to ask what system it lives in.

So: what actually needs to be involved in making any of these things work?

## 3. Definitions — 10:00

\[Splash: Defining terms\]

✱ You already know most of this intuitively. This is us putting definitions and terms to it, for us moving forward.

\[Service\]

What is a service? To me, a service is how we deliver value to someone over time.

Say there's a thing we want, like clean clothes. The service is what delivers the clean clothes. That could mean you buy a washing machine. It could mean you rent access to a washing machine. It could mean you hang your clothes outside and they get picked up and magically brought back clean. All you're looking for is the service of taking my dirty things and having them end up clean things for me to wear again.

How we do that is different for different people. And maybe selling every single human on earth a washing machine isn't the best way to actually deliver that service.

\[System\]

A service works only if you understand all the systems required to deliver that value to whoever you're delivering it to. So thinking about systems is thinking about all the interconnected elements around the thing you're trying to do. Sometimes you have to zoom all the way out to Spaceship Earth — Buckminster Fuller's idea — as the thing all of this is happening on and inside of.

Different fields call this different things. In product design or architecture, you might talk about lifecycle analysis: the systems that make a thing, and then allow it to be maintained. You can apply that to the materials of an object. You can also apply it to delivering an educational program in the lobby of a hotel. What are the systems around this that we have to account for?

\[Pause\]

Does this make sense so far? What's a service that maybe you hadn't thought of as a service before?

*(Pocket answers: the library, Spotify, the bus, MICA itself.)*

\[Service design\]

Service design is the whole experience and the operation behind it. We're designing those things — not letting them just happen accidentally. We're purposely thinking about the flow that takes what the person is looking for and delivers it to them at the right time, in the right place. That starts to sound lean-startup-y, and that's because capitalism loves this language.

\[Touchpoints\]

Service design means you have to think about touchpoints. Touchpoints are the moments the person who needs the service actually meets the service. How they start it. How they get asked what's going on when there's a problem. How it finishes. Those are all different touchpoints.

At the Hilton, the touchpoints of a guest entering the lobby are things like going through the revolving door, whether they have to talk to an agent at the front desk, signs that help them find their room or the restaurant, even how things smell and feel. These are what move people along at the pace and quality we want.

\[Same desk, different person\]

Change any one of these things and you've changed the service. One person not coming into work one day might affect it. This is why capitalists have gotten so interested in replacing people with machines — the idea that an algorithm does every step the same way every time, so you can dial in the service. But that gets us into a whole other conversation about adaptability. Often the things in a system that are most successful over time are the most flexible, the most able to adapt to change and spontaneity.

✱ And how a system adapts comes down to its feedback loops.

\[Systems design\]

So how do we design systems? Frankly, that isn't exactly right. It's more about how we design with or for systems. What are the rules, the flows, and the feedback loops that drive behavior and outcomes in the milieu we're working within? Often we can't control the externality. But we can control how things respond to it.

### Feedback loops — expanded after Nov 2 ✱

*The class wanted more here. This whole subsection is new — Claude's draft for Bjorn to put in his own words.*

\[Definition\]

A feedback loop is when the result of something comes back around and changes the thing that caused it. Output becomes input. The arrow circles back.

The test: follow the arrow all the way around. Does the thing keep growing — or does it settle down?

\[Two kinds — diagram: R and B\]

**Reinforcing loops — the snowball.** More leads to more. Or less leads to less.

- A busy restaurant. People trust a crowd, so a full restaurant draws more people, so it gets fuller. An empty one stays empty, even if the food is better.
- Desire paths. Someone cuts across the grass. The worn line invites the next person. Soon it's a path. Nobody designed it — the loop did.
- The cart: a sticky wheel, left in the lot, rusts, fewer good carts, the rest get used harder, more break.

**Balancing loops — the thermostat.** Something pushes back toward a target.

- A thermostat. Too cold, heat comes on. Warm enough, it shuts off.
- Your body. Too hot, you sweat. Cooler, you stop.
- The front desk. The line gets long, a second agent opens a window. Line shrinks, they go back to the back office.

Most lasting systems have both: a reinforcing loop that drives something, and a balancing loop that keeps it from running away. That's the limiter. Without one, you get mic feedback — that squeal when the microphone hears its own speaker.

\[Delays\]

Loops get tricky when the feedback is slow. The hotel shower: you turn the hot knob, nothing happens, so you crank it — then you're scalded. You crank it back — freezing. The delay makes you overshoot.

At a hotel: a bad stay today shows up as a bad review next week, and as fewer bookings next season. By the time anyone feels it, the cause is long gone.

\[The lobby loop — the one that matters for us\]

A lively lobby feels alive, so people linger, so it gets livelier. An empty lobby feels dead, so people pass through, so it stays empty. That's a reinforcing loop — and nearly every project in this room is trying to tip it.

\[Class question\]

In a hotel, what could throw how a guest checks in out of whack? External or internal.

*(Pocket: the conference rush. A session ends, 500 people want their rooms at once. The line gets long, people get cranky, cranky check-ins take longer, the line gets longer. What would break that loop? Others: a storm, a sold-out Orioles game, key cards down, two staff out sick.)*

\[Cart loop\]

Let's say we end up with a sticky wheel. Somebody brings the cart out to the lot, loads their stuff in, and because the wheel's stuck, they're done with that cart. They don't return it. It stays out in the lot, derelict, in the weather. It rusts. The wheel gets worse. Now there are fewer good carts. Each remaining one gets used harder — *(slow down)* — more likely to get a broken wheel or handle, more likely to get abandoned, or pushed behind the store by the store itself. Until it hits Laura Kampf's is-this-broken-enough-to-become-a-chair level.

What would break this loop? *(A return closer to the car. A quarter deposit, like Aldi. Someone whose job is fixing wheels. Each is a limiter.)*

\[Projector\]

At the Hilton, there was a cool sporting event, so they set up a projector in the lobby. They set it up in an ad hoc way, intentionally, so they could take it down and put it back up when necessary. That's a feedback loop too. ✱ They didn't design a permanent sports bar. They designed the ability to respond.

\[Your prototype drops into loops already running\]

✱ Your idea isn't landing in an empty room. A lost guest asks the front desk, the desk points the way. That loop already works. A good map feeds it — fewer questions, calmer desk. An out-of-date map gets ignored, and it quietly dies. What loop is already doing your job? Does your idea help it or fight it?

If we zoom out from the lobby: there's staff doing things, cleaning schedules, guests coming and going. Sometimes conferences, sometimes not. Sometimes a lot of people, sometimes few. Bad weather, nice weather. Doors locked or not. Somebody has a key, somebody doesn't. How do we start thinking about the systems that already exist, and the loops that might self-adapt — or break out of control? How do we at least document what we need to ask and find out, and use our abductive reasoning to account for them ahead of time?

The zoom out helps us see our prototype isn't just to test if one interaction works. What is the service we are trying to provide, and how is this participating in it usefully? Not just moving somebody one step forward for an unknown outcome.

\[Takeaway\]

You can design a service. You can only intervene in a system.

A service we can actually design. A system we have to consider — we respond to it, or intervene in it, through a feedback loop, or by accounting for an external one. We build in flexibility and adaptability, or a bridge over those things into our service. We can't necessarily design a system.

## 4. Service blueprint — 10:40

\[Draw the line\]

✱ A service blueprint is a diagram of a service over time. Across the top are the steps the guest takes. Down the side are the layers that make each step happen. Its key move is drawing a line between what the guest sees and everything that makes it happen. And Hilton legal literally drew that line for us: front of house only.

\[Layers\]

If we think about the hotel, and where things are seen and where they're invisible: there's the guest and what guests do. Then front stage — the staff and actions guests can actually see, in the spaces guests are allowed to be. Then the line of visibility. Below it, staff and staff work that guests never see. Even though we took a tour of most of the back, I still am not sure where the actual kitchen is. Then the other systems — contracts, the various owners — that support the hotel, that a guest never sees or experiences.

If all these things are working properly, the guest should never see or experience them.

In our context, unfortunately, we're stuck with our one visit of front-of-house-only information.

\[What we'd look for\]

In a perfect world, if we could observe more: where are the staff entries and exits used to service or refresh things? What carts and tools do people have? Where and how is stuff stored? When do things get cleaned, or swapped, and who does it? Who designs, prints, and collects conference badges? Is there on-site IT, or is it all Hilton corporate?

\[Ella's art table — draw on the board\]

Take one of your projects. Ella, your art class. A guest drops off their kid. The kid sits at the table, there's an instructor, there's the stuff. That's what a guest sees and interacts with. Behind the house: who's booking that instructor, who makes sure we have the stuff, where does it get stored when there's no drop-off? Bigger picture: what liability is required to manage kids while a parent is gone? Who schedules it? Who supervises those staff and approves things? Procurement?

Pretty quickly we can zoom out and see a much bigger system involved in delivering this service.

\[Two uses\]

You use a service blueprint two main ways. First, take an existing service and diagnose how it runs — by diagramming it, find fail points and feedback loops that aren't being accounted for. Second — the one that helps us in prototyping — propose a new service, and think ahead about where problems in the system, and feedback loops that can go awry, exist.

\[The blueprint's family\]

✱ Customer journey map: the blueprint's top row. Service blueprint: the journey plus everything under the line. Ecosystem map: everyone and everything around the service — our margin map. Business model canvas: a cousin — how the business makes money, not how the service runs.

\[Don't know? Mark it.\]

Making this diagram and not sure, or can't answer something? Mark it and keep moving. That reveals where we need to talk to somebody else, or pull in another thinker, designer, researcher. Who would actually be in charge of this? What desk manages it — front desk, HR? Then we loop people in to account for their view of the service.

✱ Every question mark is someone you need to talk to.

\[Under the line at the Hilton\]

Think of what we already know is invisible under the line. The building still has bonds to pay off; bondholders get paid back each year. Hilton Corporate manages the hotel — they don't own it. That means brand standards and fees that have nothing to do with what a guest interacts with, though they do affect us. And the city is under the line, because this is publicly owned. They want it to generate business for Baltimore, and convention center traffic.

✱ Every one of these has its own idea of what this lobby is for.

## 5. Margin map — 11:10

\[HMW first\]

Before we map: rewrite your how might we question around your prototype idea, with the lobby and the user as the frame.

\[Fill the margins\]

This is the cart exercise on your prototype. Just like we zoomed out on the cart, let's zoom out on each of your prototypes and ask what else is there.

A margin map is how we account for the ecology of systems around our thing. Your prototype goes small in the middle, and everyone and everything it depends on goes in the margins. I'm calling it a margin map because I like the alliteration. In the field you'll hear ecosystem map — because it visualizes the ecosystem around our prototype — or stakeholder map.

\[Observed / Guessed / Unknown\]

We mark everything three ways: observed — we saw it or heard it at the Hilton. Guessed — our best reasoning. Unknown — a question mark.

\[Example — Ella's table\]

Again, Ella, sorry, I'm using yours. The kid art table in the center, and the interconnected system around it that lets the service be delivered.

Observed: there are guests who want it quiet in the lobby, who maybe don't want a bunch of kids running around.

I first marked "parents are stuck in conference sessions and want to bring their kid" as observed. But I observed it in my own childhood, not at the Hilton. So here, it's really a guess.

Guessed: who cleans up the paint? Probably housekeeping. Who teaches the class? Art people from places we could reach out to. Does it survive the next conference — a one-off, or a checkbox Events offers when you schedule?

Unknown: what liability do we need to find out about, for kids? Who restocks supplies? Where do they get stored? Does event sales book it, or does it happen some other organic way?

\[Groups\]

Small groups of three — there are eleven of you, so one group of four. Write your prototype — your current presumed deliverable — in a square on your sheet. Then take turns: what's really near, and how far out can we zoom? Do that for each prototype. If prototypes share a question or a service, connect them to the same things — so we see how different services for one place connect to the same systems and feedback loops that run them, or derail them.

\[Debrief\]

What did you see? Did your group see the same things? Were you able to interconnect some of them? Does this help you see services and systems as layered things we're interacting with?

\[Rewrite the HMW\]

Now rewrite your how might we, with these other connections inside the question, so it helps us evolve, iterate, and improve the prototype.

✱ Before: "How might we help conference guests find their meeting rooms?" After: "…in a way the events staff can update between conferences?"

## 6. Afternoon + wrap — 12:45

\[Blueprint your prototype\]

Finish and connect your maps. Then blueprint your own prototype on sheet 2 — or the FigJam template. Ella's example stays up as a model. Mark your guesses. Write what desk would manage it. List your assumptions: what we assume, guessed or unknown, and who could tell us.

*(One-on-ones: where are you, where do you need to get, what's the next push.)*

\[One hidden actor\]

One sentence each, around the room: "The person or thing my brief never mentioned is…"

\[Is what we have enough to…?\]

Five weeks left. Each week asks: is what we have enough to understand the system around our prototype? To get useful feedback from non-designers? To change direction? To make a persuasive argument? To show all seven outcomes, even without a finished object?

We probably can't visit the site again until final presentations. So we conceptualize as much as we can, even if we can't get answers. Put a "What we assumed and couldn't verify" slide in your final deck.

✱ We're not using systems thinking as "come up with big ideas in a cool way." We're iterating — and iterating the process — toward solutions that account for multiple user journeys in a single question.

\[Meadows\]

This is reading about systems. Donella Meadows is the main person known for this research. "Leverage Points" was the starting place that her later book, *Thinking in Systems*, built around. Read it. Hopefully it helps you consider these things as you keep iterating your prototype forward. We'll talk about it a bit next week.

**Homework:** keep developing your prototype; bring the next iteration. Read Meadows. ✱ Bring one sentence: "My prototype pushes on \_\_\_. It could push on \_\_\_."

✱ Closing line: You can't see it, but you're still designing for it.
