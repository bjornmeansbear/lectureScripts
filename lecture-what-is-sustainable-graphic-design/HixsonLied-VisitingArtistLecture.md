## Two models of sustainability

I'm going to talk a lot about sustainability, so I thought we should have a couple of shared understandings before we move forward.

The typical way sustainability is presented is through this **triple bottom line** idea — that there's social sustainability, economic sustainability, and ecological sustainability, and if you are truly sustainable you're accounting for all of those things in your actions, so you end up in this sweet spot where they all overlap. This is a good practical thought model, but the downside is that you could be economically sustainable without worrying about social or ecological sustainability, or vice versa.

So an improved ideology is this idea that our **economic systems can only exist within social systems** — they don't exist apart from society — and society can't exist apart from nature. All of our choices become nature-based choices, and this thinking leads you to understand the interconnectedness amongst all things a little bit better.

## The Sustainabilitist Principles

I'm going to start with a piece of work that hopefully illustrates some ideas I'll try to explain more afterwards.

This project is called the **Sustainabilitist Principles**, and it started out as me sitting at my desk in grad school looking at the collection of things I had been reading and trying to make sense of — saying that no sane person has enough time to actually go through all this stuff. What could be done to make it clearer, or easier, or more time-friendly to access the information within these things I was finding so useful?

That started out as mapping interconnections between things, looking for shared ideas or principles. It turned into a little zine where I would write my own essays trying to illustrate a principle of sustainable design, and then show the reader where I had taken that from — so you could get a little sample of the idea and then know where to look for more information if you were intrigued.

As I was completing this, I came to a place where I looked at these and said: well, all I did was take photos of the books as they already were on my desk. Why can't I just use the books themselves in some way to do this? That became the challenge for myself, and come the exhibition we had in grad school, that was what I figured out how to do — present this as the objects themselves.

I would still say that this is a **poster**. It does what a poster would do: it is posted in a public place and has clear information it's trying to describe to you. But it was this other way to think about what a graphic design object might be in a gallery space, so it lives in a weird in-between of a sculpture and a poster.

The books themselves are the books. They are interconnected to aphoristic rewritings of the longer essays I had written. The production of the piece was meant to come together in the gallery and then dissipate back to the constituent parts after the exhibition was over — and this led to novel formal solutions:

- The strings that connect them I left as long as possible, so they would have the most reuse potential afterwards.
- The principles themselves were screen printed on found novels from a book share, so I could return the books once the exhibition was over.
- They were printed on the front matter or title pages, so the legibility of the book wasn't really affected.

This felt like a really good culmination of my time in grad school, where I'd been making work meant to be a response to the question *what does sustainable graphic design look like?* Through these investigations I'd had some results that fit into categories:

1. **It looks the same** — it's just good design, but with different materials.
2. **It looks eco-friendly** — it wears its environmentalism on its sleeve, via being intentionally reused, or on craft paper, or something like that.
3. **It's innovative** — embracing new technologies or new ideas.
4. **It doesn't exist.**

That last category I was playing around with — maybe sustainable design just doesn't exist. At first that was because I was feeling pessimistic: all these things I was making were just more waste. I was unmaking the world around me to make ephemeral garbage, to be frank. "It doesn't exist" was initially a response that I maybe *can't* make sustainable design, for these reasons.

But what it came to be was more of a different kind of prompt: the opportunity for making a designed object that has no footprint, or is fully embedded in the systems which create it, seemed like a different way to think about the making process to begin with.

## Defining it: flourishing

Let's jump into trying to define what sustainable graphic design might mean, if we're trying to embrace these kinds of answers.

If you're interested in sustainability I highly recommend the book ***Flourishing*, by John Ehrenfeld and Andrew Hoffman**. In it they try to define a different philosophy for understanding sustainability as it relates to business and culture. The key thing they point out is that **if you want to be sustainable, you have to first say what you want to sustain** — because "sustainability" as a word is always in connection to something else. You sustain *something*, and you have to define what that something is to move forward effectively.

You see this in many of the definitions floating around. This is probably the most commonly referenced one, from the **Brundtland Commission**, a UN-led body, in 1987:

> Sustainable development [is] development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

And then you see variations of this from all the different places you might look for help — equity over time, leaving things better than you found them, improving human life while not limiting other systems. They all relate to living within limits, the golden rule extended forward, thinking about the future when you make decisions in the present. Our economic and social constructs exist within a larger construct of nature, and there are ramifications in the future to things that happen in the present.

Those ideas are present in pretty much any definition for sustainability that you'll find — but **none of them are clear about telling you what they want to sustain.** Ehrenfeld tries to help us think about that, and he tells us he wants to sustain that *people, in all of their life, should be able to flourish.* He goes into great length telling you what flourishing really means, but he has a fairly clear description that many other definitions don't. The Brundtland report is focused on sustainable *development*, which includes human infrastructure growth — and there are a lot of detractors who would say that maybe isn't actually a sustainable goal.

Another designer with similar thinking is **Bruce Mau**. He has a project he's been working on for several years called **Massive Change**, and in it they describe *the welfare of all life as a practical objective* as the goal of the project — which fits nicely with Ehrenfeld's all-life-flourishing description.

So with those ideas applied to sustainable graphic designing:

> **Graphic design that supports all life flourishing.**
>
> **Graphic design for the welfare of all life.**

That's how I would like to describe sustainable graphic design. Moving forward, these are the ideas I'm working with when I try to explain what's going on in these other topics. *Design for the welfare of all life* — that's my symbol for it.

> **Q (Sam Bendix):** Would you say the most successful sustainable graphic design would need to reach into and collaborate with other disciplines, or can it exist solely through graphic design?
>
> **A:** That's a great question. It's an "it depends" answer — but definitely, if we think about the kinds of issues that need to be addressed in the context of all life flourishing, they're often complex issues that no one person is really capable of understanding or doing something about on their own. So yes, many solutions would require some kind of cross-discipline collaboration. That might be with a non-profit group that specifically works in that area; that might be with some kind of material scientist who actually knows more about what you're trying to make. Being able to collaborate with somebody, or with groups, is an imperative moving forward for creative form-makers generally.

## If the goal changes, the aesthetics change

If we define sustainable graphic design as design for the welfare of all life, that is a **different goal** than what regular graphic design today has for itself.

If we think about our fairly contemporary Western art-historical systems: when goals change, and when tools and ideologies shift, that usually corresponds with the aesthetics of that time or community adapting and shifting as well. This is where I get interested in the aesthetics of things — if I have a different practical objective for my designs, where does that lead me from a decision-making process on what I'm selecting, or how?

Here's a small example: how you might pick a font in this context. If we're trying to think sustainably about typography choice, then maybe **using less ink** is a useful way to justify which typeface to pick. But if we're already trying to use less ink, we could probably use **less space** — so if we have a slightly condensed typeface that also uses less ink, we've saved room and ink on our page. You can keep taking this to more and more extreme lengths until you perhaps end up somewhere ludicrous.

But it's a different way to help make a choice that isn't arbitrary anymore. Saying "what typeface should I pick" is like saying "what style of house do you want to live in" — it's just taste, usually. Whatever you choose to have as your taste is the answer. Having some other objective can be a way to think about it: if the thinking behind this is different, this might result in different aesthetics. **And that is okay.**

> **Q (Jen Landis):** Do you research font foundries on their sustainability within the company?
>
> **A:** Depending on the type designer, they're more or less transparent about this. I'm blanking on specific names on the spot, but for example there was a typeface that came out a couple of years ago called **Ryman Eco** — a big decorative serif, intentionally designed to save ink. It has all these inline elements knocked out of it, so the typeface looks filled when printed small, but it uses about one third of the ink because of all these missing components of the filled areas of the letterform. Used big, it becomes a cool decorative element.
>
> Where you also see this is in the digital sphere. When you're using web fonts, there's all kinds of things you can do from a performance perspective to make your website load faster that are also useful from an ecological perspective — the less you have to download to load the page, the less energy it takes to serve it. If you're speccing web fonts, you can use a limited subset of the characters and intentionally make a lower-energy version of your website. So there are ways people are thinking about this in both the physical and digital sphere.

## Critical design

If design for the welfare of all life is different from everyday designing, perhaps it leads us to some other interesting territory.

**Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby** are industrial designers who think critically about the fact that design in and of itself is an **ideological act** — it makes manifest a worldview. You turn a worldview into an object when you make a piece of design. Most of the objects around us share a pretty similar worldview of modern techno-capitalism right now, and if we're not seeing that lead to all life flourishing, then perhaps we need to be led to different kinds of objects.

These other objects they refer to as **critical design** — a design object that provides a critique of the prevailing situation. Dunne and Raby think design is particularly useful for this because it's a medium people are much more familiar with than, say, art, and it's much more concrete than music or poetry at getting across a critical vision.

So how might we use our sustainable graphic designing to provide this critique of existing social, cultural, technical, and economic things?

Two quick small-scale examples — lowercase-c critical:

- I invited some designers to one of my classes a couple of years ago to talk about how they were participating in climate activism as designers. When I made posters for their talk, I used the previous prints from the recycling bin in the Risograph room to print all of their posters on — plus some really old newsprint left over from something. All found paper that was already in the print shop.
- I used to have a **stamp** with my information on it, and rather than hand out business cards I'd stamp it on whatever paper people had with them, including other people's business cards. To me that was a really critical design gesture — pointing out the frivolity and uselessness of these printed things we were handing around all the time. Business cards used to be the thing people went crazy on when designing them, so they're doubly frivolous in their old incarnations. (I don't know if I've given anyone a business card in eight years.)

## Green Acres — where I didn't manage it

Here's a project where I can critique some of these issues of materiality and decision-making, partially because I think this one doesn't do them the best.

**Green Acres** was a catalog — an addendum book to an exhibition at the Contemporary Arts Center in Cincinnati, Ohio. The works in the exhibition were all farming-related: artists using farming as their practice. Sometimes that manifested as a sculptural object, and sometimes it was literally just a farmer with a potato field, with some other communicating meant to be happening about why an artist would do this. A really interesting array of ecologically inventive farm-practice-based work.

I thought: wouldn't it be cool to make a really sustainable book to show that off? My initial thinking was that these are art farms, in contrast to large-scale farming — so how can I visualize that contrast in the book?

- The section breaks used Midwestern aerial farm photography to create the idea that each section contrasts this in a different way.
- Artistic elements pulled from the logotype referenced farm field furrows, showing up all over the book as decorative elements.
- The actual grid design of the page was based on township squares — the tiny farm squares in the aerial photos were part of the layout of the pages.

All of that was meant to be a gesture of the artistic versus the industrial. That seemed like a really great idea to me at the time, but it turned out to be **so abstract that nobody really gets it.** No one looks at this book and says "oh man, I understand the difference between these things now because of this page grid." That's a pretty inert way to be critical of something.

What really ended up being the sustainable aspect of the book was that we used a **print-on-demand publisher**, so we could print very few copies at a time — so there weren't unnecessarily large quantities of books being printed, shipped, stored, not sold, and wasted. It was printed on recycled paper with this on-demand printer, and that ended up being the main aspect, once my cleverness wasn't apparent enough in the criticality of the thing.

> **Q (Sam Bendix):** How do you balance the desire to design sustainably with the reality of having to design things a certain way to fit a brief or a client's vision?
>
> **A:** My goal, like I said at the beginning, is to make every project an expression of myself in some way. Sometimes the way you do that is you just **don't tell** the people that you're doing the things — because if it doesn't cost them more, or change what you're delivering, they actually don't care what you're doing to deliver them the object they requested.
>
> That doesn't always work. Sometimes the brief is so constrained, or the budget so tight, or the specifications already decided, that you just do it as they requested. Sometimes it's frustrating — you ask as many questions as you can, try to change as many things as you can, and when it turns out it's not going to make any difference, you say okay, well, I tried, this is done. Maybe I need to not work with this person again, or maybe next time I can convince them to do something more because I can approach them ahead of time.
>
> That said, in my experience, when the choices you want to make have very little ramification to a client's desires, they're usually pretty open and amenable. You can recommend paper that you think looks good and that's also made of alternative fiber sources, or is more recycled, or isn't bleached — and you can pitch it to them as being *better paper*. You don't necessarily have to pitch it as being more climate-friendly. I know that's an adversarial perspective, although increasingly I find most clients are interested in that, because they see it as an opportunity to position themselves differently with their own clients or constituents.

## Modernism as the default measure of "good"

Something I talk about a lot with colleagues: as graphic designers, we tend to see the instructions for what good graphic designing is as really tied to the **formalist principles of good modernist design**. Modernism is basically synonymous with the principles of good graphic design.

If sustainable designing is ideologically different from everyday designing, perhaps the criteria of goodness that comes from modernist principles doesn't sufficiently cover what goodness might be in sustainable designing. Oftentimes when you say "design" you really mean *modernist design* — every single company rebranding right now is using humanist geometric sans-serif typography. It's all just modernism everywhere.

That isn't necessarily problematic, except that if it's the only criteria we have for measuring good formal fit, it's maybe missing opportunities, or negatively impacting kinds of visual solutions that might be better for the welfare of all life. So maybe there are other ways to think about beauty or good formalism that come not from an aging artistic style, but from some other value system or structure.

## Beauty as a reflection of values

In researching this I came across a quote by the philosopher **Alain de Botton**: if you find a piece of design beautiful, it's because it has **your values reflected in it**.

Beauty as a reflection of your values is a nice idea, and his quote also talks about flourishing — which made me think about Ehrenfeld's definition of all humans and all other life flourishing as what sustainability is. So: if the value critical to our flourishing is all other life, then *that's* what is now beautiful design in de Botton's terms.

**Sustainable design is beautiful design.** And the interesting counterpoint: the non-sustainable becomes *ugly* design, however you want to define that.

So sustainable — or beautiful — design's form should declare that humans and all life should flourish. What's nice about that kind of definition for an aesthetic direction is that it doesn't try to imply what the thing should *look* like. It implies what the thing **says**, what it symbolizes to you. It's a reflection of your values, not of your aesthetic taste — and your value system might be much broader than your aesthetic taste system.

The issue it brings up, because all these things always bring up other issues, is that beauty isn't fixed. Different people have different ideas of what is beautiful, and this is not a new idea — Western history is full of people saying this in different ways.

## Signs signaling sustainability

Some examples from the world at large of communication design — we could call these graphic design, although most are physical things — that try to signal the welfare of all life. They're trying to semiotically tell you something about how we could improve things, and they have their own unique forms that I think are beautiful, but which might not be the stereotypical idea of a beautiful solution.

**1. The vapor ring exhaust stack (Bjarke Ingels Group, Copenhagen).** A proposed idea that unfortunately wasn't able to be made, for a bunch of complicated reasons — mainly because the person who was going to help do it got arrested in a scandal in Denmark. It was part of the larger waste-to-energy plant complex where the only thing emitted by the exhaust stack is CO2 and water vapor. It's so clean they've turned it into a park: in winter you can ski down it, in summer it's hiking trails. The idea was that the stack would puff a **vapor ring every time one ton of CO2** was collected — a way to show people in Copenhagen what a ton of CO2 looks like. How do we make this immaterial thing material, so we can understand a little more about climate change and our world's systems?

**2. Reverberation crosswalks (Graham Coreil-Allen, Baltimore).** A friend of mine working on a series of brightly painted crosswalks — paint on cement and asphalt, so to me this is a form of graphic designing. They make it very clear that this is a crosswalk, where you should cross, and that if you're a driver you need to pay attention because pedestrians will be here. Each one is one tiny step closer to a more walkable, more flourishing city. A really beautiful set of gestures.

**3. A solar-powered website.** A kind of critical design that very clearly shows how things might intentionally look different when they're trying to save energy rather than use up as much as possible. All the images are bitmapped, which lets them be high resolution but very low file size, and also allows them to be colorized in different ways so that in different contexts they can control what things look like very intentionally. Making the lowest-power website that still had intentional designing became the key constraint for how it looks and works.

**4. The DC High Water Mark Project (Curry Hackett, Washington DC).** Sculptural stanchions placed in the 100-year floodplain areas in and around DC, marking both where floods have actually risen to and where floods are **predicted** to rise to. The really high black one is a danger zone for the future; the more plentiful lower ones have actually happened before. You're in this place and you can see how high the water would be, without the flood needing to happen. A beautiful gesture, a beautiful kind of sculpture — but partially beautiful because it's trying to say something about us flourishing with our environment in a useful way.

## Conclusion

I started out this trajectory really caught up with the actual formal aesthetics of the work I was making. I was worried about whether it mattered if it looked the same or looked eco-friendly.

As my work and my thinking have progressed, I've gotten to a place where I'm less concerned with finding external formal fit — formal goodness that comes from somebody else's external idea — and more concerned with thinking about **what's the context or the content of this project, and how does that lead you to the right aesthetic?**

Each project might be different. But if each project is trying to embrace the flourishing of all life, then it will be a beautiful, good project.

## Closing Q&A

> **Q (Sue Gardner):** Could you comment on the idea of greenwashing type design — design that makes something horrible or unsustainable look green and sustainable?
>
> **A:** That's one of the things that led me to want to find some other answers than purely formal ones to what sustainable things should look like. Because if there is only a single aesthetic trope that indicates sustainability, then it can easily be used **without the ideology that led to it.** That's where you end up with greenwashed things — things that use a visual trope to signal that there's something environmentally friendly or socially positive about them, but which are otherwise manufactured or produced using all of the same negative systems that led us to these problems in the first place.
>
> This was especially prevalent from about 2006 through a few years ago. There were all these computer companies — I know ASUS was making bamboo enclosures for some of their laptops and desktops. That was just so humorous to me: the idea that by slapping a little bamboo shell around this computer, you magically made it not a horrible resource, material, and workplace disaster.

> **Q (Pierce):** Considering many of our electronics are made from labor and materials that are not sustainably sourced, can any design that uses computers as a tool actually be considered sustainable?
>
> **A:** Oh my gosh, Pierce. That was one of the reasons I ended up with my "sustainable design can't exist" quandary — all of the things I take advantage of to make my work, personal or client, mainly come from systems that have not accounted for the social or natural consequences of their existence.
>
> There are some paths for you, though, if you're interested in philosophically dealing with that and turning it into your actual practice. I am Zooming to you all from my Apple laptop, unfortunately — but I also have an old Lenovo ThinkPad that, if I were trying to run Windows on it, would be useless for running contemporary Windows, but for running contemporary Linux is totally usable still. So one way I'm trying to deal with this is by using alternative tools and the opportunity to **reuse existing machinery** — so we don't have to make as many new computers, and can use systems that work on the computer for much longer, or revive computers that otherwise aren't capable of working.
>
> That said, there's a lot of progress that would need to happen, particularly around worker situations, to really have a good answer for you. I do have some extreme colleagues who basically don't use computers — they're slightly socialist, so they borrow somebody else's computer when they really have to. There are people attempting to not use some of these things at all if possible, for those reasons.

> **Comment (Adria):** We really need an alternative to our systems, which graphic design is one part of.
>
> **A:** The field of graphic design is trying to figure out what to do about this. There's increasingly this interest in an idea called **social design** — you take the tools of graphic designing that you'd be turning toward traditional business clients, and instead think about how you can work with people in the localities around you to solve problems that real people are actually having in their community, or with non-profit groups working directly with a constituency to improve their situations. There's a growing body of designers turning their practice to those kinds of interventions.
>
> To me that's part of how you can use art and design yourself — you can make things that, in Dunne and Raby's critical design idea, try to show other opportunities for us to participate in.

## Coda: the futures cone

I need three minutes to do one more thing.

The **futures cone** — this comes from an academic named [Joseph] Voros, who talks about projected futures a lot. If we think about the future not being fixed but being a set of possibles:

We're in the present, over here in the now. If we do nothing, we're on a linear line into the future. But if we *do* something, we can change what kind of future we end up in — and each time we shift further away from just-what-will-happen-if-we-do-nothing, more and more other options open up for how culture and society and life can exist.

It's really easy to see how this works with a piece of technology. Go back 20 years and say "I can't wait to get an iPhone" — the iPhone would be way over here in a potential future, not in an actual future. Then it takes a few years to reach that, and it becomes the baseline projected future. For sure we're going to have iPhones moving forward, at least for a little bit of time. That's not a crazy idea, it's just what we all participate with every day now.

So some other social system, or other kinds of value-based judgments that value natural systems over manufactured economic systems, **are possible** — if we can just point people in that direction. And then it just becomes the default future after a while. Aspirationally, that's what happens.
