## An Introduction: Stories That Belong to Everyone

Stories about "The Ash Lad" have been retold for centuries in what we now consider Scandinavia. Asbjørnsen and Moe didn't invent him. In the 1840s they wrote down what Norwegians were already telling each other, and their version is one that has stuck. That's the arrangement cultural production abided to. Stories moved around, changed in the telling, and belonged to whoever was telling them. Anyone that heard a story and liked it, would retell it themselves. Copyright is the recent thing here, not the folktale.

## One Version: Mickey, a Troll, and Some Victorian Clip Art

This workshop is a semiotic experiment. It tests how meaning shifts when images get recontextualized. Here's the pile printed in this booklet. Three sources, deliberately narrow:

1.	Mickey and Pete, from Steamboat Willie. The earliest Mickey Mouse, public domain since 2024.
2.	Victorian etchings. Nineteenth-century clip art, pulled out of context.
3.	A rewritten version of The Ash Lad Who Had an Eating Match with a Troll. A Norwegian folktale, commonly retold, built on trickery.

Any sources work, as long as everyone has the same ones, and they're freely shareable.

## The Challenge

Build a zine from the provided material only. No drawing, no outside images. Cut, paste, reinterpret.

Four approaches were offered as starting points:

- Faithful Adaptation: retell The Ash Lad as it is, using only the available imagery.
- Reinterpretation: shift the characters, setting, or themes while keeping the tale's structure.
- Surreal or Abstract Transformation: alter the story's meaning through juxtaposition.
- Subversive Remix: use irony, humor, or modern reference to comment on the story.

## Instructions: The Public Domain Remix Zine

### A Cut-and-Paste Remix
Take existing imagery. Cut it, paste it, reassemble it into something new.

### A Semiotic Experiment
Can you make random images mean something they were never meant to mean? Start with a set of unrelated images. Assemble them into a sequence. Add or remove elements to shift the meaning. Use text or composition to guide interpretation.

### Copyright and the Public Domain
Steamboat Willie is public domain — remix Mickey in a new context, no permission needed. The source material is public domain, but the zine you build from it is new work, and it's yours. License it CC BY-SA: anyone can reuse and adapt it, as long as they credit you and share their version under the same terms.

### Publish and Share
Scan or photocopy the finished zine. Distribute it, swap it, share it digitally. Someone else may remix it next — what would they do with your images?

Disney fought to keep the original protected for most of that century. Lawmakers extended copyright to life of the author plus 50 years in 1976, then to 70 years in 1998 — 95 years total before this version finally lapsed.

## Some Explanations?

### © Copyright?

Copyright grants creators exclusive rights to their work — books, music, film, art, zines — for a fixed term, currently the life of the author plus 70 years in the US. After that, the work enters the public domain, free for anyone to use.

This booklet works that boundary. Every image in it came off the pile printed on page [p.6] — Steamboat Willie stills and Victorian clip art, all public domain, cut apart and reassembled. Making something new out of public domain material produces a new copyrighted work the moment it exists, owned by whoever made it. I licensed this one CC BY-SA instead of keeping all rights. The pile it came from gets something back.

### © Creative Commons?

Creative Commons licenses sit between full copyright and the public domain — "some rights reserved" instead of "all rights reserved." A creator keeps ownership but pre-clears specific uses. CC0 releases work into the public domain outright; other CC licenses require attribution, or restrict commercial use, or both.

This booklet uses CC BY-SA: reuse and remix freely, credit the source, license your version the same way. Anyone who builds on it has to leave the door open behind them too.

### What Is the Public Domain?

Works enter the public domain when copyright expires — 95 years after publication, in Steamboat Willie's case — or when a creator dedicates them to the public outright, or because they were never copyrighted at all, which is true of most old folktales. Once there, no permission is required to use, remix, or share them.

This workshop worked entirely inside that space. The story, the Mickey stills, the Victorian clip art — none of it needed permission. New culture built directly on what came before, in full view.

## The Ash Lad that had an eating match with a Troll

There once was a farmer who had three sons — Per; Pål; and Espen, the Ash Lad. Espen was called the Ash Lad as he was smaller than his brothers, and so while they were out in the fields with their father, he would stay with his mother tending the fire, poking in the ashes. What he lacked in physical strength (Per and Pål were big strong farm boys) he made up for with quick thinking and cleverness.

The farmer and his wife were growing old, and after a poor harvest, they were struggling to make ends meet. To the farm belonged a vast forest, and the farmer decided his sons must chop wood to sell and pay off their debts.

The eldest, Per, was sent first. His mother packed him a lunch of bread and cheese, and his father handed him an axe, telling him to find the tallest trees deep in the forest. Per found a good spot of tall trees around a sunny clearing, but as soon as he started chopping, a burly troll appeared.

“If you’re chopping in my forest, I’ll eat you!” growled the troll.

Terrified, Per dropped his axe and, leaving his backpack behind, bolted home. Out of breath, he stammered out the tale. His father shook his head in disappointment. “When I was young, no troll scared me off!”

The next day, Pål tried his luck. Armed with a second axe and another backpack of lunch, he went into the forest. But just like his brother, he hadn’t chopped more than a single blow when the troll appeared, roaring the same threat. Pål, too, abandoned his axe and pack and ran home.

Now it was Espen’s turn. His brothers scoffed at him. “You? You can barely lift an axe!” But Espen insisted. He asked his mother for the biggest lunch she could make, though all she had left was a small pot of fresh curdled cheese. His father handed him a worn knife and reminded him to fetch the axes and the packs his brothers had left behind.

Espen ventured into the forest, finding the clearing with the abandoned tools and packs. He had barely started chopping when the troll appeared.

“If you’re chopping in my forest, I’ll eat you!” roared the troll.

Espen calmly reached into his backpack, pulled out the curdled cheese, and squeezed it until whey dripped out. “Leave me alone, or I’ll squeeze your blood from your bones like water from this stone!”

The troll’s eyes widened in fear. “Oh no! Please spare me! I’ll help you chop!”

The troll proved a skilled lumberjack, and by evening, they had felled and stacked a great pile of wood. As the sun set, the troll said, “It’s late — come to my cave for supper. It’s very close by.”

Espen agreed and rode on the troll’s shoulder to the troll’s cave. Inside, the troll handed him two massive iron buckets to fetch water for porridge. Espen couldn’t even budge them but quickly thought of a plan.

“These thimbles are too small,” he said. “I’ll just bring your whole well instead!”
	
“No, no, don’t take my well!” cried the troll. “I’ll fetch the water. You make the fire.”

While the troll fetched water, Espen explored the cave. He found heaps of gold and silver hidden in every corner. When the troll returned, he began cooking the most gigantic pot of porridge the Ash Lad had ever seen. Espen had an idea.

“If it’s all the same to you,” said Espen, “let’s have an eating match!”

The troll agreed, certain he could out-eat the boy. Espen, however, had strapped his backpack under his shirt. As they ate, he secretly spooned porridge into it. When the bag was full, he cut it open with his knife.

The troll stared, confused. “What are you doing?”

“It’s simple,” said Espen. “When I get too full, I cut open my stomach to make more room.”

The troll hesitated, but determined not to lose, he grabbed the knife and sliced his belly open. With a final scream, he collapsed.

Espen loaded his brother’s two remaining backpacks with as much gold and silver as he could carry and returned home. His cleverness had not only paid off the family’s debts but ensured they lived in comfort ever after. 

The End.
