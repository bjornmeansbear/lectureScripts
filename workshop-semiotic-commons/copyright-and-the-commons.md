# Copyright, the Public Domain, and the Commons

*A primer for running this workshop — or any workshop like it — without me in the room.*

---

## Two Myths

**The Myth of the Author**

In the land of creativity lived the authors — magical beings who could pull original creations down out of the aether and give them form. Authors protected their creations by locking them away in towers, like Rapunzel. Only through extreme offering and sacrifice would they grant permission to access what they’d made.

Authors were such powerful magicians that even death didn’t immediately break the spell. It took seventy years for the magic to fade, the towers to crumble, and the creations to finally enter the world. Authors, naturally, kept looking for ways to extend the spell and keep their creations forever.

**The Myth of the Commons**

In the beginning, culture was free. The land brimmed with possibility, and there existed a place called the commons.

The commons was no ordinary place. Within it, all knowledge, all resources, all innovation flowed freely, unencumbered by walls or gates or protections of any kind. Anyone could take what they needed, build on what they found, and give their creations back so others could do the same. Harmony and abundance reigned. There was no mine, no yours — only ours.

Both stories capture a binary: things are either protected, or free for anyone to use. That binary is fake. In practice, making things happens in the gray area in between.

## Copyright, Briefly

Copyright started in England in 1710. The word “author” meant someone who writes, but it now covers anyone who makes anything — a photograph, a song, a graphic design.

The foundational agreement is the Berne Convention, signed in 1886 by the US and a handful of European countries, now managed by the UN and adopted by nearly every country in the world. Berne says that the moment you make something, you own the copyright to it. No registration, no paperwork. You’re the only one who can decide whether anyone else gets to copy it.

“Copy” means more than it used to. Turning a book into a film counts. Using someone else’s characters, even in a different story, counts. Replicating a drawing, even if you re-drew the drawing, counts. Downloading a file potentially counts. The last time the US meaningfully updated copyright law was 1998 — before almost anyone used the internet to send anything to anyone, which is part of why copyright and the internet still don’t fit together well.

Copyright lasts a while. For an individual work, it’s the life of the maker plus seventy years. A corporation’s copyright — since a corporation doesn’t die the way a person does — runs 95 years from publication instead.

## Idea vs. Expression: The Chairness Problem

Copyright protects the expression of an idea, not the idea itself. You can’t copyright “a chair.” You can only copyright the specific form you give to the idea of a chair.

Four ways of solving chairness, each a unique expression protectable by copyright: the Eames molded plastic side chair. Philippe Starck’s Louis Ghost Chair, referencing the Louis XVI chair — a seventeenth-century design, public domain, usable by anyone — turned back into a new, copyrighted expression of its own. The Oka Terra Chair, by designers in Baltimore. A Gerrit Rietveld chair from 1923, a design that’s now technically public domain, except the photograph of it isn’t: I didn’t check to see if the photographer ever released those rights!

Each of these was copyrighted at some point. None of it stops anyone from making more chairs — just not these specific ones. The Rietveld chair is the exception: it’s old enough that its seventy years ran out. It’s public domain now — copy it freely. The rest, well, there are instances where entire cargo containers worth of Eames or Hans Wagner replica chairs are destroyed in European ports to protect copyright on works like this.

The idea of chairness stays open forever. No one can copyright the *concept* of a chair, four legs and a seat, the posture of sitting. What *is* copyrighted is a specific expression of that idea — an actual chair, but also a drawing of a chair, a painting of one, a photograph. Van Gogh’s Chair is just another expression of chairness, no different in kind from Starck’s remix of the Louis XVI chair: a new form given to an old idea. The moment you give chairness a new form, you’ve made something new, and that new thing gets its own copyright — even when everything it draws on is already free.

## What You’re Allowed to Do Anyway: Fair Use

Some things can’t be copyrighted at all. Facts. Math. Basic scientific data. The ingredient list of a recipe — though the prose explaining how to make it, or photos of the process, can be. That’s why the same recipe shows up worded differently across a hundred cookbooks.

Beyond that, fair use covers reproduction that copyright law technically prohibits but that convention has agreed is fine: quoting in an essay, reusing a small clip of something in news or documentary work, in service of a larger story you’re telling rather than as a substitute for the original. Parody is fair use too, if a court agrees it’s parody and not just a copy.

Fair use isn’t a permission you get in advance. It’s a defense you argue after you’ve already been sued.

Two cases show how narrow that defense is. Shepard Fairey’s Obama “Hope” poster was based directly on a photographer’s campaign photo. A court found that a drawing, even a stylized one, wasn’t transformed enough to count as fair use — Fairey lost. The Andy Warhol Foundation licensed a Prince photograph for four specific colorways of a print. When the Foundation later published a fifth colorway they hadn’t licensed, the photographer sued, and a court ruled that changing the color still counted as making a new copy — not a new use of an old one.

## The Public Domain, and Its Enclosure Problem

The flip side of copyright is the public domain — sometimes called the commons. Once a work crosses out of copyright, nobody has to ask permission to copy, adapt, or build on it. That’s where Pride and Prejudice and Zombies comes from, and why there’s always a new Sherlock Holmes show — Holmes and Watson are public domain characters, free to be reinvented endlessly.

Works enter the public domain other ways too. Anything the US government makes is public domain immediately, unless it’s classified — which is why Depression-era photographs of Dust Bowl migrants, taken by federal photographers, are so widely reused: no permission needed. The most-used photograph on Earth is probably the “Blue Marble,” the first full image of the planet from space, taken by the Apollo 17 crew in 1968. It’s a NASA photo. It became public domain in 1976, and it’s been on every kind of cover and slide deck since.

Here’s the strange part: using public domain material to make something new creates a new copyright, on the new thing. That’s how Disney’s Snow White works. The story is from the Brothers Grimm, published in 1812 — public domain by the time Disney got to it in 1937. But Disney’s specific version — the character designs, the dwarves’ names — is not public domain. It’s protected until 2032, after the Sonny Bono Act extended corporate copyright from 50 to 95 years, an extension Disney lobbied hard for. You can tell the story of Snow White however you want. You can’t use anything specific to Disney’s telling of it.

This is where the public domain gets unfair. Use it to make something new, and you re-enclose part of it — your version becomes protected, even while the source stays free. Since most people know the Disney Snow White, not the Grimm one, the version people actually want to reference is the one they can’t touch. Most of how humans have always made culture involves copying, changing, and remixing what came before. Copyright law works against exactly that.

This isn’t just a design complaint. The economist Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize studying real commons — shared fisheries, water systems, forests — and found that the standard warning, “the tragedy of the commons,” gets the diagnosis backward. Commons don’t fail because they’re shared. They fail when the self-governing rules that make sharing work get ignored by someone playing a different game. Re-enclosure is that kind of rule-breaking: taking from the shared pool and refusing to put anything back.

## Building Your Own Commons: Open Source and Creative Commons

None of this is new. Stewart Brand’s *How Buildings Learn* describes vernacular architecture working this way for centuries — a neighbor figures out how to add a shed that keeps the water out, and the solution spreads down the block. Software was the first place this got formalized into an actual license.

Richard Stallman was working at MIT’s AI lab in the early 1980s, when researchers still freely traded code the way you’d hand someone a tool. When Xerox stopped letting him access the source code for a printer he’d been troubleshooting, he flew off the handle, and decided to build free versions of everything instead — starting with an operating system he called GNU (GNU’s Not Unix). His GNU Public License is “viral”: use GNU-licensed code in your software, and your software has to be shared under the same terms too.

A looser version came later. Eric Raymond watched Linus Torvalds and a scattered group of volunteers build Linux faster than any closed company was building anything, sharing code over the internet with no central plan. He wrote an essay about it, “The Cathedral and the Bazaar” — top-down control versus everyone solving their own piece and sharing the result — and worried that a viral license like GNU’s would scare off businesses that wanted to build on open code without giving away what they built. The MIT license he helped popularize just asks for credit — one step short of the public domain. It’s why most commercial software today, including tools built by companies like Adobe, quietly runs on open-source code its makers never have to share back.

Creative Commons brings the same idea to writing, images, and design. It was founded by the lawyer Lawrence Lessig, who thought pure copyright and the pure public domain were both too extreme for how creators actually work. It sits on top of copyright law and provides a shortcut for the permission you’d otherwise have to ask for, one at a time, in advance. My favorite is CC BY-SA: attribution plus share-alike. It’s another “viral” license. Anyone can reuse and remix the work, as long as they credit the source and license their version the same way — which forces a kind of bibliography, a visible trail back to where things came from.

Other CC licenses are stricter. Attribution, no derivatives lets people reproduce the whole work, but not cut it up or rework it — useful for a photographer who’s fine with event photos being reshared, but not recontextualized. Eric Schrijver’s *Copy This Book: An Artist’s Guide to Copyright* is licensed this way: reproduce the whole book freely, but don’t cut it up and reassemble it into something else. Schrijver is part of Open Source Publishing, the Brussels collective behind Scribus, the open-source layout software. CC0 skips the license question entirely: it dedicates a work straight to the public domain, immediately, no seventy-year wait required.

## Where to Find the Material

- **Openverse** — searches multiple public domain and Creative Commons sources at once; now run by Automattic, the company behind WordPress.
- **Flickr Commons** — a dedicated section of Flickr for public domain and CC-licensed material.
- **OpenArt Images** and **public.work** — smaller, better-curated interfaces over similar source material.
- **RawPixel** — sells stock photos, but also has a large public domain section of rescanned, cleaned-up historical images. They bought and rescanned an entire William Morris pattern book themselves, at high resolution, and released the scans public domain even though they didn’t have to.
- **Unsplash** — not public domain, but free to use under its own license: no permission needed, though you can’t resell the photos or build a competing image service with them.
- **Google Fonts** and **Velvetyne** — real open-source type, not just free-as-in-cost clip-art-era fonts. You can get the actual font files and edit them.
- **[Badass Libre Fonts by Women](https://www.design-research.be/by-womxn/)** — a curated list by Loraine Furter, possible because the fonts themselves are libre-licensed and free to relink and redistribute.
- **Libre Graphics Magazine** — every issue, including the actual design files, published openly.
- **Public Domain Review** — publishes curated collections of public domain images along with real context on where they came from, not just a dump of files.
- **Archive.org**, and specifically the **Prelinger Archive** within it — Rick Prelinger’s collection of public domain film, released there because he believes culture should be free.

Worth knowing the difference between “free” meanings here: gratis means free of cost. Libre means free of restriction. Public domain material is libre. A lot of “free” stock photography is only gratis — free to download, but still licensed with limits on how you use it.

## Why This Matters for Designers

There’s a practical reason to care about all this, beyond the copyright mechanics.

Design tools are moving to the cloud, which means most of them now require an authorized, internet-connected account to function at all. It used to be that buying software meant buying the right to use it, forever, on your machine. Now the company can turn it off remotely, for any reason, at any time.

In 2019, the US imposed sanctions on Venezuela. Adobe wasn’t sure whether Creative Cloud access counted as the kind of business the sanctions restricted, so it cut off every Venezuelan IP address rather than find out the hard way. It turned out Adobe didn’t need to — but by the time that was clear, no designer in Venezuela could open Illustrator. That’s the risk of renting your tools: someone else’s caution, or someone else’s politics, can end your workday.

There’s a design reason too, not just a risk-management one. Working from public domain and Creative Commons material means you can’t just search “diverse office team high-fiving” and drop in whatever comes back first. Old material doesn’t carry the same contemporary visual clichés. It forces you to build meaning instead of borrowing one, and what you make ends up looking like it came from somewhere — instead of looking like whatever every other website is using this month.
