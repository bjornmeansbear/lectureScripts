# Variations on a Norwegian Folktale — pitch edition

*Restructure of `variations-on-a-norwegian-folktale.md` from participant takeaway into a
mailable object that gets the workshop booked. Drafted copy below where it's new; pointers
where it's lifted from existing files.*

---

## What changed

The old draft was written for someone who'd already been in the room. This one is written
for a chair or a program head who's never heard of me, opening campus mail.

Three things follow from that:

1. **No student zines.** No permissions, no cohort attached to it, no expiration. The thirteen zines go out; documentation photos of the room go in.
2. **The reading-aloud moment leads.** It's the strongest thing in the workshop and it was completely absent from the old draft. It's also the one part that can't be understood from a description, which makes it the thing worth printing.
3. **The booklet is the workshop's source material,** not its record. The folktale is in the back. The image pile is in the middle. Someone can run this without me, and saying so plainly is what makes the offer credible.

Format: 20 pages, digest (5.5 × 8.5), saddle-stitched, riso. Five sheets. Fits a 6×9 envelope. Weigh a dummy before you buy stamps — 20 pages of text weight may push past one ounce.

### 2–3 — The reading

> New. This is the opener. Concrete first, and it's the scene that sells the workshop.

A student holds up an eight-page zine and flips through it. Someone else in the room reads it out loud. There are no words in it, so they read the pictures. The maker says nothing.

Whatever the reader finds is what the zine says. Sometimes that's what the maker meant. Often it isn't.

It takes about four minutes. Everyone watches.

### 4–5 — What it is

Everyone works from the same pile of images and nothing else. No drawing, no outside material. Each person retells one story — a Norwegian folktale about a boy who out-eats a troll by cheating.

Thirteen people did this at the University of Utah. Thirteen zines came out, and they don't look like each other. Some told the story straight. One turned it into a silent sequence. One was an absurdist strip that barely touched the plot.

The narrow pile did that. Give people a wide-open image search and they converge. Give them forty pictures and one story and they go in every direction at once.

**Day one — the zine.** Short lecture: my work, the design commons, and how meaning gets made. Everyone contributes to the pile. We print it grayscale. Each student collages an eight-page zine retelling the story, no words. Then the reading.

**Day two — the poster.** One poster, built from imagery already used in the zines — yours or anyone else's. The original sources are closed now. Type is allowed, and digital tools. The poster is an aphorism: whatever moral survived the retelling, said in one gesture.

Zine and poster print back to back and unfold into one object.

### 8–9 — Why public domain

> Condense from `copyright-and-the-commons.md`. Two pages, not four. The full essay is a separate document and a chair skimming this doesn't need the whole mechanism — they need to know the workshop rests on something real.
>
> Keep: the 1928 → 2024 arithmetic, Disney as the good bad guy, the difference between public domain and CC. Cut: anything that reads as a lecture outline.

Draft:

Walt Disney released *Steamboat Willie* in November 1928. Mickey, as he appears in it, entered the public domain in 2024 — ninety-five years later. Anyone in the US can use that version freely now. Later Mickeys are separate, newer copyrights, and Disney still holds those.

Disney spent most of the century keeping the original locked up. Congress extended
copyright to life plus fifty years in 1976, then to seventy in 1998. Ninety-five years before this one cartoon lapsed. Disney is an easy bad guy here, and a good one — the lobbying is a matter of public record and the dates line up too neatly to leave out.

That's the mechanism. Copyright runs a fixed term and then the work belongs to everyone. Most old folktales were never copyrighted at all.

Making something new out of public domain material produces a new copyrighted work the moment it exists, owned by whoever made it. Students find this out by doing it, and then have to decide what to do with it. We license the results CC BY-SA — reuse and remix freely, credit the source, license your version the same way. The pile the material came from gets something back.

### 10–11 — Make one yourself

> Replaces the old "Thirteen Zines: A Hands-On Experience" section, which had thirteen pull-out zines as its object and no longer has anything.
>
> Facing pages: instructions on the left, an 8-page fold-and-cut imposition diagram on the right. Line art. Best possible riso content.

Take the folktale in the back. Find your own pile — old illustrations, vintage ads, forgotten clip art. Flickr Commons and RawPixel's public domain section are good places to start. Forty images is plenty. Fewer is better than more.

Cut it apart. Eight pages, no words. Retell the story or don't.

Fold, staple, or stitch it.

Then hand it to someone and have them read it to you.

### 18 — Who's asking

I'm Kristian Bjørnard. I teach in the graphic and product design departments at MICA, just finished a 4 year tenure as chair of undergraduate design, and run my climate-design studio Wjerk.

I've run this four times. First at the invitation of the University of Utah, January 2025. My own classes at MICA, twice. And then at HAW Hamburg (Hamburg, Germany), October 2025, where participants used their own family photographs as the pile instead of clip art. That version is warmer and riskier. The folktale version travels better.

**What it takes on your end**

Preferably two days. Studio space with wall room. Grayscale laser printing, high volume — this eats paper. Scissors, glue, tape. Day two needs computers and large-format output.

Twelve to twenty students. Junior or senior studio, or a graduate seminar.

**What it costs**

The materials are free. Everything in this booklet is CC BY-SA. Run the workshop yourself, change it, teach it under your own name. You don't need to ask me and you don't owe me anything.

I charge for showing up and running it. Write me and we'll work it out.

> Free materials, paid facilitation. It's an unusual offer and stating it flat is what makes the rest credible. No fee number anywhere — that keeps every conversation open and keeps the print run good for years.
