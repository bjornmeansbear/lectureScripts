# Some Semiotics — Examples and References

---

## 1 · Already in the talk

### Theorists, as named

**Roland Barthes** (1915–1980, France). Denotation, connotation, myth.
*Mythologies* (1957) — the essays on the Citroën DS, steak and chips, wrestling. This is
the one to assign; it's short pieces about ordinary objects and it reads.
"The Death of the Author" (1967) — meaning is made at the point of reception, not by the
maker. You cite this in the modern/postmodern passage.

**Ferdinand de Saussure** (1857–1913, Switzerland). Signifier / signified. *Cours de
linguistique générale*, published 1916, assembled by students from lecture notes after he
died — worth one clause, because a book about how meaning is constructed being itself
reconstructed by other people is the joke telling itself.
The term you want and don't currently say: **the arbitrariness of the sign**. It's his,
it's the whole basis of "nothing has intrinsic meaning," and naming it costs two seconds.

**Charles Sanders Peirce** (1839–1914, USA). Pronounced "purse." Representamen / object /
interpretant. Icon, index, symbol — his **second trichotomy**, classifying a sign by how it
relates to its object. Unlimited semiosis.
Baltimore and Johns Hopkins connection is real and local to the room. He lost the Hopkins
post and spent his last decades in rural Pennsylvania, poor and largely unpublished.

**Ben Duvall.** *New Modernisms* (Draw Down). Signs on surfaces; the
modern → postmodern → hypermodern progression; the Form / Content / Context grid, which
the deck credits as evolved from Andrew Blauvelt and Ellen Lupton.

**Paddy Whannel** — "Semiotics tells us things we already know in a language we will never
understand." Widely quoted; usually attributed via Whannel's BFI education work in the
1960s. CHECK the exact source if you ever put it in print — the attribution is solid, the
citation is vague everywhere it appears.

**Beatrice Warde** (1900–1969). "The Crystal Goblet, or Printing Should Be Invisible"
(1930 as a lecture, 1932 in print, under the pen name Paul Beaujon for other work). Type
as a wine glass — you should see the wine, not the goblet. **This is doing real work in §6
and it has no slide.** It's the most articulate statement of the neutrality claim you're
about to dismantle, which makes it the ideal thing to put up first.

**Scott McCloud.** *Understanding Comics* (1993). The picture plane / iconic abstraction
triangle — realism at one corner, language at another, pure abstraction at the apex. You
assign this in GD3 already.

**Note on the Magritte title.** The painting is *La Trahison des images* — *The Treachery
of Images*. "Ceci n'est pas une pipe" is the sentence painted on it, not the title. Worth
getting right on the slide.

**Note on Target.** You said on tape you want a slide on the logo history and on why it's
called Target. The bullseye has been there since the store opened in 1962 under Dayton's,
and the mark has simplified over time toward the wordless bullseye. The origin story
usually told is that a Dayton's publicity staffer proposed it on a marksmanship metaphor.
**CHECK before asserting** — that story circulates widely and I can't verify the specifics.
The design argument doesn't need it: the wordmark could be dropped because the connotation
had finished its work, and that's true regardless of who named the store.

## 2 · Worth adding

Ranked. The first four are the ones I'd actually put in.

### The floppy disk save icon

The strongest addition available, and it costs one slide.

A drawing of a physical object nobody in the room has handled, still meaning "save."
It began as an **icon** — it looked like the thing you were doing. The object died. The
resemblance stopped resembling anything. It is now a pure **symbol**: arbitrary, learned,
and completely stable.

One sign migrating between Peirce's categories inside a single career. It proves that the
categories are relations rather than properties, and it makes unlimited semiosis concrete
without a meme. **Put it in §12, right after "they're aspects, not bins."**

### Mr. Yuk

Pittsburgh Poison Center, 1971. They found that the skull and crossbones didn't warn
children off poison — it read as *pirates*, and Pittsburgh children additionally read it
as the *Pirates*, which is the local baseball team. The most universal danger symbol in the
West was, for the audience that most needed it, an invitation.

So they designed a green scowling face and taught it.

This is your §6 argument with a body count attached: a symbol everyone calls universal,
failing on contact with an actual audience, and a designed replacement that only worked
because it came with a teaching campaign. It also sets up the closer — *does my design
communicate the way I intend, to who I intend* — better than anything currently in the deck.

### Stuart Hall — encoding / decoding

(1932–2014.) "Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse" (1973). The maker encodes
a preferred meaning; the audience decodes it, and can decode it three ways —
**dominant** (takes it as intended), **negotiated** (accepts the frame, argues the
details), **oppositional** (understands it perfectly and rejects it).

Why it belongs: your whole talk builds to "you're always communicating to somebody
specific," and Hall gives the room a model of *the somebody* rather than an instruction to
go ask them. It also means an audience rejecting your work isn't a failure to communicate
— they may have received it precisely and disagreed. Students conflate those constantly.

Two sentences in §13, or a slide before the closer.

### Otto Neurath's Isotype, with Ellen Lupton's critique

Isotype (from the 1920s, Vienna) is the modernist universality claim built as an actual
product: a picture language meant to be legible to anyone regardless of language or
literacy. Lupton's essay "Reading Isotype" takes it apart — the system turns out to depend
on conventions you have to learn, and it encodes a particular politics about who needs
information simplified for them.

This is the §6 payload with a design case under it instead of an assertion about European
men. If you only add one thing to §6, add this. The AIGA/DOT symbol signs (1974) are the
American sequel and are probably already familiar to the room.

### Also worth knowing about

**Umberto Eco** (1932–2016). *A Theory of Semiotics* (1976). His definition is the best
one-liner in the field: semiotics is the discipline studying everything which can be used
in order to lie. If a thing can't be used to lie, it can't be used to tell the truth
either. That's your "nothing has intrinsic meaning" in a form students remember.

**Judith Williamson.** *Decoding Advertisements* (1978). Barthes's method applied to ads —
the most directly transferable book on this list for a designer.

**Dick Hebdige.** *Subculture: The Meaning of Style* (1979). Style as signification;
subcultures making meaning by recombining objects that already meant something else. This
is the citation for your line about connotations that only land inside a subculture.

**Michael Rock.** "The Designer as Author" (*Eye*, 1996). The designer-native companion to
Death of the Author, and it's an argument you can disagree with in front of them.

**Katherine McCoy** and Cranbrook; **Rudy VanderLans and Zuzana Licko** at Emigre;
**David Carson** at *Ray Gun* — the name you were reaching for on the tape. These are what
design did once it stopped believing in neutrality. If Emigre comes back into the talk,
this is the frame that justifies it.

**Adrian Frutiger.** *Signs and Symbols: Their Design and Meaning*. A designer's semiotics
rather than a linguist's. Assignable, and the PDF is in the channel.

**Zahavi's handicap principle** (biology) and **Spence's signalling** (economics). Two
fields arriving independently at: a signal is trustworthy in proportion to what it costs to
fake. This is the theory floor under the index argument below, and under
`../booklet-new-design-commons/07-signs-signaling-sustainability.md`.

### From the "candidates" pile, re-ranked

**The Ray Cat Solution — now in the script, closing §7.**

Françoise Bastide (French) and Paolo Fabbri (Italian), both semioticians, 1984: breed cats
that visibly change colour near radiation, then seed folklore, songs and proverbs so the
meaning of a colour-changing cat survives ten thousand years and the loss of every language
now spoken. The animal is the detector; the story is the documentation.

The context is real institutional work. The US Department of Energy convened a **Human
Interference Task Force** in the early 1980s to figure out how to mark the WIPP nuclear
repository for people with no shared language or culture. Bastide and Fabbri's contribution
came out of that conversation. **Sandia's 1993 markers report** is the institutional answer
— fields of spikes, thorn landscapes, menacing earthworks, all of it trying to signify
*danger* without signifying *treasure*.

Why it works where you've now put it: every other cat meaning in §7 is accidental. This is
two people deliberately manufacturing one and budgeting a hundred centuries for it to set.
It's the arbitrariness of the sign under maximum load, it's the same animal the whole
lecture runs on, and it hands §3's nuclear aside a payoff.

CHECK before it goes on a slide: the piece is usually cited to *Zeitschrift für Semiotik*
vol. 6 (1984), which ran a special issue on the nuclear-marker problem, under a title along
the lines of "Living Detectors and Complementary Signs." I'm confident about the authors,
the year and the proposal; less so about the exact title and translation. "The Ray Cat
Solution" is a later popular coinage, not theirs.

Accessible source for the room: the **99% Invisible** episode "Ten Thousand Years" — where
most designers first meet this, and assignable as listening.

**The recycling triangle.** A Möbius loop meaning *recyclable* wrapped around a resin code
meaning *what plastic this is*. The connotation hardened into a fact that isn't true, and
the material benefit runs to whoever put it there. Barthes's myth arriving a second time,
in a place students touch daily. **This is your best second myth example after the American
Dream** — better than IKEA, which you said on tape you hadn't cracked yet.

Underneath it is the index argument that's yours and worth making: you can draw an icon of
anything and assign a symbol to anything, and both cost an afternoon. An index costs
whatever the thing costs, because the thing has to have actually happened. Which is why
greenwashing runs on icons and symbols.

**Wind barbs.** Pure convention, zero resemblance, unreadable until taught. The
counterweight to the Big Red Boot. One slide, ten seconds.

**The butcher's chart.** French and American charts disagree about where a cow's parts
are. Same animal, cut into different signs by different cultures. There's a full cow run in
the channel. This is a second act, not a garnish — hold it for the 45-minute version.

**Apple's AI icon.** An industry failing in public and in real time to agree on a symbol.
Good, current, and it dates fast.

### Small ones that pay for their slide

- **Emoji drift** — 🙏 as prayer, then thanks, then high-five. 💀 meaning laughter. Signs
  visibly changing meaning inside the students' own lifetimes.
- **Mourning colours** — black in much of Europe, white in much of East Asia. The black cat
  point, generalised.
- **The green light** — you already say it. It's the cleanest symbol example in the talk
  and it takes four words.

The Noun Project icon grid **moved to §6**. It reads as a digression in the connotation run
because it isn't a connotation example — it's the neutrality argument in a second medium.
Sat next to the Helvetica/Bodoni question it costs fifteen seconds instead of thirty, and

site this week. The credit slides (510, 1011) already exist.

## 3 · Chairs — shot list

For §5 (denotation) and §8 (one form, six materials). None of these slides exist yet.

### §5 · The chair-ness ladder

Same structure as the cat ladder, five or six slides:

1. The word **chair**, plainly set
2. A chair icon — Noun Project, CC BY, credit on slide as with the cat
3. **C-H-A-I-R** broken apart
4. **의자** / **stol** / **椅子**
5. The dictionary definition
6. **chair-ness**

Then the edge cases, four images, fast — *is this chair-ness?*

- A stool (no back — does it still qualify?)
- A beanbag (no legs, no back, no frame)
- A throne (all chair, plus something else — and it sets up §10)
- **A tree stump somebody sits on daily** — the one to land on. A chair only because of
  use. That's the interpretant doing the work, eighteen minutes before Peirce arrives.

Then: to chair a meeting, to be a department chair. The word without the seat — same job
as the Caterpillar slide, and it's yours.

### §8 · One form, six materials

| Chair | Material | What the material says |
|---|---|---|
| **Emeco 1006 Navy**, 1944 | Brushed recycled aluminium | Institution. Indestructible. The Navy bought it. |
| **Emeco 111 Navy**, 2010 | 111 recycled PET bottles | Virtue. And it's dated to the year. |
| **Fukasawa HAL**, Vitra, 2010 | One shell — wood, ply, plastic, recycled, many bases | The controlled experiment. Silhouette constant, meaning not. |
| **Starck Louis Ghost**, Kartell, 2002 | Injection-moulded polycarbonate | Louis XVI form, forty-euro material, and you can see through the joke. |
| **The monobloc** | One-piece polypropylene | Chair-ness with everything else stripped off. |
| **Breuer Wassily / B3**, 1925–26 | Drawn steel tube | Modernism's own furniture, in bicycle-handlebar stock. |

**The monobloc is the one that has to survive a cut.** It's the only one carrying the
context reversal: cheap and disposable in most of Europe and North America, and in many
other places a real marker of having arrived — you own furniture, you can seat guests.
Same object, opposite connotations, nobody redesigned anything. That is §7's black-cat
argument made in a material students see every day.

It's also authorless, uncopyrighted and endlessly copied, which walks straight into
`../workshop-open-source-design/` and the commons material if you ever want the bridge.

**The Wassily belongs to §6, not §8** — it's the Modernism payload, not a materials
comparison. Steel tube was chosen for what it signified about industry and the future by
people who told you the choice was purely rational.

**Two more if you want them:**

- **Rietveld's Red Blue chair** (1918, painted c. 1923) — form as pure ideology, comfort
  declined. Plans published; endlessly rebuilt. Another commons hook.
- **Herman Miller Aeron** (1994) — mesh and aluminium, and within a decade the chair
  meant *dot-com money* rather than *ergonomics*. Connotation hardening into myth, on a
  chair, inside living memory.
