## 1 · Magritte, the mug, what semiotics is (0:00–3:00)

Open on the painting, before the word "semiotics" has to carry anything.

> Who recognizes this painting?

Let them get there. Someone will say *this is not a pipe*.

> So what's the point of painting a pipe and then writing on it that it isn't a pipe?
>
> Right — it *is* a painting of a pipe. It's meant to make you think of pipe-ness. It is
> not a pipe. You can't smoke it.

Then the mug. This is yours, so tell it as yours.

> I used to design for one of the art museums in town, and we made this mug. It says
> "this is not a coffee mug" on the mug. My mom asked why. I said because it's a tea mug.

Then land the definition while they're still amused:

> Semiotics is the study of signs and symbols and how they carry meaning. For us that's
> text and images, but it's also the materials you pick, the form of an object, how an
> interface works, the shape of the legs on a chair, the sound a button makes.
>
> It's the philosophy you actually use as a designer, whether or not you know the word.
> How do we make choices about how a thing looks and works and what it's made of — and
> then what meaning do people get out of that?

**Say the method out loud here.** Two objects run the whole talk. Tell them:

> We're going to do this with two things all the way through. A chair and a cat.

Whannel's line is on slide 001 and does not need reading aloud — it's funnier as a thing
they notice than as a thing you perform.

## 2 · Signs signaling on substrates (3:00–4:30)

Ninety seconds. This is the frame, not the lecture — the full version is
`../lecture-signs-signaling-on-substrates/`, and that's where it should stay.

> Ben Duvall describes graphic design as manipulating elements on a surface in order to
> communicate. Type and images are already signs. They arrive carrying meaning. The way
> you set a word adds a second layer on top of what the word says.
>
> A signifier is the physical form. The signified is the idea you're attaching to it. Put
> those together and you get a sign — one unit of meaning. Put signs on a surface and you
> get graphic design.
>
> Widen the surface and you get everything else. A screen, paper, a chair, an ATM, a
> drinking glass. And the surface is a sign too. We'll come back to that.

**Decision made:** this lecture borrows the six slides and points at the other file. The
outline left this open; the test run settles it — you used it as a frame and moved on in
about two minutes, which is what it's for here.

## 3 · Meaning was decided by somebody (4:30–6:30)

> Everything we understand got made up by somebody. Tying a concept to a word. Tying a
> concept to a form. Bolting simple concepts together into complicated ones. All invented,
> all agreed on by a culture.
>
> When cultures don't agree, that's where the conflict is. And there are concepts that
> have no sign at all in some places — the thing doesn't exist there, so the word doesn't.

Then the two slides that carry the rest of the hour:

> Nothing has intrinsic meaning.
>
> But meaning matters. Being clear matters.

Say the guardrail immediately. Do not leave the gap open.

> I don't think the modernists were right that there's one correct universal way to say a
> thing. I do think that when you make something, people in different times and places
> will read it differently, and that's fine.
>
> It's less fine if you're trying to keep people out of a nuclear waste site for ten
> thousand years. Different problem.

**That nuclear aside is a plant.** Keep it to one sentence and don't explain it — you pay
it off at the end of §7 with the ray cats, and it lands harder if they've half-forgotten
it. It's also the hook to `../lecture-design-for-the-future-today/`.

## 5 · Denotation — chair-ness (7:00–10:00)

*[400–401 hold. Swap the cat run at 402–410 for the chair run.]*

> Denotation is the literal definition. The dictionary meaning. As close to fact-based
> reporting as meaning making gets.

Run the same ladder the cat run used, on a chair — word, icon, letters, other languages,
definition, then the *-ness*:

> The word "chair." An icon of a chair. C-H-A-I-R — five letters we decided mean chair.
> 의자. Stol. 椅子. A seat with a back, for one person.
>
> All of those are trying to say the same thing. What they're trying to convey is
> chair-ness.

Then the edge cases, which is where this earns its three minutes. Four or five images,
fast:

> A stool. Is that chair-ness?
>
> A beanbag.
>
> A throne.
>
> A tree stump somebody sits on every day.

The stump is the one to sit on. It's a chair only because somebody uses it as one — which
is the interpreter doing the work, and it plants Peirce eighteen minutes before he arrives.

Then the joke that's available to you and nobody else in the room:

> And then "chair" doesn't have to have anything to do with sitting. You can chair a
> meeting. You can be the chair of a department. I was, for a while. The word did not come
> with a seat.

That does the same job the Caterpillar slide does, earlier and about you.

**Cut for time:** the catness photo parade. Nine slides of "is this catness?" that you
narrated as a digression while delivering it. The chair ladder asks the same question in
half the slides and sets up §8.

## 6 · Neutral? → Modernism (10:00–13:00) — **payload**

This is the first thing they should still have next week. Protect the three minutes.

> Denotation assumes the sign you picked is neutral — that it just conveys the meaning and
> adds nothing. When we say something's neutral in design, that's what we mean. No extra
> hidden layers.
>
> That's Beatrice Warde's crystal goblet. The type is a wine glass. You're supposed to see
> the wine, not the goblet.
>
> So — is "cat" set in Helvetica somehow more neutrally cat than "cat" set in Bodoni?

*[419–420: the three settings]* Take answers. Don't answer it for them yet.

> Here's what I think. Neutrality is a made-up concept, and what it actually means is
> either "this reflects the beliefs of whoever made it" or "this reflects the status quo."
> Both of those are ideologies. Neither is neutral.
>
> Modernism tells you it's a neutral formal discipline. I'd like you to consider that it's
> just another ideological formalism carrying its own baggage — mostly assembled by
> European white guys working out what communication meant to other European white guys,
> sixty to eighty years ago.
>
> It isn't neutral. It affects meaning like everything else does. And because it's the
> status quo style, and because so many of the "rules" of design came out of it, it gets
> to imply there's a correct way to design a thing and a wrong one.

### Second proof, in icons (~15 sec)

*[The Noun Project "cat" grid — the slide exists.]*

The type version above is one question. This is the same question in a medium they'll use
this week, which makes it the more useful of the two.

> Here's what you get searching "cat" on the Noun Project. Every one of these is somebody
> trying to draw the generic cat.
>
> None of them managed it. That one's playful, that one's sleepy, that one's cute, that
> one's a logo for a vet. There is no neutral cat in here.
>
> Same as there's no neutral way to set the word.

Fifteen seconds, one slide, and it lands because §6 has already loaded the idea — it was
costing thirty and trailing off when it sat in the connotation run.

**Optional third proof — the Wassily.** Breuer's tubular steel chair, Modernism's own
furniture, in the same drawn steel tube as bicycle handlebars. A material chosen for what
it signified about industry and the future, by people telling you the choice was rational.

**Run one or the other, not both** — you're at three minutes. The icon grid proves
neutrality is unavailable. The Wassily indicts Modernism specifically. Pick whichever the
room needs, and the grid is the safer default because they use that website.

## 7 · Connotation — cats (13:00–16:00)

Cats keep this section. It's the run that works.

> Connotation is everything that isn't literal. Not stated, not defined — the extra that
> comes along. Some of it is obvious to everybody. Some of it only lands for a subculture.
> A lot of it is emotional, cultural, personal. And it varies by who's looking and where
> they're standing.

*[503/504/505 — the grotesque, the display face, the cat-C]*

> Here's "cat" in a plain grotesque. Here's a cat typeface. Here's one where the C *is* a
> cat — ears, eyes, a tail making the bowl of the letter.
>
> That last one is a letter and a picture at the same time. Hold onto it, we're coming
> back to it.

Then the run of other meanings, which is the part worth keeping:

> What else does "cat" mean? Sneakiness. Mystery. Elegance. Grumpiness. Curiosity.
> Stealth. Independence.
>
> A cat burglar is a sneaky infiltrator. Nobody thinks about the animal.
>
> A black cat is bad luck here and good luck somewhere else. Same cat. The luck isn't in
> the cat, it's in the room.
>
> You can't use Garfield to stand in for a generic cat — even if you don't know who
> Garfield is, he's doing something too specific.
>
> And then this is a cat. But it's short for Caterpillar, and none of this has anything to
> do with the animal.

Land it:

> None of those meanings are in the animal. They're in us.

### The ray cats (closes §7, ~45 sec)

Now pay off the nuclear line from §3.

> Back to the ten-thousand-year problem for a second.
>
> In the eighties the US government had a real committee on this. How do you tell people
> to stay off a nuclear waste site when every language we speak now will be gone? You
> can't write it. You can't draw it. Anything you build big enough to notice reads as a
> monument, and monuments get dug up on purpose.
>
> Two semioticians, Françoise Bastide and Paolo Fabbri, proposed cats.
>
> Breed a cat that changes colour near radiation. Then — and this is the actual proposal —
> seed the culture with songs and proverbs and folk tales saying that when the cat changes
> colour, you leave. Don't explain why. Just make it a thing everybody's grandmother says.
>
> The cat is the instrument. The folklore is the manual. The plan is that the story
> outlives the language it was first told in.

Then the turn:

> Everything else today was a meaning that just happened. The burglar, the black cat,
> Garfield — nobody planned any of those.
>
> This is two people sitting down to install one on purpose, and budgeting ten thousand
> years for it to set.

Stop there. Don't draw the line to their own work — the room gets there, and saying it
flattens it.

**What pays for this:** the Noun Project riff, which has moved to §6 where it belongs — it
was never a connotation example, it was a neutrality proof sitting in the wrong room. Net
cost of the ray cats after that move is about fifteen seconds.

## 8 · One form, six materials (16:00–18:30) — **new**

The section the chairs bought you. This is connotation you can point at, and it's the most
purely designerly moment available in the talk.

> Same form. Different material. Watch what happens to the meaning.

Run them fast. The form is constant; only the substance changes:

> Emeco's Navy chair, 1944, aluminum, built for the US Navy. It means indestructible, it
> means institution, it means the government bought it.
>
> The same chair in recycled Coke bottles. Now it means virtue, and it means 2010.
>
> Naoto Fukasawa's HAL family for Vitra — one shell, offered in wood, plywood, plastic,
> recycled plastic, on a dozen bases. Same silhouette every time. The wood one is for a
> nice house. The plastic one is for a cafeteria.
>
> Starck's Louis Ghost — an eighteenth-century French court chair, injection-moulded in
> clear polycarbonate. The form says aristocracy. The material says forty euros. The joke
> is that you can see straight through it.
>
> And the monobloc. The white plastic stacking chair. The most-produced chair on earth,
> no author, no copyright, no design history. It's what chair-ness looks like with
> everything else stripped off.

Then the turn — this is the point of the section:

> The monobloc means cheap here. In a lot of places it means you've arrived, you own
> furniture, you can seat guests. Same object. Opposite meanings. Nobody redesigned it.

Then hand it to them:

> Your material is not a production decision you make at the end. It's a sign, and it's
> often louder than the form.

**This connects to your own studio work** — see the references file for how the monobloc
lands on the commons material, and `../workshop-open-source-design/`.

**Cut for time:** run three of the five. Navy, Louis Ghost, monobloc. The monobloc is
non-negotiable — it's the only one carrying the context reversal.

## 9 · ICE cooler → Target (18:30–21:00)

The cooler was silent in the deck. It is not silent now.

> What do the signs on this box tell you?
>
> There's ice in it. How do you know it's *cold* ice? Because they grew icicles off the
> letters.
>
> That's connotative. Somebody decided the word "ice," neutrally set, wasn't enough. They
> needed snow on the letters so you'd really get it. Cold, icy ice.

The editorial position is the part worth saying out loud:

> Some people think that's unsophisticated. Modernists think it's unsophisticated. I think

Then the target on the box, which is the best worked example in the deck because it does

> What's the literal read of this symbol? A target. You shoot at it.

> And it's arbitrary — there's nothing about concentric rings that means retail. We just

## 10 · Myth (21:00–23:00) — **payload**

> Barthes's third idea. When a connotation becomes the main thing people see — when they
> stop being able to see the literal meaning underneath it — it's become naturalized. It
> reads like a fact about the world instead of a decision somebody made.
>
> That's myth. And it's useful to whoever it benefits.

*[702 — The American Dream]*

> The American dream. The signs attached to it: your own house, a car, a savings account,
> a family, the suburbs. None of that is a fact about how life works. It's a set of
> associations that hardened.
>
> And people arrange their actual present around it — what job to take, what debt to
> carry — because they believe it's waiting for them.

The one-line sharpener, which also links the future lecture:

> That's the future being used against people. Same cone, pointed the wrong way.

**Second example if you want one:** the throne. You've already shown one in §5 as a
chair-ness edge case, which makes the callback free — a chair that has been so thoroughly
naturalized as power that we no longer see the furniture. IKEA is in your notes but you
said on tape you hadn't found the version of it you wanted; the throne is built already.

## 11 · Saussure → Peirce (23:00–26:00)

You apologized on tape for going in reverse order. Don't. **The order is correct and it is
deliberate** — the room has now felt denotation, connotation and myth, so the machinery
arrives as an explanation of something they've already experienced. Every textbook does
this backwards. Say it as a choice:

> We did all of that without naming anybody. Now here's where it comes from.

**Saussure — sixty seconds. No biography.**

> Swiss, writing about language. He thought a language is a system of signs, and that if
> you got the system right you could describe how meaning works underneath any language.
>
> Signifier and signified. The word "cat," the sound a cat makes, a picture of fur — all
> signifiers. The furry creature and the concept of cat-ness — signified. Together they're
> the sign.
>
> He's a modernist about it. One-to-one. Get the system right and meaning becomes an
> algorithm.

**Peirce — ninety seconds. The parts that matter.**

> Peirce is American, he's in Baltimore for a while and teaches at Hopkins, and he wants
> logical rules for *any* kind of sign, not just words.
>
> He says there are three things, not two. The sign. What it points at. And the person
> making sense of it.
>
> That third one is the whole difference. The interpreter is inside the model. So the
> multiple meanings Barthes is describing aren't a complication for Peirce — they're built
> in from the start. A different reader gets a different meaning, by design.

Then the line that opens the last section:

> And the meaning of a sign is another sign. Which can be interpreted again. Which
> produces another sign. It never closes. He calls it unlimited semiosis.
>
> Memes are the clearest version of this any of us have. Same image, small changes, and it
> means something different to every audience it lands on — and each of those becomes the
> thing the next one is riffing on.

**Cut for time:** Peirce the hermit in the Pennsylvania woods, the death dates, and which
of them was older. It's a good story and it costs ninety seconds you don't have. It's in
the references file.

## 12 · Types of signs → Big Red Boot (26:00–28:30)

**Attribute them.** They're Peirce's second trichotomy, and saying so turns two sections
into one argument.

> These are Peirce's, and they're the three ways a sign can relate to what it points at.
>
> Iconic — it resembles the thing. A picture of a cat.
>
> Indexical — it was *caused by* the thing. A litter box means cat.
>
> Symbolic — it's arbitrary. The word "cat." A green light meaning go. The Target
> bullseye.

**Fix from the test run:** at the litter box you said "it's a symbol, right?" while
describing an index. Say index. You corrected yourself later in the talk, but the room
heard the wrong word first.

> And technically this litter box needs to be used. An empty one is a litter box a cat
> owner bought. A used one is a trace the cat actually made — same category as a
> footprint. That's a real index.

**Then the move that makes this section land — they're aspects, not bins.**

> Most signs are two of these at once. A photograph of a cat resembles a cat *and* was
> caused by one. Go back to that cat-C from earlier — that's a letter and a picture in a
> single mark. Symbol and icon at the same time.

*[Re-show 505.]* You already own this slide. Reaching back for it costs nothing and it
uses evidence the room has already accepted.

**Big Red Boot closes it.**

> MSCHF's Big Red Boot. Their line is that it's "really not shaped like feet, but
> extremely shaped like boots."
>
> What are they going for? Boot-ness. But whose? Because I only see one thing when I look
> at it, and it's Astro Boy.
>
> Which is connotation — that's me bringing something to it that isn't in the object.
> And it's an icon of a symbol. It doesn't resemble a boot. It resembles the *cartoon
> convention* for a boot, which was already arbitrary.

**McCloud, if you have thirty seconds:**

> Scott McCloud has a diagram in *Understanding Comics* — realism at one corner, language
> at another, pure abstraction at the top. What actually works is usually a combination:
> the image doing some lifting, the words doing some, the form and colour doing the rest.
>
> That's where this becomes useful to you as a form-giver.

Cut McCloud before you cut the Boot.

## 13 · Context, the grid, the closer (28:30–30:00)

The Duvall grid lives here now.

> Last thing. Everything we just did depends on context — where a sign is, when, and who's
> in front of it.

*[3005 — the grid]*

> Modernists worked on **form**. Single forms, maximum meaning, and the designer decides
> what the form means.
>
> Postmodernists added **content** — the content you're working with drives the form, for
> different audiences.
>
> What you have to work with now is **context**. Not just that your thing goes out to many
> audiences, but that their reinterpretations go out to many more.
>
> That's why we're still bad at understanding each other, with more shared references than
> anyone has ever had.

Then the closer. Two questions and a demand:

> You can't communicate universally. You're always talking to somebody specific.
>
> So the question to ask is: **does my design communicate the way I intend, to who I
> intend?**
>
> You might not be able to answer that yourself. You might have to go ask them.

And the part that turns it into studio policy — this is the strongest ending on the tape
and it should stay exactly where it is:

> And when you bring work to critique, that's on you too. If we don't know the context
> you're working in — who it's for, where it lives, what references it's leaning on — we
> can only tell you whether we like it. That's not useful to you.
>
> The context is part of the research. Bring it with the work.
