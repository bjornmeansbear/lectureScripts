# The Big Red Boot, at the Fourth Order

MSCHF built a shoe out of pure signification and almost no shoe. That makes it a near-perfect
teaching object for Baudrillard, because you can walk up the four orders of the image and
watch a boot detach from reality one rung at a time.

---

## 1 · The four orders

Baudrillard's core move in *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981): the sign's relationship to
reality decays in stages.

1. **It reflects a basic reality.** A photograph of a boot. A faithful copy — the good
   appearance, representation in the sacramental order.
2. **It masks and perverts a reality.** A stylised ad boot, idealised, still pointing at
   real boots. A malefic appearance; it distorts something that exists.
3. **It masks the absence of a reality.** The image pretends there's an original when there
   isn't. It plays at being a copy.
4. **It bears no relation to any reality whatsoever.** Its own pure simulacrum.

The Big Red Boots live at stage 4. The clever part is how they route through stage 3 to get
there.

## 2 · The referent is a drawing

A cartoon boot — Astro Boy, Wario, Dora's monkey, the platonic doodle a five-year-old makes
— was never a real object. It's an image with no original in the world. It's a convention
for depicting *boot*.

MSCHF then manufactures a physical thing that copies that convention. A real, wearable
object whose source material is a fiction. The copy has no original.

That's the hyperreal: the model precedes and generates the real thing, rather than the real
thing being represented by a model. The map precedes the territory.

## 3 · More real than real

Baudrillard's formulations: the simulacrum is *always already reproduced*, and the hyperreal
is *the meticulous reduplication of the real, preferably through another reproductive
medium.*

These boots are more boot than a boot. Glossier, redder, rounder, more emphatically BOOT
than any leather pair could be, because they're built from the exaggeration logic of
animation. They out-signify their own referent. That excess of signification around a hollow
centre is what he means by hyperreal.

## 4 · Sign-value over use-value

In *For a Critique of the Political Economy of the Sign* (1972) the object splits four ways:
use-value (function), exchange-value (price), symbolic value (gift, relationship), and
sign-value (status within a code of differences).

The Big Red Boots crush use-value to near zero. Awkward to walk in, hard to get off,
functionally absurd — precisely so that sign-value can occupy the whole object. Nothing is
left to distract from a pure statement of position within a code.

**The uselessness is load-bearing.**

## 5 · Simulation, not disguise

Baudrillard separates faking from simulating. To dissimulate is to pretend not to have what
you have — the real is still underneath. To simulate is to produce the symptoms with nothing
beneath them, and the difference between true and false collapses.

The boots don't pretend to be functional footwear you'd wear in the rain. They simulate
footwear so totally that *is this a real shoe* stops being an answerable question. They
produce the symptoms of a shoe with no shoe-truth under it.

## 6 · The precession of the model, and the implosion

The intuitive order: real boots exist → someone draws a cartoon boot → MSCHF riffs on the
drawing. Baudrillard inverts the arrow. The model comes first — the cartoon-boot code, the
MSCHF concept, the virality mechanics — and the physical boot is generated to satisfy it.

Demand didn't create the object. Nobody needed these. **The code needed them.**

And when signs only refer to other signs, meaning implodes into fascination. You don't ask
what they're for. You stare. The virality, the memes, the *I can't believe this exists* —
that's the hyperreal doing its actual work. Not communicating meaning, but circulating
fascination in place of it. The boots mean nothing and are about nothing, and that void is
exactly why the internet couldn't stop looking.

## 7 · The objection — and it cuts deeper than "both readings hold"

MSCHF is an art collective doing institutional critique. So the boots are simulacra that
know they're simulacra, and that's a problem for the reading rather than a flourish on it.

Baudrillard's hyperreal is insidious *because it is unmarked*. Disneyland works as his
example precisely because nobody announces it — it exists to make us believe the rest of
America is real. A simulacrum that raises its hand is doing something else.

Which means a marked simulacrum arguably falls back to **order 3**: it masks the absence of
a reality, and tells you it's doing so. The self-awareness keeps dragging the object back
down the ladder it climbed.

So the boot may be a fourth-order object wearing a third-order costume.

Worth noticing that this is the same structure as the boot itself. In the lecture it's an
icon of a symbol — one order nested inside another. In the theory it's a fourth-order
simulacrum performing a third-order one. **The nesting repeats at every level you look at
it**, which is either the strongest thing about the object or the reason to distrust the
whole reading.

Either the most Baudrillardian thing possible, or the joke curdling into another commodity
with a concept attached. Capital reabsorbing the critique. Both readings hold, and the
tension is the essay.

## 8 · One honest caveat about the ladder

**The four orders are historical epochs for Baudrillard, not rungs a single object climbs.**
He's describing successive orders of the image across the counterfeit, production and
simulation eras — a periodisation, not a diagnostic you run on one product.

Walking one boot up all four is a teaching adaptation. It's a good one, and it's how the
idea becomes legible in ten minutes to people who haven't read the book. But a student who
goes and reads *Simulacra* will notice the four orders aren't presented as a test you apply
to objects.

Say it's your device. It costs nothing and it's the difference between a reading and a
misreading.

## For students who want to go further

- **Jean Baudrillard, *Simulacra and Simulation*** (1981). Start with the first essay, "The
  Precession of Simulacra" — the map, the four orders, Disneyland. That's the whole apparatus
  in about twenty pages.
- **Jean Baudrillard, *For a Critique of the Political Economy of the Sign*** (1972) for
  sign-value specifically.
- Sitting next to this in `examples-and-references.md`: **Veblen** (*The Theory of the
  Leisure Class*, 1899) on conspicuous consumption, which is the sociological version of the
  same observation about the $350.

CHECK before quoting: the exact wording of "the meticulous reduplication of the real" and
"always already reproduced" — both are real Baudrillard formulations but translations vary,
so pull them from the edition you're citing rather than from here.
