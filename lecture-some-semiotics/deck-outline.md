## 1. What is semiotics — slides 000–006

*[SEMIOTICS! title]*

Open on the joke, before anyone gets nervous about the vocabulary.

> "Semiotics tells us things we already know in a language we will never understand" — Paddy Whannel

Then the plain definition: the study of signs and symbols. How we create, interpret and use text, materials, images and forms to convey meaning.

Then the claim that earns the next thirty minutes:

> "Semiotics is the main philosophy you really take advantage of as a designer."

And the working version of it: how we select and combine type, materials, images and forms; what we want those choices to mean; and how those choices are interpreted.

**Say the method out loud here.** The whole lecture runs on one animal. Tell them.

## 3. Meaning — slides 200–206

*[MEANING!]*

Connotative and denotative meanings, named before they're taught.

> "Wield control over connotation & denotation in your work…"

Then the two that carry the rest of the talk:

> "Anything that has a meaning, it was DECIDED by someone, and agreed upon by others."

> "Nothing has intrinsic meaning!"

And immediately the guardrail, so nobody files this under nothing-means-anything:

> "BUT MEANING MATTERS. BEING CLEAR MATTERS."

## 5. Denotation — slides 400–422

*[cat]*

The literal or dictionary definition. The core, objective meaning.

The run: the word → the Noun Project icon → c / a / t as three letters → 고양이 / katt / 猫 → the dictionary definition → "cat-ness."

Then the turn, which is where this section stops being a glossary — slides 417–422:

> "neutral?"
>
> "Neutral is meant to mean: without adding additional layers of hidden meaning."
>
> "'neutrality' is often code for 'this reflects the creator's beliefs' or is reflective of the status quo… these ARE ideologies, and NOT neutral."

*[Modernism]*

Modernism is where the section lands. That's the payload — the claim that a style that presents itself as neutral is making an argument and hiding it.

## 6. Connotation — slides 500–517

*[cat / Cat / at]*

The associations a sign drags along — emotional, cultural, subjective, and different depending on who's looking.

The run: the same cat as burglar (thief on a roof) → the black cat that means bad luck in one culture and good luck in another → the cat on the windowsill as independence → the cat with yarn as playfulness.

The point is that none of these are in the animal. They're in the room.

## 8. Myth — slides 700–703

*[Barthes, myth]*

> "When connotations become 'naturalized' — a secondary meaning or association becomes dominant… Myths are often used by those in power to control others."

*[The American Dream!]*

## 9. Saussure — slides 800, 900–909

*[Saussure / Peirce]* *[Ferdinand de Saussure, 1857–1913, Switzerland]*

Language as a system of signs. Signifier and signified.

*[signifier: C-A-T, *meow*, *prrrrr* / signified: the actual furry creature, the mental image, cat-ness]*

Note for later: the theorists come *after* the phenomena on purpose. The room feels denotation, connotation and myth first, then gets the names and the machinery. That's backwards from every textbook and it's the right order — don't let a later pass "fix" it.

## 10. Peirce — slides 1000–1011

*[Charles Sanders Peirce, 1839–1914, USA]*

The triad: representamen / object / interpretant. What the sign is, what it points at, what a person makes of it.

Peirce over Saussure because the interpreter is in the model, and because Peirce is after rules for *any* kind of sign, not just language.

> "The meaning of a sign is always another sign."
>
> "UNLIMITED SEMIOSIS — open, dynamic, no meaning is ever final."

## 11. Types of signs — slides 2000–2009

*[word, gesture, image, sound…]*

A sign is anything that can be interpreted as having meaning.

- **Linguistic** — made of words.
- **Iconic** — resembles the thing. A picture of a cat.
- **Indexical** — caused by the thing. A litter box.
- **Symbolic** — arbitrary. The word "cat." (Linguistic signs are a subset of these.)

*[MSCHF Big Red Boot]*

> "Big Red Boots are REALLY not shaped like feet, but they are EXTREMELY shaped like boots."

Anchor the section on this, not the definitions. An object built to resemble the *sign* of a boot rather than the *function* of one — iconicity and reference coming apart in a thing you can buy.

## Not yet in the deck — candidates

- **Nuclear waste warning markers.** Signing *danger, do not dig* to people 10,000 years out with no shared language or culture. The arbitrariness of the sign under maximum load, and a real problem real people were paid to fail at. Ties to `lecture-design-for-the-future-today/`. (American Hysteria, "Talking to the Future," in the channel.)
- **Magritte, *The Treachery of Images*.** One slide. The canonical version of the joke this whole talk runs on.
- **Adrian Frutiger, *Signs and Symbols: Their Design and Meaning*.** Free PDF in the channel. A designer's semiotics rather than a linguist's. Assignable.
- **Wind barbs.** A notation nobody can read without being taught — pure convention, zero resemblance. The counterweight to the Big Red Boot.
- **The butcher's chart.** There's a whole cow run in the channel (Grocer's Encyclopedia beef diagrams, cattle breeds). The same animal cut into different signs by different cultures — French and American charts disagree about where a cow's parts are. Either an abandoned parallel to the cat or a second act.
- **Apple's AI icon.** An industry failing, in public and in real time, to agree on a symbol.
