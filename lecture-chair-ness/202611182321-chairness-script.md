# Chairness

## 0. What is a chair?

*[slide 1 · the chair drawing: Chair-ness (or, what is a chair?)]*

I'm going to talk about chair-ness, or what is a chair.

In my practice, making things is a kind of researching. I get interested in a topic, and then I make things to learn about it. That often turns into some sort of more extended project, or leads into the next thing. It's an iterative, flowing process.

*[slide 2 · the definition]*

So what is a chair? A designed object for sitting, typically with a seat, a back, perhaps legs.

*[slide 3 · function, structure, intention; the Windsor chair]*

What makes something a chair could be a little broader. Does it support a body? Is it a surface to sit on? Does it offer respite?

*[slide 4 · ten chairs from the Vitra Design Museum]*

With that very limited range of vague ideas, there's a huge number of ways it has already been explored. Even really bizarre chairs, of strange materials, strange aesthetics, strange shapes, are still understandable as chairs.

*[slide 5 · Yoko Ono's burning chair. Let the quote sit for a beat.]*

That's because of the Platonic ideal of chair-ness we all have pretty readily available. 

## 1. Chairs are design history

*[slide 6 · Sudjic quote; Thonet No. 14, Sedia 1, Aeron]*

The chair has been a medium for art and design to explore new technologies and new aesthetics. Thonet's No. 14 is considered the first really modern chair. Entire factories were built to steam and bend wood. It was bolted together, not joined, made in pieces, packed into crates, and shipped all over Europe to fill the cafés in Berlin and Paris. Enzo Mari's Sedia 1 tried to teach people about design by asking them to build it themselves — Mari hoped that through learning a little bit about construction you would value "good" design more. And since the late '80s, task chairs like the Aeron have been where ergonomics and materials get worked out: how bodies work, and what comfort is supposed to mean.

## 2. How I got here

*[slide 7 · the Oka Chair in the pasture]*

Justin Beitzel, one of our Product Design adjuncts, has been working on the Oka Chair. It's a circular, regenerative chair. All of its materials come from the Baltimore bioregion: fallen lumber, wool from a single sheep farm, hemp grown in western Maryland. I wrote about it in a sustainability book chapter, and through the examples I used in that chapter, I started getting way more into chairs. Chairs are easy to get into. (You literally get into them).

*[slide 8 · the are.na channel]*

I started collecting: images, quotes, tutorials on chair making.

## 3. Making

*[slide 9 · the first two chairs; Wegner]*

Around then, Chris Attenborough and I were wrapping up a collaboration on plastic recycling. We were looking for sort of the next thing to have fun making. We've got a lot of waste material.  So we started making chairs ourselves. These were the first two.

*[slide 10 · "restaurant chair"; Chaise A at Union]*

It started out as sort of a joke — despite there being so many chair designs out there, you tend to see the same kinds of chairs in most restaurants and spaces... Search "restaurant chair" and the same chair comes up everywhere: some version of Xavier Pauchard's Chaise A. Union's taproom is full of them too. Someone left a Google Maps review of Union: "Horrible uncomfortable stools. I stood most of my visit because of them. But the beer was good." It seemed sort of funny to start putting a bunch of new chair styles into Union...

*[slide 11 · six photos of our chairs at Union]*

So how many different kinds of chair can we fit in that room?

*[slide 12 · the rules, next to a build session]*

The rules are simple. One chair each, per week. One work session, four hours or less. Found and leftover materials only. Functional and safe to sit in. Constraints are what end up creating variety.

*[slide 13 · People are harder on chairs than we thought; repairs, the luggage-tray chair, the ladder chair]*

The chairs have been out in the tasting room at Union, and it turns out people are harder on chairs than we thought. Pine and plywood held together with a few deck screws don't hold up quite like we thought. My luggage-tray chair tips over backward if you put something heavy on it. Chris's ladder chair, we decided, was too dangerous to sit in. And a lot of what we made looks like a chair, but when you sit in it, it's hard, it's upright, it's more uncomfortable than you might imagine. So what are better ways of joining, fastening, attaching? How do we use material better? Where else do we look at for inspiration moving forward?

## 4. Making sends you back to reading

*[slide 14 · Red Blue Chair, Sedia 1, FLOW]*

Building teaches you things the reading doesn't. Rietveld made his chairs at right angles. Once I was making chairs fast, it seemed obvious why: a right angle is the simplest way to nail a bunch of things together quickly.  Mari's chairs do this too. People are still rebuilding Sedia 1, fifty years later, here out of steel studs. So you can then take a thing, try a new material and see what happens…

*[slide 15 · Catherine Prouvé quote; Prouvé's Métropole, the Shaker tilter chair]*

Prouvé's chairs have these really over-engineered, gigantic back legs. It turns out he really liked tipping back in chairs. A behavior leads to a really clear engineering feature, not just an aesthetic one. The Shakers knew people leaned back too, so they put tilter buttons on the back feet to keep the hard maple from scratching their floors.

*[slide 16 · CKB-001: Tips back gracefully]*

That led me to make a chair that tips back gracefully. I also thought about how to show via the chair itself that it wanted to lean back.

*[slide 17 · 95° / 105°]*

I started carrying a tape measure, and finally got out the Humanscale books. They try to tell you what size and shape to make everything, based on a handful of standard bodies. A dining chair has about 95 degrees between the seat and the back. A lounge chair is more like 105.

*[slide 18 · the word cloud]*

By making a chair, you end up needing to think about ergonomics and materials and history and construction and comfort and accessibility and semiotics and sculpture and architecture and craft and style and the future. What chairs do I want to exist in my preferable future? Each of these can send you down a new rabbit hole, and give you something to bring back to the next thing you make.

## 5. Results

*[slide 19 · 16 chairs. 8 sessions. Under 32 hours.]*

We made 16 chairs over the summer. Between the two of us, each spent less than 32 hours making eight objects.

*[slides 20–24 · click through while you talk, about one slide every six seconds: building, sketch then chair, stools, off the bench, all of them]*

One thing we wanted to show students: if you can find some focused time, and don't get overly concerned with making it correctly, just safe and functional, you can get quite far quite quickly. You can amass a body of work. You can iterate and learn a lot, so the next one is better, or faster, or more complex. Just do things.

## 6. The answer, so far

*[slide 25 · Solveig in the chair she and I made; Moholy-Nagy. Stays up for questions.]*

The last chair is one I made with my daughter, Solveig.

My idea of researching is influenced by László Moholy-Nagy's line, "Designing is not a profession but an attitude." It's a way to approach the world: I make things to help me understand it, or to help me participate in it. And that's a self-improving, cyclical process that carries on through different interests.
