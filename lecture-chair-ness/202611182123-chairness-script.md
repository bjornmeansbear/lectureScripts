# Chairness

## 0. The question

*[slide 1 · your Chairness chair drawing. "Chair-ness (or, what is a chair?)"]*

Chair-ness. What is a chair? This is an ongoing project and thought experiment.

*[slide 2 · the definition: "a designed object for sitting, typically with a seat, back, and often legs." No script words yet. Read it aloud, or let it sit.]*

*[slide 3 · function, structure, intention, next to a Windsor chair. This paragraph moved here from section 3.]*

The essential qualities that make something a chair, regardless of style, material, or context: function, to support a seated body; structure, a surface to sit on; and intention, to provide respite.

*[slide 4 · ten chairs from the Vitra Design Museum on two floor lines. No script words yet. A pause works here.]*

## 1. Chairs are design history

*[slide 5 · Thonet No. 14, Sedia 1, Aeron on one floor line]*

Chairs offer a confluence of historical significance, familiarity, and industrial complexity. Throughout history, the chair has been a tangible manifesto for new technologies, aesthetics, and social movements. When someone finds a new material, or a new idea about how things should look, they often try it on a chair first.

Thonet's No. 14 bentwood chair, from 1849, pioneered mass production and flat-pack shipping.

*[still slide 5 · check: the deck says Sedia 1, 1973, from the Vitra record]*

Enzo Mari's Sedia Uno, from 1974, paved the way for today's DIY maker culture.

Herman Miller's Aeron chair changed ergonomics and office furniture.

Everyone understands a chair. It's at work, at home, in public, in private.

For most of history, "designed" chairs mostly belonged to people with money. That only started to change in the late 1700s and 1800s. Subsequently, especially with industrialization, chairs began to become more and more ubiquitous...

*[slide 6 · Cranz quote]*

Galen Cranz, in *The Chair*: "Biology, physiology, and anatomy have less to do with our chairs than do pharaohs, kings, and executives."

A chair makes a good form to experiment on.

## 2. How I got here: reading

*[slide 7 · the Oka Chair in the pasture, with its question. Everything from here to the are.na paragraph runs over this one slide, about 345 words. Cut to about 170.]*

My interest in chairs goes back a long way. My grandparents are Norwegian, and they always had Scandinavian furniture. I always like the chairs in their house. Then the Eameses, and mid-century furniture generally. Enzo Mari. Jasper Morrison. Donald Judd. Joseph Kosuth. Conceptual art and modern design.

My friend Chris Attenborough teaches fabrication here in the first year. He's also a partner in Union Craft Brewing. The project we collaborated on before this one started with the plastic waste coming out of the brewery. We built plastic recycling machines.

Through that project and some of my other work ended up leading to an invitation to write the design chapter in a book on sustainability. I started from the circular economy and the plastic. Meanwhile Justin Beitzel, who is an adjunct that teaches for us in Product Design, was building a chair with every material sourced from within 100 miles. His chair moved me from just circularity to bioregionalism, and really onto chairs, not just as a thing I liked, but that I really started too think about more closely.

The chapter's main case study is Justin's "Oka Chair." Picture it resting in a sunlit pasture, surrounded by the sources of its materials: a flock of sheep whose undyed wool forms its cushions, trees whose salvaged urban kin provide its timber, a web of woven hemp cordage under the cushions. The Oka Chair began with a simple question: "How might we design a chair that gives back at each stage of its life cycle?"

*[slide 8 · New materials, new forms: Flax Chair, Parupu, Solid C2]*

The chapter then sets contemporary chairs that try to be circular against the old guard of "good" chairs. It argues that constraints become creative opportunities: sourcing locally compels forms to emerge from the materials themselves; and that experimenting with new technologies and materials leads to novel forms.

*[slide 9 · About 500 chairs: a screenshot of the are.na channel]*

Along the way I've saved a lot of the chairs I came across to an are.na channel: about 500 so far. A few are design classics, Wegner, Rietveld, Prouvé. Most aren't. A throne, a log, a plastic patio chair, a Hockney painting of someone sitting.

## 3. What is chair-ness?

*[slide 10 · Kosuth, One and Three Chairs: "What is a chair?"]*

All of that kept raising the same questions. What is a chair? What is a chair actually for? How do you know when you've made one? How little material can you use and still have a chair? Is a stool a chair? Is a bench?

*[slide 11 · Yoko Ono's burning chair: "the chair in your head did not burn or disappear." Her line says this paragraph. You could shorten yours. Check the quote's wording and source.]*

Chair-ness is a Platonic ideal: a perfect, abstract form that exists in thought, with every physical chair an imperfect manifestation. Strip away style, material, and history, and you get a seat, a back, a place to rest.

*[no slide · the Navy Chair is on the cut list]*

Chair-ness can show up through many materials, traditions, and styles. Emeco's Navy Chair started in aluminum in 1944. Later it came in recycled plastic, and then in wood made by Amish craftsmen. One iconic form, multiple materials (the later stylistic encrustations no longer tied to materiality or original meaning... but that's a different presentation).

*[slide 12 · Gibson quote. Photo still needed: logs around a fire]*

In 1979 the psychologist James Gibson described a surface that's roughly horizontal, flat, rigid and knee-high. He said it "affords sitting on." A log along a hiking trail does that. But does just sitting on a log make it a chair. Six logs arranged around a campfire maybe are chairs.

*[slide 13 · "An invitation to repose." Photo still needed: someone on an upturned bucket]*

Early on, Chris and I tried to say what makes something a chair. We landed on rest. You bring some things together so a body can take a load off. A construction worker sitting on an upturned bucket counts. I would pose then that nothing is constant in chairs except that they offer an invitation to repose.

## 4. The method: making

*[slide 14 · our chairs at Union, the Wegner quote on a card. Photo still needed: someone sitting in one]*

So, here we are, making chairs out of found materials, as an exploration in reuse, circularity, and adhocism. Chris and I have built 16 chairs so far. The chairs live in Union's taproom, and the plan is to keep filling the room with them.

Hans Wegner: "A chair is only finished when someone sits in it."

*[slide 15 · "restaurant chair": nine Chaise A chairs, and Union's red ones]*

Search "restaurant chair" and the same chair comes up everywhere: some version of Xavier Pauchard's Chaise A and Tolix Stool. Union's taproom is full of them too. So how many different kinds of chair can we fit in that room? Chairs can take so many forms. Why does every restaurant use the same ones? Ours only get made from what's on hand, no two come out alike. 

*[slide 16 · The rules]*

1. One chair per artist, per week.
2. Built in a single work session.
3. Found and leftover materials only. Nothing new except sandpaper, fasteners and finish, and we reuse fasteners when we can.
4. It has to be functional and safe to sit in.
5. Finishing and cleanup can happen after the build session.
6. Repairs and tweaks are allowed as the project goes on.

The materials are offcuts from Chris's or his neighbor's shop, and leftovers from our other projects. A session is four hours or less. We meet on Friday mornings, respond to the materials available that day, and leave with a chair. Constraints are what end up creating variety.

*[no slide · the office chair base was dropped]*

I was riding my bike and passed the bottom of an office chair on the side of the road. That became the start of a stool. A found object works as a prompt for me. It shows up and starts asking questions. How could this be for sitting? What chair does this suggest?

## 5. The loop: making sends you back to reading

*[slide 17 · Red Blue Chair, Sedia 1, FLOW (2022). Your own Sedia reference chair goes here too.]*

Building teaches you things the reading doesn't. Gerrit Rietveld made his chairs at right angles. Once I was making chairs fast, it seemed obvious why. A right angle is the simplest way to drill or nail a bunch of things together quickly. Enzo Mari's Autoprogettazione furniture does this too.

*[slide 18 · Tipping back: Prouvé's Standard chair, a Shaker chair with pewter tilter buttons]*

As we made more chairs, I started actually trying to find out more about "notable" chairs. Making sent me back to reading, and the reading sent me back to the shop. I learned for example why Jean Prouvé's Standard chair has oversized back legs: Prouvé liked to tip back in chairs! That big chunky leg shape is to support his preference for sitting, over-engineering the major connection point. Is there another way to do that? Can a chair show you what it's for (other than just sitting!)? That led me to make a chair that tips back gracefully.

*[slide 19 · the Shaker tilting chair: "Pick it up. Hang it on the wall." This paragraph moved up from after the stuffed-chair paragraph. It still opens with "Or", which pointed back to Wegner and Juhl, so it needs a new first word.]*

Or a Shaker chair. The joinery is beautiful in its simplicity. There's no decoration, because Shakers weren't into that (it's a gift to be simple!). And the top of the back is often handled, so you can easily pick up the chair to hang it on the wall. Clean the floor, clear the room, easy, functional.

*[slide 20 · 95° / 105°. Photo still needed: a tape measure on a seat]*

Then I realized I don't know anything about ergonomics. I know when one chair is more comfortable than another from personal preference. I can't always tell you why. This hasn't really led us to make more ergonomic chairs... but maybe next...

I started carrying a tape measure. When I found a chair I liked, I measured how high the seat was off the ground and the angle of the back. I had bought the reprinted Humanscale books from Henry Dreyfuss Associates a while ago. I finally got them out. They try to tell you what size and shape to make everything, based on a handful of standard bodies. Chairs are very standardized. They also have small irregularities that make a huge difference in comfort. However, comfort often depends on the body sitting, and on taste.

On average the angle of seat to back is 95° for a dining chair, 105° for a lounge chair.

*[no slide · stuffed chairs, Wegner and Juhl are on the cut list]*

I hate stuffed chairs. I almost never find "comfy chairs" comfy. I want as little cushion as possible. Chairs I keep going back to as “good” are chairs like Hans Wegner's Wishbone Chair, and Finn Juhl's Chieftain chair, which is a wood frame holding a few thin leather covered plates.

*[slide 21 · the word list: ergonomics, materials, history, construction, comfort, accessibility, semiotics, sculpture, architecture, craft, style, futures]*

*[Claude's draft from 2026-11-16, not yet in your words. Rewrite it or cut it.]*

So what does the research actually look like? It lives in three places. An are.na channel of about 500 chairs. Sixteen chairs in a taproom. And a folder of notes, essays and quotes that keeps growing.

Getting into any one chair opens onto everything around it. Prouvé's back leg is about construction and weight. The tape measure is about comfort, and then about whose body the standard was drawn from. The Shaker chair is craft and belief. Rietveld's chair is a chair, a sculpture and an argument about architecture. The restaurant chair is a style signal.

Making is how I get in. It shows me what I don't know, and then I choose what to go read.

## 6. Results

*[slide 22 · the luggage-tray chair and the ladder chair. Photos still needed]*

They haven't all turned out as safe as we hoped. I built a chair with a sort of luggage tray on the back. Put something heavy on it and the chair tips over backward. Chris built one out of an old ladder. We decided it was too dangerous to sit in.

*[slide 23 · all 16 chairs; card: 16 chairs. 8 sessions. Under 32 hours. Photo still needed]*

Beyond the first rules, we haven't set many for each other. But as we've built them, we've started to think of them as a family.

Chris painted one red. The next one got a little lip of blue. A later one needed something, so it got a small hint of red as an accent. Most of the wood comes from the same supply: ash, maple and white oak from Baltimore trees that came down, plus plain pine offcuts. The ad hoc construction, the shared wood and a few spots of color hold the set together.

They're a family because they share a process, construction methods (usually a bunch of deck screws) and a set of ideas more than that they reflect common forms.

Students often seem surprised by how long things take. Sometimes they're equally surprised that things don't always take that long.

The goal on a first try is to make the thing. Going through the motions shows you what you know and what you don't. It shows you the fixes for the next one right away. In design we call that prototyping. I think it's just making.

So when a student tells me there wasn't time, I can point at these. Eight sessions. Four hours or less. Sixteen chairs in under 32 hours. They won't win design awards. They will never be notable chairs... but they were made to find out what a chair is.

## 7. The answer, so far

*[slide 24 · Moholy-Nagy quote]*

I don't know where Chair-ness goes next. Who are we making chairs for? Can we make a Shaker chair in four hours? Something that references one? Are these really just sculptures you can sit in? Is this helping us get better at making chairs? Maybe the point is just making a lot of them, running from practical to fantastical.

*[the ending decided in the notes, not yet read aloud]*

László Moholy-Nagy: "Designing is not a profession but an attitude." It's how I take part in the world as a designer.
