# Chairness

## 1. Sixteen chairs

*[a chair in the Union taproom]*

My friend Chris Attenborough and I have built 16 chairs so far.

Chris teaches fabrication here in the first year. He's also a partner in Union Craft Brewing. The chairs live in Union's taproom, and the plan is to keep filling the room with them.

*[the rules]*

1. One chair per artist, per week.
2. Built in a single work session.
3. Found and leftover materials only. Nothing new except sandpaper, fasteners and finish, and we reuse fasteners when we can.
4. It has to be functional and safe to sit in.
5. Finishing and cleanup can happen after the build session.
6. Repairs and tweaks are allowed as the project goes on.

The materials are offcuts from Chris's or his neighbor's shop, and leftovers from our other projects. A session is four hours or less. We meet on Friday mornings, respond to the materials available that day, and leave with a chair.

*[the Yelp review]*

Someone complained that the taproom didn't have enough types of chairs. They're all the same metal Tolix-style seat, the Chaise A, or stool. What they probably meant was: these are hard, is there something softer? We chose to read it literally. How many different kinds of chair can we fit in this room?

Sometimes a joke is a good way into a whole line of investigation.

*[Chaise A, over and over: a "restaurant chair" image search]*

Search "restaurant chair" and the same chair comes up everywhere: some version of Xavier Pauchard's Chaise A or maybe an Emeco Navy Chair. Chairs can take so many forms. Why does every restaurant use the same ones? Ours can only be made from what's on hand, so no two come out alike. Constraints are what end up creating variety.

So how is making chairs on a Friday morning research?

## 2. Why a chair

*[plastic recycling machines]*

The project we collaborated on before this one started with the plastic waste coming out of the brewery. We built plastic recycling machines.

That project ended up leading to an invitation to write the design chapter in a book on sustainability. I started from the circular economy and the plastic. Meanwhile Justin Beitzel, who is an adjunct that teaches for us in Product Design, was building a chair with every material sourced from within 100 miles. His chair moved me from just circularity to bioregionalism, and both of them onto chairs. The chapter ended up first, using the Oka Chair, as its main casestudy for sustainable design, and then more broadly setting contemporary chairs that try to be circular against the old guard of “good” chairs.

*[Thonet No. 14]*

Chairs turn out to be a good record of design history. When someone finds a new material, or a new idea about how things should look, they often try it on a chair first. Thonet steam-bending wood is one. 

Everyone understands a chair. It's at work, at home, in public, in private.

*[Dror Pimentel quote]*

The philosopher Dror Pimentel: "A chair is a human object, since only humans can sit. Only humans can sit, since only humans can stand."

For most of history, chairs mostly belonged to people with money. That only started to change in the late 1700s and 1800s. Subsequently, especially with industrialization, chairs began to become more and more ubiquitous...

A chair makes a good form to experiment on.

## 3. Found things are questions

*[the office chair base]*

I was riding my bike and passed the bottom of an office chair on the side of the road. That became the start of a stool.

A found object works as a prompt for me. It shows up and starts asking questions. How could this be for sitting? What chair does this suggest?

A theme like this gives the experiments something to hang on.

## 4. What I didn't know

*[Rietveld, Red Blue Chair; crate furniture]*

Building teaches you things the reading doesn't. Gerrit Rietveld made his chairs at right angles. Once I was making chairs fast, it was obvious why. A right angle is the simplest way to drill or nail a bunch of things together quickly. You can learn a lot about joinery by trying to make things without any sophisticated joinery.

*[Prouvé, Standard chair]*

As we made more chairs, I started actually trying to find out more about “notable” chairs. I learned for example why Jean Prouvé's Standard chair has oversized back legs: Prouvé likes to tip back in chairs! That big chunky leg shape is to support his preference for sitting, over-engineering the major connection point. Is there another way to do that? Can a chair show you what it's for (other than just sitting!)?

Then I realized I don't know anything about ergonomics. I know when one chair is more comfortable than another from personal preference. I can't tell you why always.

*[tape measure on a chair seat]*

So I started carrying a tape measure. When I found a chair I liked, I measured how high the seat was off the ground and the angle of the back. I had bought the reprinted Humanscale books from Henry Dreyfuss Associates a while ago. I finally got them out. They try to tell you what size and shape to make everything, based on a handful of standard bodies.

Chairs are very standardized. They also have small irregularities that make a huge difference in comfort. However, comfort often depends on the body sitting, and on taste.

I hate stuffed chairs. I almost never find "comfy chairs" comfy. I want as little cushion as possible. So the chairs I keep going back to are chairs like Hans Wegner's Wishbone Chair, and Finn Juhl's Chieftain chair, which is a wood frame holding a few thin leather covered plates.

*[Shaker chairs hanging on wall pegs]*

Or a Shaker chair. The joinery is beautiful in its simplicity. There's no decoration, because Shakers weren't into that (it's a gift to be simple!). And the top of the back are often handles, so you can hang the chair on the pegs along the wall. Clean the floor, clear the room, use it for something else.

Finding out how other times and places thought about sitting keeps giving us new ideas. 

## 5. A family

*[the chairs together]*

Beyond the first rules, we haven't set many for each other. But as we've built them, we've started to think of them as a family.

Chris painted one red. The next one got a little lip of blue. A later one needed something, so it got a small hint of red as an accent. Most of the wood comes from the same supply: ash, maple and white oak from Baltimore trees that came down, plus plain pine offcuts. The ad hoc construction, the shared wood and a few spots of color hold the set together.

They're a family because they share a process, construction methods (usually a bunch of deck screws) and a set of ideas more than that they reflect common forms.

## 6. Four hours

Students often seem surprised by how long things take. Sometimes they're equally surprised that things don't always take that long.

The goal on a first try is to make the thing. Going through the motions shows you what you know and what you don't. It shows you the fixes for the next one right away. In design we call that prototyping. I think it's just making.

So when a student tells me there wasn't time, I can point at these. Two people, a few hours at a time, equals 16 chairs. They won't win design awards. they will probably never be notable chairs... but, they were made to find out what a chair is and can be.

## 7. Where it goes

*[a chair in use]*

I don't know where Chair-ness goes next. Who are we making chairs for? Can we make a Shaker chair in four hours? Something that references one? Are these really just art pieces? is this helping us get better at making chairs? Maybe the point is just making a lot of them, running from practical to fantastical.

Early on, Chris and I tried to say what makes something a chair. We landed on rest. You bring some things together so a body can take a load off.

This might look like an art project. It's how I take part in the world as a designer.
