## The arc

The talk asks a research question, then answers it two ways: by reading and by making. Each one sent you back to the other.

| # | Section | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | The question | "Chair-ness. What is a chair? Does the chair afford sitting?" on the title slide |
| 1 | Chairs are design history | Why chairs: Thonet, Mari, the Aeron, Pimentel, Cranz |
| 2 | How I got here: reading | Influences, the book chapter, the Oka chair, the are.na collection |
| 3 | What is chair-ness? | The Platonic ideal; function, structure, intention; the Navy Chair; Gibson's logs; the bucket; "an invitation to repose" |
| 4 | The method: making | "And yet here I am…", Chris and the 16 chairs, Wegner, the "restaurant chair" search, the rules, the office chair base |
| 5 | The loop | Making sent you back to reading: Rietveld's right angles, Prouvé's back legs, the tape measure, Humanscale, Shaker chairs |
| 6 | Results | The two failed chairs, the family, 16 chairs in under 32 hours |
| 7 | The answer, so far | Open questions; notable and un-notable chairs; Moholy-Nagy |

Two echoes are built in. Leave them unexplained:
- The chapter claims constraints are creative opportunities (section 2), and the restaurant-chair search tests it: "Constraints are what end up creating variety" (section 4).
- Prouvé thickens his back legs because that's where the weight goes when you tip back (section 5), and then your luggage-tray chair tips over backward (section 6).

How to cut (from the bjorn-voice skill):
- Find the sentence that was always the point, and cut everything that isn't it.
- Don't add tag lines to round a paragraph off.
- If a story already makes the point, don't also state the point.

Count the words again after cutting. At 123 words a minute, 1,000 words is 8:08.
